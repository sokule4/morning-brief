## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Monday, November second, and this is your Morning Brief. Naftali Bennett says he'll recommend Gadi Eisenkot for prime minister, and President Herzog is begging the party leaders to stop "climbing trees." Trump and Netanyahu reportedly had a tough phone call about when, and whether, to hit Iran again. And America votes tomorrow, with Democrats leading the polls and nearly fifty million ballots already cast. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in Israel, where the coalition picture got a lot clearer overnight. Quick reminder of the math: Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three seats, the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot has forty-eight, and the Arab parties have a record nineteen. Sixty-one is a majority. On Sunday evening, Naftali Bennett's party, B'Yachad, with twelve seats, announced it will recommend Eisenkot to President Herzog. Its statement said Bennett "made clear throughout the election campaign that he would respect the voters' decision." That matters because Bennett is a former prime minister from the right, and some people thought he might drift toward Netanyahu. He didn't.

The Arab parties are moving the same way. A senior Joint List official told the news site Walla that both the Joint List and Ra'am are expected to recommend Eisenkot, and one Joint List lawmaker told Haaretz the party won't even ask for cabinet seats. Yair Golan, who leads the left-wing Democrats, has been talking with Joint List leaders Ahmad Tibi and Yousef Jabareen, and they plan to meet this week. Ynet counted it up: Eisenkot could get about sixty recommendations even without Avigdor Liberman's party, against about forty-six for Netanyahu.

So who's the holdout? Liberman. He leads Yisrael Beytenu, a right-wing secular party that is part of the anti-Netanyahu camp but hates the idea of relying on Arab parties. Ynet reports he had a tense meeting with Eisenkot on Saturday and scolded him over his campaign and his push for a minority government, a government that survives without a majority because some parties agree not to vote against it. On X, Liberman wrote that if there's any deal with the Arab parties, "Yisrael Beytenu will not be there." He holds his first faction meeting today and is expected to say what he'll do.

And here's a twist. The two ultra-Orthodox parties, Shas and United Torah Judaism, who were Netanyahu's loyal partners, met on Sunday. A Shas rabbi, Moshe Maya, said there's "no reason to rule out coalition negotiations with Eisenkot." Their big demand is a law exempting yeshiva students from the army, which is exactly what Eisenkot's camp has promised not to pass.

President Herzog spoke on Sunday at a memorial for Chaim Weizmann, Israel's first president. "The election results are an established fact, they are the reality," he said. Then, to the party leaders: "Stop climbing trees," meaning stop locking yourselves into positions you can't climb down from. He said, "we will try to put up ladders, to enable the leaders to position themselves," and called for "creative solutions in order to prevent another election." Here's the timeline. On Wednesday, Herzog gets the official results and starts consultations, where each party tells him who it recommends. The new Knesset is sworn in next Tuesday, and by November eleventh he has to hand someone the mandate, the official first shot at forming a government, for twenty-eight days plus a possible two-week extension.

One more piece of that story. The head of the Central Elections Committee, Supreme Court Justice Noam Sohlberg, made an unusual video statement on Sunday, standing next to the Shin Bet chief, David Zini, and the attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara. "The election for the twenty-sixth Knesset was conducted properly," he said. Last week he asked the Shin Bet to look into suspected foreign money, an alleged effort by Qatar or Turkey to pay Arab voters to turn out. He said there are "serious suspicions," but "suspicions are only suspicions," and the idea that they could overturn the result "is incorrect." Likud answered that "a dark cloud hangs over Israel's election results," pointing out that just four thousand six hundred twelve votes decided whether one seat went to Ra'am or to the right. Eisenkot accused Likud of trying to delegitimize the vote. Even if that one seat flipped, Netanyahu would still be far short of sixty-one.

Now Iran, and a split between allies. Axios reports that Trump and Netanyahu spoke by phone on Saturday, and that Netanyahu wants a new offensive right after tomorrow's US midterms, while Trump is leaning toward giving diplomacy more time. An Israeli source called the call "tough." Trump called it "a very good talk." Publicly, Trump says he's not "in much of a hurry," though he complained that the Iranians "make a deal, and then they back out." Qatari mediators met Iranian officials on Sunday for a third day in a row, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump hasn't decided on military action. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said there's no deal yet and no meetings planned with Americans, and hoped the US would show "enough rationality to choose diplomacy." But a hardline deputy parliament speaker said the window for talks "is now closed."

Meanwhile, the pressure points keep multiplying. Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy says two more tankers exploded after hitting mines on Sunday while using what it called an "unauthorized route" through the Strait of Hormuz. That's Iranian state media and hasn't been confirmed. Iran also declared Britain's Royal Navy and Royal Air Force "terrorist organizations," in revenge for the US labeling the Revolutionary Guard a terror group, and accused Britain of giving its bases to American and Israeli attacks. And a senior Iranian cleric called for a review of Iran's membership in the NPT, the treaty where countries promise not to build nuclear weapons. That's worth watching, because leaving it would be a huge escalation.

In Gaza, the ceasefire is a year old, but Sunday was deadly. According to Al Jazeera, at least eight Palestinians were killed. An Israeli drone strike near Khan Younis killed five people that Gaza's Interior Ministry says were police officers on duty, and the Israeli army says it targeted "a terrorist." In Gaza City, another strike killed three, including a four-year-old boy. Hamas called it a "deliberate war crime." Gaza's Health Ministry says more than fifteen hundred people have been killed since the ceasefire began.

In the West Bank, OCHA, the UN office that tracks humanitarian crises, says October saw three hundred ninety-seven attacks by Israeli settlers on Palestinians, in just the first twenty-six days. That's the highest monthly number since it started counting in two thousand six. Many came during the olive harvest, when Palestinian farmers go out to their groves.

Now to America, which votes tomorrow in the midterms. Nearly fifty million people had already voted early by Friday. Democrats lead the generic ballot, the poll asking which party you want running Congress, by nearly nine points, and Trump's approval is around thirty-seven percent. Democrats need just three seats to flip the House. The Senate is harder: they need to gain four, and races in Maine, Michigan, Ohio, Texas and Alaska are toss-ups, too close to call. Even Ted Cruz admitted, "We could lose Texas." There's legal drama too. In Alaska, a second man named Daniel J. Sullivan, the same name as the Republican senator, is on the ballot as an independent. In California, a federal judge issued a restraining order, a court order to stop something, against Justice Department demands to set aside about twenty thousand ballots from alleged noncitizens. Trump rallied in Iowa and Kansas on Sunday: "Tuesday, we're going to see how it all works out."

In Europe, Poland's court fight has turned into a full constitutional crisis. Here's how it started. Poland's top court, the Constitutional Tribunal, the court that decides if laws are constitutional, was filled with judges appointed by the right-wing Law and Justice party, which ran Poland until two thousand twenty-three. Prime Minister Donald Tusk's government says those appointments were illegitimate, and in March parliament elected new judges. The chief judge, Bogdan Święczkowski, refused to let them in, so on Friday night the government suspended him. On Saturday, President Karol Nawrocki, an ally of Law and Justice, held crisis talks, but Tusk's party boycotted them. The court's own account called it a coup d'état, a seizure of power. The European Commission sided with parliament, saying appointing judges is its right. What's next: neither side is backing down.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where it was a huge Sunday. The New York City Marathon celebrated fifty years of the five-borough course, with about sixty thousand starters from more than one hundred forty countries, a record field. The men's race was won by Andreas Almgren of Sweden, in his marathon debut, in two hours, six minutes and one second. That's the third-fastest time in the race's history, and he's the first European-born man to win in New York in thirty years. Italy's Yemaneberhan Crippa was second. The women's race had a thrilling finish. Sheila Chepkirui of Kenya won her second New York title in two hours, twenty-four minutes, thirty-six seconds. Behind her, American Jessica McClain passed last year's champion, Hellen Obiri, in the final meters to finish second, the first American woman on the New York podium since two thousand eighteen.

There were great stories in the pack too. Channel Seven anchors Liz Cho and Mike Marza ran for the Alzheimer's Association in honor of their colleague Bill Ritter. "I feel overwhelmed, it was the most spectacular experience," Cho said.

Sunday was also the last day of early voting, and New Yorkers showed up. The Board of Elections counted about six hundred ninety thousand early check-ins citywide over nine days, with Brooklyn leading at nearly two hundred twenty thousand. After the first three days, turnout was running about seventy-six percent ahead of the last midterm in two thousand twenty-two.

The candidates spent the day chasing swing voters, people who could go either way. Governor Kathy Hochul hit the Upper West Side, eating at Barney Greengrass with Congressman Jerry Nadler, and then watched the Bills game at a sports bar in Astoria with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Mayor Zohran Mamdani. It was AOC's first in-person campaign appearance with Hochul this year. "I think this is the example of how we must come together to win, period," AOC said. Asked how big a win she needs, Hochul joked, "Plus one makes me happy enough." Her Republican challenger, Nassau County Executive Bruce Blakeman, campaigned upstate and on Long Island. "Everywhere I go, people are telling me how miserable they are under Kathy Hochul," he said, and he told supporters to make a list of fifty people to get to the polls. Today he's doing a sweep through Binghamton, Buffalo, Rochester and Syracuse, ending in Manhattan. Hochul's closing ads all end with the same line: that Blakeman will "always put Trump ahead of you." Polls are open tomorrow from six in the morning to nine at night.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, and a political drama in Michigan that's splitting Jewish Democrats on the eve of the vote. Quick background: Haley Stevens is a pro-Israel Democratic congresswoman who lost the Senate primary to Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive who is very critical of Israel. On Sunday, two days before the election, Stevens appeared with the Republican nominee, Mike Rogers, at a Black church in Detroit. Jewish Insider headlined her message as "I do not stand with Hasan Piker," the left-wing streamer who's been campaigning for El-Sayed. "Michigan is at its best when we stand up for each other: Jewish families, Black congregations," she said.

How did it get here? Last week her fiancé, the Democratic megadonor, a very big party funder, Jason Turkish, said he'd vote for Rogers. Then party elders tried to set up a unity event with El-Sayed. Stevens said she'd only join if he condemned an ad from a progressive group that blamed Israel for high grocery prices, and dropped an ad featuring Tucker Carlson. El-Sayed said he "did not agree" with the ad, but the unity effort collapsed. Senator John Fetterman backed Stevens. But hours after her appearance, several Michigan Democrats, including some Jewish ones like former congressman Andy Levin and state senator Jeremy Moss, urged voters to reject her move. One liberal Jewish group even floated a write-in, writing a name not on the ballot, for Emma Lazarus, the Jewish poet whose words are on the Statue of Liberty.

That fits a bigger picture. JTA reports that "politically homeless" has become the new phrase for American Jews. Surveys from September found Jewish voters still favor Democrats by about seventy-one to twenty-three, but more than a third of Jewish Democrats say anti-Israel candidates make it harder to identify with the party. Political scientist Kenneth Wald predicts that outside the Orthodox community, "Jews will likely vote as they have voted."

Also in Michigan, federal prosecutors charged four more people in a plot targeting Jewish groups and University of Michigan leaders. That brings the case to twelve defendants. The targets included the Jewish Federation of Detroit, which was vandalized with "intifada" graffiti on the first anniversary of October seventh. The FBI says the group threw noxious chemicals through the windows of families' homes and discussed ways to harm targets, including poison and bombs. Two of the new defendants are still at large.

In Illinois, tomorrow's ballot includes non-binding local measures, votes that send a message but don't change the law, asking the state to repeal its anti-BDS law. BDS is the movement to boycott, divest from and sanction Israel. Illinois was the first state, in two thousand fifteen, to ban state pension money from going to companies that boycott Israel. The biggest measure is in Oak Park, just outside Chicago. The ADL warned repeal would be "catastrophic" for Jews, while local activists called getting on the ballot "a victory for democracy."

In Germany, the new federal antisemitism commissioner, Katharina von Schnurbein, who took office last week, gave a stark warning. "We have reached a point that has probably never existed since the Holocaust," she told a German newspaper. "Those who make antisemitic statements today no longer fear any consequences." A monitoring group recorded a record eight thousand seven hundred twenty-five antisemitic incidents in Germany last year, about twenty-four a day.

And something lighter. Saturday was one hundred years since the death of Harry Houdini, the most famous magician in history. He was born Erik Weisz in Budapest, the son of a rabbi, and his family emigrated to America partly to escape antisemitism. He died on Halloween, nineteen twenty-six, and his grave in a Jewish cemetery in Queens has become a spot for Halloween séances. One scholar called him "the epitome of the self-made man and immigrant success story."

## Tech, startups and AI

Now to tech, starting with Israel's scoreboard. Israeli startups raised at least eight hundred seventy-two million dollars in October, according to Calcalist, and over nine hundred million according to the research firm IVC and LeumiTech. The bigger number: in the first ten months of the year, Israeli startups raised eleven point nine billion dollars. That's already more than all of last year, though still far below the record of twenty-five point six billion in two thousand twenty-one. Cybersecurity and AI dominated. Mave, which uses AI to sort security alerts, raised twenty-eight million in a Series A, and Rein Security, which protects AI agents, raised twenty-five million.

October was also busy for exits, when a startup is sold or goes public and investors cash out. Calcalist counted nine. Two stand out because they were so small and so fast. Geniez AI, a six-person company, was sold to Rocket Software for up to about one hundred million dollars. And Inferize, a seventeen-person startup that's only ten months old, was bought by the AI cloud company Nebius for somewhere between one hundred and one hundred fifty million. That's the new pattern: tiny AI teams getting bought mostly for their people.

Now the big AI money story. OpenAI's latest fundraising is coming in short. The Wall Street Journal reports OpenAI has about eleven billion dollars in commitments, less than half of its thirty billion dollar target. The Financial Times says it's now aiming for about twenty billion at a one point two five trillion dollar valuation, down from one point four trillion. After last week's tech stock slide, it's another sign that investors are starting to push back on sky-high AI prices.

From China, DeepSeek released an open-source, meaning free for anyone to use and modify, prototype of what it calls a "Super-Agent" for coding. It watches for work requests on GitHub and in the Chinese work-chat app Feishu, does the job in its own session, and replies, for example with finished code. Its stated goal is to "take as little human attention as possible." Bloomberg framed it as a direct shot at Anthropic.

And the French AI lab Mistral released the weights of its biggest model yet, Mistral Large Four, a one trillion parameter model. That makes it an open-weight model, one anyone can download and run. Mistral itself warned that it has "a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks," meaning it could help hackers, which brings us neatly to cyber.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, and the theme of the week is hackers using AI agents. CrowdStrike, the big American cybersecurity company, says a China-linked hacking group it calls Vapor Panda used a system of AI agents, mostly running on DeepSeek's model, to target more than two hundred organizations in at least fourteen countries over eleven days starting October seventeenth. One AI "planner" coordinated "worker" agents that did the hacking. Targets included American pharmaceutical, semiconductor, defense, nuclear and financial companies, and CrowdStrike confirmed nine cases where the attackers got inside.

Then Chainalysis, the blockchain analysis company police use to trace stolen crypto, disclosed on Friday that it was breached by someone using what it called "offensive agentic techniques," meaning AI agents acting on their own. The attackers got customer users' names, emails and job roles, but Chainalysis says no investigation data, passwords or MFA codes, the second step of a login, were touched. It says the breach is unrelated to the Binance heist.

And a warning for anyone who uses AI coding tools. For about eleven hours last Wednesday, a tampered copy of Anthropic's Claude Code extension sat in the marketplace of Cursor, a popular AI code editor. Cursor says up to five thousand users may be affected. Reports say the fake version tried to install a backdoor, a hidden way back into your computer. Cursor said it "was not developed by Anthropic" and released a cleanup tool. That's a classic supply-chain attack, poisoning a trusted tool so it reaches lots of victims at once.

One more breach with a marketing angle. Amplitude, the product analytics platform marketers use to track how people use an app, had a security incident that exposed some Coinbase customers' contact details. Other Amplitude customers were hit too but haven't been named. The lesson for growth teams: every tool in your marketing stack is also a door into your customer data.

An executive move from Israel's cyber world. Ehud Schneorson, a former commander of Unit 8200 and co-founder of Paragon, the Israeli company that makes cyber-intelligence tools for governments, is stepping back from management. Paragon was bought by an American private equity firm in late two thousand twenty-four for up to nine hundred million dollars, and the merged company is going public through a SPAC, a shell company that lists on the stock market and then merges with a real business, at a one point two five billion dollar valuation. Schneorson is joining Ribon, a stealth startup founded by Idan Nurick, who used to serve under him. Ribon has already raised about two hundred million dollars at a valuation above one point five billion.

Now marketing trends. Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI all backed a new common signal that lets websites opt out of AI training while staying in search results. Anthropic has already turned it on, and the others plan to in twenty twenty-seven. For content teams, that's a real decision for GEO, generative engine optimization, meaning getting your brand mentioned in AI answers: block the AI models, or feed them. Google, meanwhile, is taking ads in AI Mode and AI Overviews, the AI answers at the top of search, to ten more countries, with Britain coming later this year. Advertisers can't opt out of those placements separately from regular search. In Britain, the competition regulator opened an investigation into Meta's terms, which would let it use websites' content for its AI features without giving businesses a choice, and Meta paused the rollout.

## Business story

Time for our business story, and it starts in a Tel Aviv restaurant kitchen. Eran Heffetz co-owned several restaurants and bars in Tel Aviv, and he had a problem every restaurant owner knows. Staff kept leaving. New hires often spoke different languages. And every new person needed training all over again, but classroom training doesn't work for people who spend their whole shift on their feet. "Bites grew out of a problem I experienced personally while running restaurants in Tel Aviv," he says.

Meanwhile, two people who had built learning materials at Kidum, the Israeli test-prep company, Tal Nagler-Almog and Hagai Horovitz, had an idea: teach workers the way TikTok teaches everyone, in short videos on their phones. They brought Heffetz in as CEO, and in twenty twenty they founded Bites. Nagler-Almog is now COO, chief operating officer, who runs day-to-day operations, and Horovitz is CIO, chief information officer, in charge of technology.

The product is simple to describe. A company feeds in its procedures, and AI turns them into branded videos of up to ninety seconds. Those reach workers through WhatsApp, text message, Teams or Slack, in more than one hundred fifty languages, with no app to download and no password. The turning point was generative AI, which let them make training content at scale. "AI has served as a significant catalyst," Heffetz says.

The target is what's called deskless workers, people who don't sit at a computer, like waiters, builders and hotel staff. That's about two point eight billion people, roughly eighty percent of the world's workforce. On one construction site run by the Israeli company Electra, workers speak six different languages. Other customers include Unilever, Coca-Cola, Shake Shack and Wynn Resorts.

Last week, Bites raised a forty million dollar Series A led by PeakSpan Capital, bringing its total to fifty-five million. It has only about thirty employees. Here's the number a marketer should notice: net revenue retention, how much more existing customers spend year over year, of one hundred sixty-one percent. That means a customer paying one hundred dollars last year is paying one hundred sixty-one this year. Next, Bites plans to grow in the US and move into banking and insurance. It's a great example of a founder turning a personal pain point into a product.

## Art and design

Next, art and design. In Washington, the Hirshhorn Museum reopened its sculpture garden on Saturday after a sixty-eight million dollar redesign by the Japanese artist Hiroshi Sugimoto. It's more than an acre, with thirty thousand new plants and trees and hand-stacked stone walls. Rodin's famous "Burghers of Calais" now stands in its own stone room at eye level, and new works include a pool-like blue sculpture by Elmgreen and Dragset and a piece by KAWS. "The garden allows us to slow our own clock," Sugimoto said.

And New York's big November auctions are coming up. The headline consignment, art handed to an auction house to sell, is from Bill Koch, the billionaire collector and brother of the Koch brothers. ARTnews reports he's selling more than forty million dollars of art at Christie's, led by a Monet field of oats and poppies estimated at twelve to eighteen million dollars, and a Cézanne still life with a top estimate of eighteen million. ## Wellness

And your wellness idea for the week: the wall squat. You lean your back against a wall, slide down like you're sitting in an invisible chair, and hold. That's isometric exercise, where your muscles work without moving. A new British study of one hundred adults with slightly high blood pressure had them do four wall-squat holds with two minutes of rest in between, three times a week for four weeks. Then some cut back. Systolic blood pressure, the top number, dropped almost fourteen points in people who kept going three times a week. And people who cut down to just once a week still kept a drop of about six and a half points. People who stopped completely lost the benefit. It's a small, short study, but the takeaway is easy: a few minutes against a wall, even once a week, can help.
