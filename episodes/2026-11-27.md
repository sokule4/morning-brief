## Israel

We start here in Israel, where Eisenkot's coalition puzzle had a good Thursday.

On Tuesday night, you'll remember, Yair Golan, who leads the Democrats, the left-wing party with eight seats, walked out of talks. He was angry that Eisenkot's party, Yashar, was still negotiating with Likud about a unity government with Netanyahu's party. On Thursday, the two sides issued a joint statement saying they'd agreed in principle on a preliminary coalition deal, a first draft of an agreement to govern together.

According to the Democrats, they'd get four ministries: public security, which oversees the police, labor and social affairs, communications, and culture and sport, plus some posts in the Knesset. Golan wrote on X: "We promised the people that we would replace Netanyahu and defend democracy."

A word of caution, though. Earlier the same day, the Times of Israel's live blog reported a messier picture: that Eisenkot had first offered the Democrats only one big ministry, and that United Torah Judaism, the ultra-Orthodox party, vetoed the idea of Democrats lawmaker Gilad Kariv, a Reform rabbi, becoming education minister. So the exact list of jobs could still move.

Why does United Torah Judaism get a say? Because Eisenkot needs it. Quick reminder of the math. In the October twenty-seventh election, Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three of the Knesset's one hundred twenty seats. Eisenkot's bloc won forty-eight. The two Arab-led parties hold nineteen. Nobody has sixty-one. One option, according to Reuters and the Algemeiner, is a minority government, about fifty-five seats with Eisenkot's bloc plus United Torah Judaism, kept alive from outside by the eight seats of the United Arab List, the Islamist Arab party also known as Ra'am.

And that runs straight into the hardest issue in Israeli politics: the draft. For decades, most young Haredi men who study full time in yeshivas, religious seminaries, have been exempt from army service, while other Israelis serve for years. After two years of war, many Israelis see that as deeply unfair. Haredi leaders see Torah study as sacred and fear the army would pull young men away from religious life. Early Thursday, the Times of Israel reported that Yashar and United Torah Judaism reached agreement on key principles of a Haredi draft law. The details haven't been published, and the Times of Israel says the framework would keep wide exemptions for yeshiva students in exchange for the party's support. On the same day, Haredi protesters demonstrated in Jerusalem and Bnei Brak against the jailing of yeshiva students who refused to report for service. So you can see how tense this is.

Avigdor Liberman, whose Yisrael Beytenu signed up with Eisenkot this week, told Kan, the public broadcaster, that a government with United Torah Judaism and what he called a "clear Jewish majority" could be presented by the end of next week. He said: "There will be a government with a Jewish majority."

Meanwhile, Haaretz reported a secret back channel between an aide to Naftali Bennett and Michael Rabello, Netanyahu's negotiator, and claimed Likud offered seven spots on its list to lawmakers in Eisenkot's camp willing to switch sides. Bennett denies it. And from Shas, the other Haredi party, which fell from eleven seats to six, its leader Aryeh Deri reportedly said that if his rabbis force him to support a new government, "it will be very difficult for me to remain in politics."

What's next: Eisenkot's deadline is December sixth, and the president can add fourteen more days.

The new Knesset is also busy undoing the old government's work. Early Thursday, it passed, fifty-one to thirty-two, the first reading of a bill to cancel the broadcasting law pushed by Shlomo Karhi, Netanyahu's communications minister. A first reading is the first of three full votes a bill needs to become law.

On security, the army said it killed Khaled Abu Nada in Deir al-Balah, in central Gaza, describing him as the head of Hamas's banking and investment network. Defense Minister Israel Katz threatened a new Gaza offensive if Hamas doesn't give up its weapons, and Hamas's chief said the Israeli strikes "cannot be accepted." And Jared Kushner, the US envoy, said Israel "needs to get out of the constant wartime mindset and focus on economic diplomacy."

## World

Now to the world, and a deadly day in Kuwait.

On Thursday morning, Kuwait's army said Iranian drones targeted what it called "a vital site." Its forces shot them down, but falling shrapnel injured four people: two Italians, a Pakistani and a Kuwaiti. Later that morning, Kuwait's defense ministry said both Italians had died. Italy's prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, posted her "deepest sorrow" on behalf of her government.

Iran's answer was flat denial. Its armed forces spokesman rejected the claims "categorically and completely," and said no Iranian operations had been carried out. He called it a "false flag," an attack staged to blame someone else, by Israel and US forces. On Friday, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the incident "suspicious" and a "pre-cooked recipe for war," and offered to cooperate on finding out what happened.

Why would Kuwait be in the line of fire? The war began in February with US and Israeli strikes that killed Iran's Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei. Since then, Iran has attacked American bases and their hosts around the Gulf, and Kuwait hosts thousands of US troops. A memorandum of understanding, a first-step agreement, was signed in June, but talks have stalled. Iran says Qatari and Pakistani mediators are still passing messages. President Trump said the US has "victory at hand" and is giving Tehran a chance to negotiate a deal.

The fight over the Strait of Hormuz is also getting tighter. Iran's army says ships must now use northern lanes and get Iranian military permission. The Revolutionary Guard claimed that a gas carrier and two oil tankers hit sea mines this week, which hasn't been independently confirmed. The US military says the lanes are mine-free and keeps up its naval blockade of Iran's ports. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called Iran a "sinking ship," saying it "doesn't have a drop of oil on the water left to sell."

And the Red Sea war between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis, the Iran-backed movement that controls northern Yemen, kept going. The Houthis said they hit the Ras Tanura refinery, one of Saudi Arabia's biggest oil sites. Khaleej Times reports that an attack on a Saudi refinery killed one person and caused a temporary shutdown, though the two sides give different locations. Saudi forces say they shot down two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, and Sanaa, the Houthi-held capital, was hit soon after. The UN's aid chief, Tom Fletcher, said renewed fighting has forced more than three hundred sixty-four thousand people from their homes since August.

Now Ukraine. On Thursday, President Zelenskyy warned that Russia is "completing preparations for a new massive strike" on Ukraine's energy system, as winter sets in. Overnight, Russia had already fired ballistic missiles and one hundred thirteen drones, and repair crews were restoring power in six regions. Zelenskyy is begging for more air defense, especially Patriot interceptors, the American missiles that shoot down missiles, which have been in short supply since the Iran war began. On the diplomacy, Kushner said Putin "has agreed not to use nuclear weapons," but described the talks as being at "a little bit of a deadlock," a standstill, over Ukrainian territory.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where your Christmas shopping list just got shorter. ## Jewish world

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and an Israeli chip company that got a second life. Altair, based in Hod Hasharon, raised one hundred million dollars, Globes reported Thursday. The round is led by Maverick Silicon, a chip investment fund, with Pitango, an Israeli venture fund that backed Altair before its first sale.

Here's the story. Sony bought Altair about ten years ago, reportedly for two hundred twelve million dollars. In April, Sony shut down its development operations in Israel. Instead of scattering, about two hundred of the remaining employees took control of the company under CEO Nohik Semel, and Sony kept a stake. Now Altair is moving into chips that run AI agents, AI tools that can carry out tasks on their own, directly on devices, rather than in a far-away data center. A hundred million dollars for a company that was just spun out of a giant is a big vote of confidence.

And one wild number from the US. On Wednesday, the FCC, America's communications regulator, authorized SpaceX's "Starmind" project: up to one million satellites carrying AI chips in orbit, with the full million due by twenty forty. Scientists have questioned the economics, and how you keep chips cool in space.

And a story about AI building AI's own plumbing. Anthropic says it rebuilt its Messages API, the system developers use to send requests to Claude, in Rust, a fast and safe programming language, with its own Claude agents doing much of the work. The agents compared the old and new versions against live traffic, and human engineers reviewed the fixes. Anthropic reports about seventy percent less computing power per request and a twenty percent faster first response. For tech companies, that's the real promise of AI agents: not just chatbots, but cheaper, faster infrastructure.

## Business story

Our business story is about what happens to your secrets when a chatbot company goes broke.

Character.AI is the app where people chat and even talk on the phone with AI "characters," from anime heroes to made-up friends. It became hugely popular with teenagers, and then came lawsuits from families, including a wrongful-death suit in twenty twenty-four, and investigations by states. On October twelfth, its parent company filed for Chapter eleven, a type of bankruptcy, in Delaware. Chapter eleven lets a company keep operating while it sells assets or restructures its debts.

The first bidder was Disney, offering twenty million dollars as the stalking-horse bidder, the first offer that sets the floor price in a bankruptcy auction. But Disney only wanted a license to the code and patents, not the app, the brand or the users' data.

That's what worries the states. This week, forty-two state attorneys general, the top legal officers of each state, moved to stop the company from selling its users' chats. They say that's more than twenty-one million American accounts, including over two point one million registered to minors. They call it a sale "with no safeguards for the people whose conversations are being sold." New York's Letitia James put it simply: these conversations "could be sold off to the highest bidder." The states want any buyer to verify ages properly, block bots that pretend to be therapists, and give users a chance to delete their data before any sale.

Why does it matter to you? In marketing we talk a lot about first-party data, what customers tell you directly. This is the most intimate first-party data imaginable, and the case could set the rules for what happens to AI chat histories everywhere.

## Art and design

Next, art, and one record from last week's New York auctions that deserves more attention. At Sotheby's, Aaron Douglas's painting "Harriet Tubman," from nineteen thirty-one, sold for fourteen point four eight million dollars. Douglas was one of the leading painters of the Harlem Renaissance, the explosion of Black art and culture in Harlem in the nineteen twenties and thirties. ## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a surprising one: peppermint oil. Researchers at the University of Lancashire in England, publishing in the journal PLOS One, tested it on forty adults with slightly high blood pressure. It was a randomized trial, people randomly split into groups, and half got a placebo, a fake with no active ingredient, flavored like peppermint so nobody knew who got what.

The real group took a tiny amount of food-grade peppermint oil in water, twice a day, for twenty days. Their systolic pressure, the top number, dropped from about one thirty to one twenty-two, about eight and a half points lower than the placebo group. The placebo group barely changed.

The caveats are big: only forty people and under three weeks. The researchers say it's no reason to stop prescribed medication, and never swallow essential oils that aren't made to be eaten. But a peppermint tea after dinner never hurt anyone.

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a quiet holiday week for cyber deals. I couldn't confirm a new Israeli cyber round or a new acquisition announced in the last forty-eight hours, so let's look at two smaller rounds from this week with useful lessons, and then the bigger Israeli picture.

First, Sequent, a London startup, raised two point two million dollars in a pre-seed round, the very first money before a seed. It was co-led by Frachtis and Metaplanet, the investment firm of Jaan Tallinn, the Skype co-founder, who was also an early backer of Anthropic. Sequent does formal verification, using math to prove that code follows its rules. An engineer writes a safety rule in plain words, like "no user can withdraw more than their balance," and Sequent's AI finds a mathematical proof that the code obeys it, which is then checked by a separate proof tool. It starts with smart contracts, programs that run automatically on a blockchain and often hold millions of dollars in crypto. Its first paying customer is Curve Finance, a well-known crypto trading platform. The CEO, Charles Cooper, leads the development of a programming language called Vyper, and the CTO, Ramana Kumar, was an AI safety researcher at Google DeepMind. Two point two million is small, but it's normal for a pre-seed, and the company says the money goes to engineering, sales and marketing hires.

Second, on Monday, Matters dot AI, a data security startup with teams in the San Francisco Bay Area and Bengaluru, India, raised twelve million dollars in a pre-Series A round, a bridge between seed and Series A. It was led by Tanglin Venture Partners, and it brings the total to about eighteen million. Matters tracks where a company's sensitive data lives, where it moves, and who, or which AI agent, touched it. It's used by more than fifty organizations, including big banks and retailers, and the money is meant to grow in North America.

Now the Israeli picture. Calcalist's running list of Israeli funding rounds shows seven Israeli cyber rounds in November alone that we've covered: Dante Security, Q dot security, Vero Security, Corma, Daylight, Bay and Dreeze. Most of them are about AI agents: watching them, controlling what they can reach, or using them to defend companies. That's the clearest trend of the month, and it tells you which story Israeli investors are buying right now.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, Sequent publishes its prices. Ninety-nine dollars a month for individuals, two hundred for auditors, five hundred for teams. That's rare in security, where most companies hide pricing behind "talk to sales." Public pricing works when your first buyers are individual engineers who want to try something today, without a procurement process. It's the product-led growth playbook, where the product itself brings in customers, and it's why developer-focused security companies often grow from the bottom of an organization up. The trade-off: once you go after big enterprises, you usually need custom deals, and the price list gets harder to keep simple.

Second, look at how Matters dot AI positions itself. It doesn't say it's a better version of one tool. It says it can replace five separate categories of data security products. That's the consolidation pitch we keep hearing, from CrowdStrike and Zscaler down to tiny startups, because buyers are tired of juggling vendors. And notice its CTO's line: "no one can trust an agent with data they cannot follow." It takes a fear, AI agents running loose with company data, and turns it into a clear reason to buy now. One sentence like that does more work than a page of features.

Third, a look back at what broke through this year. A roundup of twenty-five standout cyber campaigns by Everything-PR, a site whose owners are connected to the PR firm Five W, so read it as a PR firm's view, points to a common thread: make the invisible visible. NordVPN staged live hacks in city centers to show how easily public Wi-Fi traffic can be intercepted. Cloudflare turned its live attack data into dashboards that reporters keep quoting. And Wiz built its brand partly on its founder, Assaf Rappaport, as a public voice. Security is abstract, and the campaigns that win give people something they can see.

And a calendar note: AWS re:Invent, Amazon's big cloud conference in Las Vegas, starts Monday and runs through December fourth, with the main keynote by AWS CEO Matt Garman on Tuesday. Previews of the agenda are dominated by AI agents moving into real production, and there's no keynote dedicated to security. That's a signal in itself: security companies at the show will try to attach their message to the agent story, things like proving who an agent is, limiting what it's allowed to do, and keeping a record of what it did. Expect a wave of security launches and partnership news timed to the conference next week, and watch which companies tell that agent story most clearly.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Sequent. A brand-new company that says it's hiring in sales and marketing, with public pricing and a technical product that badly needs simple storytelling. Early-stage companies like this often hire a first marketer who does everything, from the website to developer content. It's based in London, so it may be remote-friendly, but it's worth checking.

Second, the Israeli AI-agent security wave. Dante, Bay and Vero all raised in November, and they're competing for the same buyers with similar-sounding messages. The company that tells the clearest story will win, and that's exactly the kind of problem a product marketer is hired to solve. If you reach out, lead with how you'd make their category easy to understand.
