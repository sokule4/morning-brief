## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Wednesday, October fourteenth, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. Israeli TV reports Netanyahu pushed President Trump to restart the war with Iran before Israel's election. The Houthis hit a building belonging to Saudi Arabia's oil giant, as their war with the Saudis keeps escalating. And the Vatican says Pope Leo has lung cancer, after surgery to remove part of his lung. Plus, the New York governor's debate, and a big week for Frieze in London. Let's get into it.

## Headlines

We start with a report that landed late last night and is going to dominate the day in Israel. According to Channel Twelve News, Prime Minister Netanyahu urged President Trump to resume fighting against Iran before Israel's election on October twenty-seventh, and before the US midterms a week later. Citing unnamed American officials, Channel Twelve says Netanyahu raised the idea "several times" in talks last week, pointing to worrying Iranian steps on its nuclear program. Trump reportedly told him to "go ahead and prepare," but made no commitment.

Here's the part that will get a lot of attention. Israeli security officials reportedly told the channel they see no new intelligence on Iran that would justify an operation in the next two to three weeks. And the army chief, Eyal Zamir, reportedly warned that renewed fighting could even lead to postponing the election. Netanyahu's office denies he tried to start a war before the vote, and says he doesn't discuss the content of his conversations with Trump. The White House didn't comment. Trump himself, asked about Iran on Tuesday, said, "We'll make a decision on Iran very soon," and later added, "Wait until you see what we do to Iran." Just a day earlier, he'd said the midterms have "no impact" on his thinking about the war.

Netanyahu also gave a long interview last night to Channel Fourteen, the pro-government channel, and made news on two fronts. First, he confirmed that Egypt's intelligence chief at the time, Abbas Kamel, secretly flew to Israel days before October seventh, 2023, but said, "He didn't meet with me." Reports say Kamel came to warn that Hamas was planning something big. Second, on his own corruption trial, he called for a criminal investigation of the people who prosecuted him, including former attorney general Avichai Mandelblit, calling the case "a political witch hunt." He has been on trial since 2020 and denies all wrongdoing. His main rival, Gadi Eisenkot of the Yashar party, answered that Netanyahu is "a liar" and in "complete panic."

So where does the race stand, thirteen days out? A useful breakdown from JTA: most polls put Netanyahu's bloc at about fifty-two or fifty-three seats, and the opposition bloc led by Eisenkot at roughly the same, after slipping from the high fifties. Both are well short of the sixty-one needed to govern, with the rest going to Arab-led parties. Thirty-eight parties are running, but no more than fifteen are expected to cross the electoral threshold. The analyst Shmuel Rosner sees "a very small chance that either bloc will be able to reach sixty-one seats." If nobody can form a coalition, Israel could head to yet another election, with Netanyahu staying on as caretaker prime minister, meaning he runs the government temporarily until a new one forms. On the right, the drama is about small parties. Itamar Ben Gvir ruled out a surplus-vote deal, an agreement where two parties pool leftover votes, with Ofer Winter's new party, and called Winter "Bennett two."

Now the Gulf, where the war between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis got more dangerous on Tuesday. The Houthis targeted an administrative building of Aramco, Saudi Arabia's giant state oil company, in Dhahran, in the east of the country. It caused a fire, and the Saudi Energy Ministry says no one was hurt. Over Riyadh, debris from two intercepted missiles fell on homes, injuring four people, including a child, and damaging fifteen houses. The Saudi-led coalition hit back at oil sites in Yemen, including a central fuel station in the capital, Sanaa, which it says the Houthis used to store missiles and drones. The Houthi military spokesman called Aramco sites "a legitimate target" and told civilians near them to evacuate.

The ripple effects keep spreading. Riyadh's main airport, King Khalid International, is suspended through October seventeenth. The US State Department now requires American government employees to get special permission to use any Saudi airport. Reuters reported that Saudi Arabia's public missile warning system had stopped working days before the deadly weekend attacks on its airports. Near the Strait of Hormuz, a tanker and a container ship were hit by projectiles of unknown origin. And President Trump, who called the Houthis "misbehaving very badly," floated a surprising idea: asking Turkey's President Erdogan to step in, saying "President Erdogan, I think, would do as I ask."

Next, news from Rome. Pope Leo the Fourteenth, who is seventy-one, has been diagnosed with lung cancer. On Tuesday, surgeons at Gemelli Hospital removed the lower part of his right lung, in a ninety-minute operation the Vatican called "successful," with "no complications." Tests confirmed adenocarcinoma, the most common type of lung cancer. The Vatican hasn't said what stage it is. Doctors note that nodules caught this way are usually early-stage. All his audiences are canceled through October twentieth, and his trip to South America, planned for November sixth, is now in question.

A few quick ones closer to home. The IMF, the International Monetary Fund, raised its forecast for Israel's economy this year to four and a half percent growth, a full point higher than in April, with inflation expected at just one point eight percent. At Ben Gurion Airport, an Ethiopian Airlines cargo flight was refused permission to land because one of its pilots was Indonesian, and reports say the crew list only reached Israel after the plane had taken off. Israel has been tightening crew checks since the flydubai attack. Separately, a Delta plane from New York was moved to a remote area after an anonymous bomb threat, and given the all-clear. And in the north, the ninth grader killed by an old Hezbollah bomblet on Monday's school hike has been named as Elazar Yair Wilensky. His family described him as "smart, wise and full of joy for life." Five children remain in serious condition. May his memory be a blessing.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where last night was the one and only debate for governor.

Governor Kathy Hochul and her Republican challenger, Nassau County Executive Bruce Blakeman, faced off for an hour at NY1's studios above Chelsea Market. Most reporters' verdict: both stuck to their talking points, with no big gaffes and no viral moment. Hochul's strategy was clear. She mentioned Blakeman's support for President Trump at least ten times, and told viewers, "This is your choice, New Yorkers." Blakeman shot back, "You like to talk about Donald Trump all the time because you don't want to talk about your record," and opened by saying, "I'm going to ask you to vote for change."

The sharpest fights were on immigration and money. Blakeman blamed the recent ICE shooting in Marble Hill on Hochul, saying, "It's because Kathy Hochul has made us a sanctuary state," meaning a state that limits cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. There had been a five-year-old in the back seat of that car, and Hochul said Blakeman's position should "shock the conscience of everybody." On energy bills, Blakeman said, "It's out of control, and it's because of you, Kathy," and promised to cut electric bills in half and allow fracking, a method of drilling for natural gas that New York has banned since 2014. Hochul pointed to rebate checks of up to two hundred dollars, and blamed Trump's war with Iran for higher gas prices. And the best line of the night may have been Hochul's, when Blakeman listed his celebrity endorsements, including "Will and Grace" star Debra Messing. Her reply: "Oh, you got two votes. Good for you."

Where it stands: public polls show Hochul ahead by nine to nineteen points. Early voting runs October twenty-fourth to November first, and Election Day is November third.

One story from the campaign trail worth knowing. On Sunday, Blakeman met privately with Hasidic leaders in Williamsburg and said Mayor Mamdani wouldn't stop antisemitic riots, asking, "you think Zohran Mamdani is going to stop them?" City Comptroller Mark Levine, a Hochul supporter, called that "wildly irresponsible."

At City Hall, Mayor Mamdani says he's keeping his police commissioner, even as he orders the first full review of how the NYPD uses force in a decade. "Jessica Tisch is the police commissioner, I have no plans to change that," he said. Tisch also apologized for a mistake: the department failed to notify the Civilian Complaint Review Board, the city agency that investigates police misconduct, when the officer involved in the 2024 killing of Win Rozario retired. She said, "As a result of human error, we failed to do so, and I apologize." The head of one police union called the review an appeasement of the mayor's "radical base."

This one's for your tech side. After a standoff over a subpoena, a legal order to testify, Elon Musk's company SpaceXAI has agreed to appear before the City Council's technology committee this Friday. It skipped an October fifth hearing where OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta all showed up. Council Speaker Julie Menin says "New Yorkers and the public at large deserve to hear directly" from AI companies The Council is weighing rules like human "kill switches" for AI.

And a fun one. New York Comic Con turned twenty this weekend, with about two hundred and fifty thousand people at the Javits Center. Six hundred and forty-nine fans dressed as Spider-Man tried to set a Guinness World Record. They fell short by just thirty-six Spideys. In sports, in case you missed it, the Knicks shut down contract talks with Karl-Anthony Towns and Josh Hart, just as the new season approaches.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting with a disturbing story from America.

The Times of Israel pulled together three alleged violent plots aimed at people linked to AIPAC, the main pro-Israel lobbying group in Washington. The newest: in late September, a man named Benny Caldera Junior was arrested in Texas after a friend went to the police. Prosecutors say he planned to "shoot up" the Texas state capitol, and that he showed the friend information about AIPAC and said these people "have to be exterminated." Investigators found a revolver with its serial number filed off and a shotgun. In another case, back in December 2024, a Florida man was arrested with three guns near what he believed was an AIPAC office. And in June, an alleged plot around a UFC event at the White House included a plan to target lawmakers who had taken pro-Israel donations. One thing all three have in common: friends or family tipped off police.

In Britain, new government figures show anti-Jewish hate crimes in England and Wales rose ten percent, to three thousand one hundred and sixty-two incidents in the year to March. And here's the number that stands out: Jews were targeted at a rate of a hundred and seventeen religious hate crimes per ten thousand people, by far the highest of any religious group. Muslims were next, at thirteen. The Community Security Trust, the charity that protects British Jewish communities, called it "an unacceptably high level." The government has pledged two hundred and fifty million pounds over three years for protection.

In the Netherlands, a fight over soccer. The city of Alkmaar has banned Israeli fans from Thursday's Europa League match, a major European club competition, between AZ Alkmaar and Hapoel Be'er Sheva. The mayor says she fears "disorder may break out," with protests planned. Israel's Foreign Ministry is demanding the ban be reversed, and Foreign Minister Gideon Saar raised it with his Dutch counterpart. The mayor's office says the decision is final. The 2024 attacks on Maccabi Tel Aviv fans in Amsterdam hang over matches like this.

Now a hopeful one. The Weitzman National Museum of American Jewish History in Philadelphia wants to join the Smithsonian, America's national family of museums in Washington. A congressional commission studying the idea held its first meeting this month. Its vice chair is the bestselling novelist Dara Horn, who wants schools to teach Jewish history as more than just the Holocaust, including Jewish contributions to American ideas like religious freedom. The timing is tricky: the Trump administration has been pushing for more control over the Smithsonian's content. The commission has two years to report.

And if you're in the mood for something moving, a new documentary called "The Righteous Road Trip," by Oscar-winning filmmaker Vanessa Roth, screens today at the Museum of Jewish Heritage in Manhattan. It follows a Jewish man on a roughly thirty-one-thousand-kilometer trip through Poland, Slovakia and Hungary, bringing Christmas trees and gifts to the Righteous Among the Nations, non-Jews who saved Jews during the Holocaust.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting with an update on Israel's biggest deal story.

Remember Decart, the Israeli AI startup in talks with Amazon? Reports last week put the price at about seven billion dollars. Now Calcalist reports the number is lower, about five and a half billion, with a deal possibly signed by the end of the month. That's still well above Decart's four billion dollar valuation in May. And there's a twist. Calcalist reports Anthropic had walked away from buying Decart about a month ago, after finishing due diligence, the deep check of a company's books before a deal, and would have paid more. Decart then tried Nvidia again, which said no, and turned to Amazon.

Next, an exit that sounds almost too good to be true. Rocket Software, a Boston-area company owned by Bain Capital, bought a one-year-old Israeli startup called Geniez AI, in a deal estimated at up to a hundred million dollars. Geniez had six employees. What does it do? It puts AI agents on mainframes, the giant old computers that still run banks and airlines, to monitor them and fix problems using plain language. The founders, Gil Peleg and Dan Shprung, are veterans of Mamram, the army's computing unit, and this is their second exit: they sold their last mainframe startup, Model9, in 2023. The lesson for you: repeat founders in boring-sounding markets can move incredibly fast.

There's a flip side in the Israeli job market. Microsoft's Israeli research center, which employs about three thousand people, has called around fifty people on its Copilot Studio team, the product for building AI agents, to pre-dismissal hearings, the step Israeli law requires before a layoff. About half are expected to find other roles inside. And the center's longtime head, Michal Braverman-Blumenstyk, is stepping down, with Ohad Jassin taking over in November.

One more from Israel. Rafael, the defense company, showed off a full-size model of Sky Sonic, an interceptor designed to stop hypersonic missiles, ones that fly more than five times the speed of sound and can change course.

Now globally. Google finally launched Gemini Four Argon, its newest frontier model, meaning one of the most advanced AI models available. The headline is price. It costs developers two dollars per million tokens going in, tokens being the small chunks of text AI reads and writes, and ten dollars per million coming out. That's about half of what Anthropic charges for its top model, and a fraction of OpenAI's. Independent rankings put it roughly tied with OpenAI's top models, but still behind Anthropic's newest Claude models. The introductory prices double after November thirtieth.

A story that sounds like a spy movie: a former OpenAI engineer, a thirty-five-year-old Saudi national, has been charged with hiding an audio recorder in a TV's USB port inside an executive conference room in San Francisco. OpenAI says there's no evidence its models, code or customer data were accessed, and he's been fired.

And quickly: Apple announced HomeView, a two-hundred-ninety-nine-dollar smart-home screen with face recognition and Siri. It ships November thirteenth, but not in Europe, because of the EU's AI rules. And the IMF warned that if the AI boom doesn't deliver the productivity it promises, global output could end up about one percent lower by 2028.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM, and a story that shows the gap between a headline and reality.

In July, CrowdStrike announced it was buying the Israeli cyber company XM Cyber, and the deal was described as a six-hundred-million-dollar exit. Now Calcalist reports that only about fifty of XM Cyber's roughly three hundred Israeli employees are left. That's about two hundred and fifty jobs gone. The structure explains it. CrowdStrike paid about a hundred and forty-five million dollars in cash, plus about two point one million of its own shares, and it bought the code and forty-five patents, not the customers, revenue or team. For context, the German retail giant Schwarz Group bought XM Cyber for seven hundred million dollars back in 2021. The takeaway for your job search: when you hear "exit," ask what was actually bought, because it tells you whether the sales and customer teams have a future.

On the hiring side, Pluto Security came out of stealth, meaning it went public about its existence, with forty-two million dollars. It was founded last year by veterans of Microsoft, Palo Alto Networks and Unit Eighty-Two Hundred, the Israeli army's elite intelligence tech unit. Pluto secures the AI tools employees build for themselves, the agents and automations that pop up inside companies without security's approval, sometimes called shadow AI. It says it already has more than forty paying enterprise customers, including Oscar Health and Sixt, and annual recurring revenue, the yearly value of its subscriptions, in the millions. It has over fifty employees in Israel and the US, so it's one to watch for GTM roles.

In case you missed it, here's the big picture. Calcalist found Israeli cyber startups raised about two point four billion dollars last quarter, close to half of all cybersecurity funding worldwide. About twenty of the twenty-six rounds went to companies working on AI agents and machine identities. That's the hottest corner of the market right now.

Two more Israeli acquisitions with a GTM angle. Salesforce is buying Loops, a Tel Aviv analytics startup, for about thirty million dollars, to fold into Tableau. Loops has an AI agent called Scout that watches your company's numbers, spots when something changes, and explains why, right inside Slack or email. And Freshworks is buying Torii, which helps companies see which software and AI tools employees are paying for, down to the level of individual tokens. Its CEO's pitch is a great one-liner: "One less blind spot, not one more dashboard."

Now two quotes worth stealing for interviews. At a London event, OpenAI's head of B2B marketing for Europe, the Middle East and Africa said, "Field is the one I would definitely put out as our strongest channel right now." Field marketing means in-person events and local teams. Even the most famous AI company in the world says face-to-face selling is winning. And on the buyer side, JPMorgan's CEO Jamie Dimon said the bank won't let outside AI agents operate inside its systems: "We're not going to allow just any outside agent to come in." If you're selling AI agents to banks, that's the objection you'll hear first.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Character dot AI, the chatbot company that went from a multibillion-dollar Google deal to bankruptcy court, with Disney waiting to pick up the pieces.

Character dot AI was founded in 2021 by two star Google researchers, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, who had built one of Google's early chatbots. Their idea was simple and very sticky. Instead of one assistant, you could chat with thousands of characters: a famous person, an anime hero, a therapist-style listener, or one you invented yourself. Young users loved it, and by 2023 investors valued it at about a billion dollars, making it a unicorn.

Then, in 2024, came one of the strangest deals in tech. Google paid to license Character's technology and hired the founders and part of the team back, in a deal widely reported at about two point seven billion dollars. It wasn't a purchase of the company. It was what people call an acqui-hire, buying the people rather than the business. The company that was left behind stopped building its own big AI models and focused on the consumer app.

That's when the trouble grew. The company faced lawsuits from families who said its chatbots harmed teenagers, and it moved to restrict how minors could use the app. In May of this year, Pennsylvania sued, alleging a chatbot presented itself as a licensed psychiatrist. And Disney had accused the startup of letting users chat with its copyrighted characters without permission.

Now the ending. On Monday, Character dot AI filed for Chapter Eleven, the type of bankruptcy that lets a company keep operating while it reorganizes or sells itself. It estimates assets and liabilities of fifty to a hundred million dollars. Disney has agreed to buy some of its assets, including intellectual property, for twenty million dollars, as the stalking-horse bidder, meaning its offer sets the minimum price in an auction and others can bid higher. Here's the twist: last month, Disney hired Character's former CEO, Karandeep Anand, as its chief technology officer. The sale could close around the start of December, and if no buyer keeps the app running, it could shut down.

Three lessons. One: users aren't a business model on their own. Millions of fans didn't add up to a sustainable company once the core team left. Two: in AI, trust and safety are part of the product. Lawsuits about kids shaped this company's fate as much as technology did. And three: watch where the talent goes. Google got the founders, Disney got the CEO, and the company itself got the bill.

## Celebs, culture and fashion

Okay, time for celebs, culture and fashion, starting with a case that's finally closed after thirty years.

Duane Davis, known as "Keffe D," was sentenced on Tuesday in Las Vegas to forty years to life in prison for the 1996 murder of Tupac Shakur. Prosecutors never said he pulled the trigger. They said he was the "shot caller" who organized it, in revenge after Tupac and Suge Knight beat up his nephew. Much of the case rested on Davis's own words, including his memoir, where he described being in the car that pulled up beside Tupac's. He's the only person ever charged, and he plans to appeal.

Remember Kelly Clarkson's feud with the Brooklyn magic shop? The store, Art of Play in Brooklyn Heights, has now given its side. It says the singing bird automaton Kelly wanted, nicknamed Molly, is permanently attached to the wall and isn't for sale, that a nearly identical one from a Seattle maker costs fifteen thousand dollars, and that it offered her a two-thousand-dollar bird from a German clockmaker instead. The owners say they've been harassed by "eight million people who have never visited our store." Kelly hasn't responded.

Now fashion, and a big signal from the money side. LVMH, the world's biggest luxury group, which owns Louis Vuitton and Dior, reported that organic sales, which strip out currency swings, fell five percent last quarter in its fashion and leather goods division, worse than analysts expected, mainly because of weak demand in China. Part of the problem is self-inflicted: Louis Vuitton sued a Chinese tea chain called Molly Tea over a logo it said was copied. It won the case, but lost the room, with Chinese social media calling for a boycott. LVMH shares are down almost forty percent this year.

And closer to home, Tel Aviv is getting its first fashion museum. The Tel Aviv Fashion Museum will open in early 2027 at the Azrieli Sarona complex, across three floors, founded by Dana Azrieli and the fashion historian Ya'ara Keydar. Keydar curated the Alber Elbaz show at the Design Museum Holon, and says, "For the first time in Israel, fashion has a permanent home." ## Art and design

Next, art and design, and today Frieze opens in London.

Frieze London and Frieze Masters run today through Sunday in Regent's Park, with almost three hundred galleries from forty-eight countries. Two new sections are getting attention. At Frieze London, "The Code Universe" looks at the forces shaping pop culture. At Frieze Masters, the fair for older art, "Queering Modernism" traces how gay, lesbian and queer artists shaped modern art, with works spread across the whole fair. One highlight is a shared booth from Victoria Miro and David Zwirner with Alice Neel's portraits of queer subjects. For your agency, Sophie, also look at the Editions section, limited-edition prints at accessible prices. It's where many new collectors make their first purchase. And tonight, Christie's sells the Mondrian we told you about, estimated at twenty to thirty million pounds, so we'll have the result tomorrow.

A beautiful and bittersweet story from Long Island. Temple Beth-El in Great Neck, a Reform synagogue that once had fifteen hundred families and now has about four hundred, has sold its building. That means it has to find a new home for its bimah, the raised platform where the Torah is read, designed by the sculptor Louise Nevelson. It's called "The White Flame of the Six Million," a Holocaust memorial about fifty feet long and eleven feet high, the longest continuous wall sculpture she ever made, and unusually, all white, from an artist famous for black. The sanctuary was deconsecrated, formally retired from religious use, after Yom Kippur, and the work will be taken apart and stored. Curators from the Jewish Museum in New York and the North Carolina Museum of Art have shown interest.

And a first in diplomacy through art. Israel returned twenty-two ancient artifacts to Greece, mostly ancient Greek painted pottery around two and a half thousand years old, plus a small bronze figurine. Israeli authorities seized them in their fight against antiquities trafficking. Greece's culture minister said, "This is the first time that Israel has returned antiquities to Greece."

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today: don't skip breakfast, especially on stressful weeks. A study from Binghamton University of about four hundred college students, published this year, found that people who ate breakfast five or more times a week had higher resilience, meaning they bounced back from stress better. Getting at least twenty minutes of movement a day helped too, while sleeping under six hours a night was linked to lower resilience. The lead researcher, Lina Begdache, put it nicely: resilient people "may still feel stress, but they are able to step back, think clearly, and adapt." It's a link, not proof. But something simple with protein before your first call is an easy win.
