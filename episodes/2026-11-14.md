## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, November fourteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. Last night, for just over four hours, not a single plane was allowed to take off anywhere in the United States, and flights to America from Israel and Europe were halted too. Officials blame an old, overloaded communications system, not a cyberattack. Here at home, Gadi Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of a "racist and violent" campaign against Arab citizens, while Netanyahu warned the ultra-Orthodox parties that Eisenkot's camp "will use you and throw you away." And in your Cyber Watch: an Israeli cyber company valued at one point five billion dollars before it was officially founded, and IBM buying its way back into security.

## Israel

We start here at home, where the race to form a government got sharper and more personal on Friday. Quick reminder of the math: in last month's election, Eisenkot's four-party bloc won forty-eight seats, Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc won fifty-three, and the Arab-led parties won a record nineteen. Nobody has the sixty-one seats needed for a majority. Eisenkot holds the mandate, and he has twenty-eight days from when he got it, about a week ago, plus a possible fourteen-day extension. So the real deadline is early December.

The big moment Friday was a long post Eisenkot wrote on X. He accused Netanyahu and his circle of driving a "racist and violent" campaign against Arab citizens for political reasons, and called efforts to push them to the margins "an anti-state act." He wrote, "The prime minister of Israel is the prime minister of all citizens, including those who did not vote for him," and, "One cannot, should not, and I will not allow an entire sector to be slandered." He also stood by his own lawmaker, Elyasaf Peretz, whose mother threatened that he would quit the Knesset if the party sat with an Arab party, and condemned threats against Yashar lawmakers and their families.

Why does this matter? Because Eisenkot is walking a tightrope. He has said he won't build a government that depends on the Arab parties, partly because partners like Avigdor Liberman, the leader of Yisrael Beytenu, a secular right-wing party, refuse. But he can't afford to insult a fifth of the country, or the parties that endorsed him. On the other side, Netanyahu has made unproven claims of election fraud by Arab parties, which Israel's election authorities rejected.

Meanwhile, Netanyahu is fighting for the ultra-Orthodox. According to the Jerusalem Post, he warned the Haredi parties against joining Eisenkot, saying, "They will use you and throw you away," and a source says he's personally calling rabbis and sending messengers to the religious leadership. That's because Eisenkot's preferred plan, Ynet reports, is to bring in United Torah Judaism first, forming a minority government of about fifty-five seats, then expand with Shas or with defectors from Likud. The fallback is a unity government with Likud itself, and Eisenkot's team is set to resume talks with Likud next week. The obstacle hasn't changed: United Torah Judaism wants yeshiva students exempt from the draft, and Eisenkot campaigned on service for all.

Also on the Arab side, Joint List chair Yousef Jabareen hinted at talks with Eisenkot's bloc and listed demands, including repealing the Kaminitz Law, the law that eases demolition of illegally built homes, which hits Arab towns hard. And on Monday, Liberman is set to finally take over the Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, the panel that oversees the army and sees classified intelligence. He called it "the first step toward establishing a good government with a stable coalition majority."

Two more things. Israel barred Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the far-left French presidential candidate, and Mathilde Panot, who heads his party's group in parliament, from entering the country. France's foreign minister, Jean-Noël Barrot, called the move "demagogic," meaning it plays to emotions for political gain. And more than fifty countries and organizations, led by Britain, Qatar and Jordan, urged Israel to let winter supplies into Gaza right away: shelter, fuel, water and medical supplies, warning of hypothermia and disease. Israel says the claims distract from Hamas's responsibility, and it is reportedly refusing items it says Hamas could also use for military purposes.

## World

Now to the world, and a day when American air travel simply stopped. At about four in the afternoon New York time on Friday, the FAA, America's aviation regulator, ordered a nationwide ground stop, meaning no plane on the ground anywhere in the country could take off. Planes already in the air, about thirty-five hundred of them, were allowed to land. At the FAA's request, Eurocontrol, the European body that coordinates air traffic and includes Israel among its forty-two members, also suspended all departures to the United States. Here in Israel, that hit overnight flights.

What happened? Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said routers, the equipment that moves data between systems, got overloaded at the twenty-two regional centers that guide planes between airports, and started sending "inconsistency in the data from the radars." So they stopped everything as a precaution. CBS reports an update to a private communications network run by the contractor L3Harris set off a reboot and then a crash. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there's no sign of a cyberattack. The stop was lifted at eight-fifteen p.m., after just over four hours. Reuters calls it the longest nationwide grounding since the September eleventh attacks. More than forty-seven hundred flights were canceled on Friday, about ten thousand were delayed, and around seven hundred more are already canceled for today.

The deeper story is age. Duffy said bluntly, "What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system." Congress approved twelve and a half billion dollars last year to modernize it, and on Friday the FAA and White House asked for another thirty billion. Mullin estimates a full overhaul could cost about fifty billion.

On Iran, the war at sea is getting more dangerous. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas hit a naval mine and exploded while using what it called an "unauthorized and dangerous route" through the Strait of Hormuz, and that four oil tankers were hit by explosions on Wednesday and Thursday. And a confidential report from the IAEA seen by the Associated Press says Iran told inspectors that talks on monitoring its nuclear program can't go ahead while it faces military threats. From Washington, the signal is pressure without new bombs: the Times of Israel reports Secretary of State Marco Rubio says there's "no need" for further strikes because the economic pain on Iran "is so great." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who moved onto a military base after Iranian threats, says he won't be intimidated.

And on Ukraine: Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met President Zelensky in Ireland and promised to include European officials in the next round of talks with Russia. Kushner said Putin is "willing to give it a try." But France's President Macron posted that there is "no more US support" for Ukraine, and that Europe must carry the load.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where Mayor Mamdani got something from President Trump. The two spoke directly, and Trump agreed to delay enforcing new food stamp rules for at least ninety days, nationwide. Here's the background. SNAP, the federal food stamp program, now requires stores that accept it to stock at least seven varieties in each of four food groups, dairy, grains, protein, and fruits and vegetables, up from three. That's at least eighty-four items on the shelf. For bodegas, the small corner stores New York runs on, that's a big ask, and SNAP can be fifteen to thirty percent of their sales. More than ten thousand bodegas operate in the city, and about one point six million New Yorkers get SNAP.

The Agriculture Department had refused to delay the rules earlier this month. Then Mamdani called Trump and Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins himself. "I am grateful that President Trump has agreed to delay," he said, adding he'll "give credit whenever someone takes a decision that makes life easier for working New Yorkers." The rules still stand; the city now has three months to help store owners comply, including connecting them with distributors. It's a striking moment: a democratic socialist mayor and Trump finding a deal on bodegas.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world. Former Missouri congresswoman Cori Bush, a member of the progressive "Squad" who blamed the pro-Israel lobby AIPAC for her primary defeat, says Israel revoked her visa. She says a TSA agent, an American airport security officer, told her at a checkpoint on her way to Tel Aviv. She recently registered as a foreign agent, someone officially working for a foreign group, for a European organization that runs what JNS calls anti-Israel tours of the West Bank. Israel's Diaspora Ministry says people working for such groups need a work visa sponsored by the group.

Trump, meanwhile, complained that most Jewish voters in Michigan backed Democrat Abdul El-Sayed in last week's Senate race. "It's a tough group to get," he said, repeating his long-running frustration that Jews vote Democratic despite his support for Israel.

And a more hopeful note: ZAKA, the Israeli volunteer rescue organization best known for recovering victims after attacks, and Chabad, the Hasidic movement with emissaries all over the world, announced a joint international emergency unit. It will train Jewish communities in first response, search and rescue, and caring for the dead according to Jewish law. ZAKA's CEO, Dubi Weissenstern, said that together they can "reach every Jew, wherever they may be."

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the other outage of the week. Microsoft's cloud services went down starting Thursday afternoon, and more than a day later, some still weren't fully back. Outlook, Teams and Copilot, its AI assistant, were hit, with email the worst, for users in seventy-four countries. The list of victims is wild: banks in Taiwan went back to fax machines, Euronext, the European stock exchange operator, told companies to place trades by phone, Norway's parliament lost email, Britain's health service switched to paper records, and rail operators in Germany, Britain and Canada had trouble with tickets. Microsoft blamed overloaded servers, said there was no sign of a cyberattack, and reported "encouraging recovery progress." It has more than four hundred fifty million commercial Microsoft 365 subscribers.

Put this next to the FAA story and you get the theme of the week: the world runs on a few giant systems, and when one of them fails, there's no backup. A Cambridge researcher, Walter Pasquarelli, called the outage "first and foremost a communication risk," meaning companies need a plan for how to talk to people when email itself is gone.

## Business story

Our business story is about a fight over who owns the customer. United Airlines just became the first American airline to say it will block personal AI agents, the assistants that act for you online, from booking flights. That covers its app and website, and bookings through travel sites. United's official reasons are security: if you hand an AI assistant your login, it can see everything in your account, make purchases you didn't want, or spend your miles. It also points to rules, like check-in, where a real person has to confirm their bags don't hold anything dangerous.

But CEO Scott Kirby gave the real business reason at an event in Chicago, according to the Wall Street Journal: "I don't want to create another middleman." Airlines spent twenty years pulling customers away from travel agencies and onto their own websites, where they can sell upgrades and loyalty cards. Now Meta, OpenAI and others want AI assistants to do the booking, which would put a new company between United and you. Delta's CEO, Ed Bastian, says he's "cautious" too, while Southwest went the other way and launched a ChatGPT plug-in. The lesson for any brand: when your customer stops visiting your website, you lose the chance to sell to them. Expect more companies to pick a side.

## Pop culture, celebs and fashion

Now pop culture, and a comeback test. "Ebenezer," Johnny Depp's dark take on "A Christmas Carol," opened in theaters Friday. It's from Paramount, directed by Ti West, who's known for horror, with Depp as Scrooge, Rupert Grint as Bob Cratchit, Ian McKellen as the ghost of Jacob Marley, and Tramell Tillman from "Severance" as the Ghost of Christmas Present. It's Depp's first big studio movie since his twenty twenty-two court fight with his ex-wife, Amber Heard. In music, U2 released "Carnaval De Luz," their sixteenth studio album, and it holds an emotional final track: "Torn," a country duet written with Dolly Parton. ## Art and design

Next, art meets fashion. Balenciaga is celebrating twenty-five years of the Le City bag, the slouchy motorcycle bag that defined the early two thousands, with Damien Hirst, the British artist famous for his dot paintings and a shark in a tank. The collection covers his multicolored spots arranged in a butterfly shape, butterfly-wing mandalas, his famous works as embroidered patches, and his Spin Paintings, made by pouring paint onto a spinning canvas, stretched across the bag. ## Travel

Now travel, and something brand new from Ben Gurion. El Al will launch the first nonstop flights between Israel and Argentina, Tel Aviv to Buenos Aires, starting Sunday, November twenty-ninth. Flights go twice a week, Sundays and Tuesdays from Tel Aviv, on a Boeing seven eighty-seven with business, premium and economy cabins. It's a long one: fifteen and a half to sixteen and a half hours, over twelve thousand kilometers. Until now you had to connect, through Madrid, Rome or Miami. Buenos Aires is a great December idea, because it's early summer there: steak, tango, and one of the biggest Jewish communities in the world.

And if you or anyone you know is flying to the US this weekend, expect knock-on delays and cancellations after Friday's ground stop. Airlines say it'll take time to get planes and crews back where they belong, so check your flight before you leave for the airport.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and those sudden jolts as you drift off to sleep, the feeling of falling that snaps you awake. They're called sleep starts, or hypnic jerks, and they're common. A new study in the journal PLOS Mental Health, from the University of Salzburg in Austria, surveyed one hundred ninety-four adults. People who got these jolts at least weekly had worse sleep quality, more insomnia, and higher anxiety. Many recalled them coming during times of emotional stress more than after short nights. It's a small survey, and the lead author admits, "Our data cannot show what causes what." The practical idea: if the jolts keep you up, work on winding down, not just sleeping longer. A calm routine before bed, less screen time, slow breathing. And if it's affecting your sleep a lot, there's a proven approach called cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, a short talk therapy that retrains sleep habits.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

The last two days were quiet for brand-new rounds, so we start with the strangest Israeli deal of the month, which we haven't covered yet.

In case you missed it, Globes reported this week on Ribon, a Tel Aviv cyber company that was valued at one point five billion dollars before it was even formally incorporated. Investors committed one hundred fifty to two hundred million dollars in three tranches, meaning installments, each at a higher price. The first came at a one hundred million dollar valuation, led by Sequoia, Peter Thiel's Founders Fund and investor Zach Frankel. The second, at five hundred million, added Vine Ventures, the Israeli fund that, also this week, closed a two hundred thirty-six million dollar third fund, taking it past half a billion dollars under management. The third, at one point five billion, was led by General Catalyst. Globes says there's no known precedent for a round that sets a unicorn valuation from day one.

Why the faith? The team. Ribon is led by Ehud Schneorson, a former commander of Unit 8200 who co-founded Paragon, the spyware company sold for about nine hundred million dollars, together with co-founder Idan Nurick. It's also hiring people from Wiz. What it builds is "sovereign" cybersecurity: AI-based cloud systems for governments, so a state doesn't have to depend on outside software vendors or foreign AI model providers. Remember yesterday's story about digital sovereignty becoming a buying decision in Europe? Ribon is betting on that same idea, at the level of whole governments. One catch Globes points out: a sky-high valuation on day one can hurt employees, because their stock options get a higher exercise price, the price they pay to buy the shares, under American tax rules.

Second, IBM is back in security. On Thursday, it announced it's buying AuthMind, an identity security company, meaning it watches who and what is using a company's logins. Specifically, AuthMind tracks how credentials, like passwords and access tokens, are actually used after they're handed out, and it spots "shadow agents," AI bots working outside the company's rules. It will be folded into HashiCorp Vault, the credentials tool IBM already owns. AuthMind had raised about twenty-eight million dollars, IBM's venture arm invested in its seed round in twenty twenty-three, and the price wasn't disclosed. It's IBM's first notable security buy since it sold its QRadar cloud security business to Palo Alto Networks in twenty twenty-four. The reason is scale. Gartner, the research firm, predicts that by twenty twenty-eight, an average Fortune five hundred company will have more than one hundred fifty thousand AI agents in use, up from fewer than fifteen in twenty twenty-five. And SailPoint, an identity company, says it found more than ten thousand AI agents nobody knew about at a single Fortune five hundred customer. You can't secure what you can't see, and that's AuthMind's pitch.

This fits a pattern. CB Insights counts nine startups securing agent and machine identities bought since January, several of them Israeli-founded, like Oasis, which Cyera bought for up to a billion dollars, and Apono. In the deals with known prices, buyers paid a median of about five times the money the startup had raised. That's why investors keep funding this category: there's a clear exit.

Third, a quick number on Israel's biggest exit. Calcalist ran the math on Palo Alto's purchase of CyberArk. It was announced at about twenty-five billion dollars, recorded at twenty-one billion at closing in February, and the Palo Alto shares CyberArk holders received are now worth about thirty-seven billion, almost double. That's even after Palo Alto's stock fell about twenty-two percent in the past month. Palo Alto reports earnings this Wednesday.

## People moves

On people moves, I couldn't confirm a new marketing chief at a cyber company in the last two days, but two revenue and finance hires in the fraud and identity space are worth knowing.

Feedzai, which uses AI to catch payment fraud for banks, named Joost van Mook its CRO, the executive in charge of sales. He comes from Backbase, a banking software company, where he was chief commercial officer, and spent more than a decade at SAP. He'll lead global sales, revenue operations and partnerships across North America, Europe, Latin America and Asia-Pacific. Feedzai's recent win shows what he's selling: it signed on to monitor fraud for Canada's upcoming Real-Time Rail, the country's new instant-payments system.

And Socure, an American identity verification and fraud company recently valued at five point two billion dollars, named Rohan Sivaram its CFO, the chief financial officer. He helped take the data company Confluent public, and earlier worked in finance at Palo Alto Networks. Socure also recently bought a company called Fravity and launched Agent Intelligence, tools that check AI agents and the people or companies behind them. Notice the positioning: an identity company that used to verify humans is now selling the verification of bots. And when a company at that valuation hires a CFO with IPO experience, it's often a hint that it's thinking about going public.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and an interview you should read in full. CTech spoke with Daniel Bernard, CrowdStrike's chief business officer, who runs its channel, alliances and partnerships. Before that, he spent four years as CMO of SentinelOne, so he's a marketer who now runs distribution. CrowdStrike employs more than five hundred people in Israel and has bought six Israeli companies since twenty twenty. "We're long on the country," he said.

Three lessons. First, partnering, in his words, is "the rule," and acquisition "the exception." CrowdStrike wants startups to plug into its Falcon platform and sell to its customers, which means partner marketing is a growth engine, not a side job. Second, he argues AI makes product features easy to copy, but customer trust is hard to copy. That's a direct argument for brand. Third, his advice to Israeli startups: they've historically focused too much on product and technology and struggled to build businesses abroad. After a round, he says, hire top talent in other markets, let decisions be made outside headquarters, and invest in marketing and brand.

A second voice backs him up from the money side. Ziv Conen, a partner at New Era, a Boston-based Israeli venture firm, told CTech, "The next Palo Alto has already been born, and in all likelihood, it happened in Tel Aviv." But he also warned that well-funded rivals can outrun better products: "Capital carries enormous weight today, perhaps even more than we would like to admit." The marketer's takeaway: the best technology doesn't win by itself. Distribution, partners and a brand people trust are what turn funding into market share.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, CrowdStrike's Israel operation. A company with five hundred people here, a chief business officer who came from marketing, and a strategy built on partners, means roles in partner and alliance marketing. That's a strong fit for a growth marketer who understands startups.

Second, the agent-identity startups CB Insights names as likely acquisition targets, like Token Security, Keycard, Aembit and Defakto. Companies racing toward an exit usually push hard on pipeline and brand to raise their price, which means marketing hires. Just go in knowing the company may be bought within a year or two.

And third, keep Ribon on your radar. It's early, and it sells to governments, so it won't need a big marketing team tomorrow. But a company valued at one point five billion dollars on day one will eventually need someone to tell its story to the world.
