## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Thursday, November fifth, and this is your Morning Brief. Today is decision day at the President's Residence: Herzog meets the last parties, ending with Eisenkot and Netanyahu, and the Arab parties could push Eisenkot close to a majority. In America, Democrats have won the Senate too, and Vice President JD Vance says the real reason Republicans lost is the Iran war and the relationship with Israel. And Trump now says the US may need "a heavy hit" on Iran, even as Qatar pushes a new peace plan. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start here at home. Yesterday Justice Noam Sohlberg, the head of the Central Elections Committee, handed President Herzog the certified results of last week's election. The committee approved them three to zero, with two abstentions, and said its investigation found nothing suggesting the vote was compromised. Sohlberg added that the claims of foreign interference were unproven, and warned against casting doubt on Arab citizens' votes. That matters because Netanyahu and his allies have repeatedly claimed, without evidence, that the Arab parties "stole" the election.

Then Herzog opened his consultations, the meetings where each party tells the president who it wants as prime minister. Quick reminder of the math: the Knesset has one hundred twenty seats, you need sixty-one to govern, and nobody has it. Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three seats, Eisenkot's camp forty-eight, and the two Arab-led parties a record nineteen.

Here's how night one went. The Religious Zionist party, Shas, the Sephardi ultra-Orthodox party, and Moshe Feiglin's small Zehut party all recommended Netanyahu. But Feiglin also questioned the election's legitimacy and said he'd ask Herzog to call another vote. Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu, a right-wing secular party, recommended Eisenkot. And Ra'am, the Islamist Arab party, announced it will recommend Eisenkot too. Ynet estimates that brings him to about fifty-six.

The big surprise is on the ultra-Orthodox side. Degel HaTorah, the faction that holds four of United Torah Judaism's seven seats, was instructed by its rabbis to recommend neither candidate. The trigger: Netanyahu's coalition failed to pass a law exempting Haredi men from military service, which is the community's top demand. Another faction leader, Yitzhak Goldknopf, says he'll abstain too. So Netanyahu, who started with fifty-three, may end up with as few as forty-six recommendations, a number Eisenkot made a point of repeating yesterday.

There were sharp moments. Herzog clashed with Religious Zionist lawmaker Orit Strock, who argued that Arab parties shouldn't be legitimate coalition partners. Herzog called those generalizations troubling, condemned what he called "the coarseness and racist expressions" of recent days, and said Arab citizens, about a fifth of the population, deserve "great respect." He also supports a full investigation into the suspected foreign interference. Meanwhile, the attorney general publicly accused the head of the Shin Bet of undermining the rule of law, because he reportedly wants to question four Arab politicians but has only provided evidence about one.

Today, eight more parties meet Herzog, including the Joint List, the other Arab-led party, with eleven seats. If it recommends Eisenkot, he passes sixty-one. Channel 12 reports it will likely coordinate with him, endorsing if he asks or abstaining if he prefers. Why would he prefer that? Because his own partner, Liberman, says a government can't rely on Arab parties. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, and Likud go last tonight. Then Herzog has several days to choose. His pick gets twenty-eight days to form a government, plus a possible fourteen-day extension. If that fails, Israel votes again early next year. Herzog's message: "The elections are over. Now is the time to face reality."

Now to America, where the picture is finally clear: Democrats have won the Senate, so they'll control both chambers of Congress from January. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed beat Republican Mike Rogers and will be the first Muslim ever elected to the Senate. In Maine, Democrat Troy Jackson beat Senator Susan Collins, who conceded. In Ohio, former Senator Sherrod Brown is back, beating Senator Jon Husted. And in Iowa, Josh Turek beat Ashley Hinson. Two races are still open. In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads Senator Dan Sullivan by less than half a point, and Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, where voters rank candidates and the lowest are eliminated round by round, so it could take weeks. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by just sixty-five votes at last count.

Chuck Schumer, likely the next majority leader, the senator who controls what reaches the floor, promised to "entangle" Trump "in legislative razor wire." And the blame game started fast. JD Vance posted that "the real reason we lost is obvious," calling it "the war," and listed ways to win voters back: end the Iran war, focus on the cost of living, and change America's relationship with Israel. Trump himself told Fox News that "a part of it was Iran." For Israel, that's a striking shift: the vice president is now openly treating closeness to Israel as a political liability.

Which brings us to Iran itself. On Wednesday Trump told Fox News, "We have to do something large," and mentioned a possible "heavy hit," just after telling the Financial Times he had no immediate plans to attack. Background, quickly: after a forty-day US and Israeli air campaign earlier this year, a June agreement paused things, but strikes resumed, and the US now blockades Iran's ports while Iran chokes the Strait of Hormuz. Iran has offered to reopen the strait over two days if seven conditions are met, including lifting sanctions and ending the blockade. Iran's national security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, called Washington's reply "unconvincing." Qatar is now pushing a draft with three days of confidence-building steps, small moves to rebuild trust, aiming for direct talks "within days." On the water, the Revolutionary Guard says four oil tankers hit mines south of the strait and are still burning, a claim nobody has independently confirmed. Iran's chief negotiator read the US vote as "NO TO WAR," while the supreme leader, Iran's top authority, says anyone expecting America to change is "seriously mistaken." What's next: US planners are finishing preparations for a major operation, but officials stress there's no order yet.

One more big story you may have missed. In Spain, last Wednesday, a high-speed train from Madrid to Seville slammed into a stopped train from behind near Urda, in Toledo province, south of Madrid. Several cars fell down an embankment, sixty-nine people were killed and about a hundred injured. It's Spain's second major rail disaster this year, after a crash at Adamuz killed forty-six. On Tuesday, Transport Minister Óscar Puente resigned and said he won't run in Spain's general election on November twenty-ninth, explaining he couldn't stay after "two accidents of this severity in less than a year." The conservative opposition calls it "a systemic failure," and Vox, the far-right party, wants criminal accountability for Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez. The government approved thirty million euros in compensation and ordered an extraordinary safety review. With an election in three and a half weeks, this lands right on Sánchez.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, and now we know what actually changes after all five ballot proposals passed. The biggest one for daily life: outdoor dining. Restaurants will no longer need public hearings, and the City Council loses its veto over individual sidewalk cafes. The city estimates approvals will drop from up to eight months to about two, starting next March. Another lets the Department of Transportation start street safety work, like bike lanes, while it's still doing community outreach, instead of after, which the administration says could speed projects by at least a third. There's a single hub for building permits, merging forty approvals from eighteen offices, though that won't launch until twenty twenty-nine. And the rainy day fund now has a target of twelve percent of tax revenue. All five won with between sixty-two and seventy-two percent. Mamdani's reaction: "Now comes the work of turning these votes into results." Critics like Common Cause warn that speed shouldn't come at the expense of public participation.

Mamdani also weighed in on the national results, calling them "a referendum on politics at large." His line: "The American people want change they can feel in their wallets," and "Our job is not to better manage a decline." He celebrated El-Sayed's win, and said he sees limited room to work with Trump.

And just north of the city, Republican Congressman Mike Lawler conceded to Democrat Cait Conley, a former national security official, in the Hudson Valley's seventeenth district, which includes all of Rockland County. More than one in five voters there are Jewish, so both candidates ran as strongly pro-Israel. Lawler, who is Catholic and close to the district's large Orthodox community, tried to tie Conley to Mamdani. It didn't work.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and the day after the midterms. JTA published six Jewish takeaways, and a few stand out. First, a weakened Trump is bad news for Netanyahu, who has leaned on him for years. Second, Hakeem Jeffries, likely the next House Speaker, the leader of the House, and Chuck Schumer will face heavy pressure from their own base to bring bills limiting support for Israel to a vote. Third, AIPAC, the big pro-Israel lobby, spent hundreds of millions but hasn't found a winning formula, and in many races its backing became a liability. And fourth, a strong night for Jewish politicians with presidential hopes. Jon Ossoff won reelection in Georgia with nearly sixty percent, and JTA says that may make him the frontrunner to become the first Jewish president. Josh Shapiro in Pennsylvania and JB Pritzker in Illinois also won big. All of them have criticized Netanyahu.

A fight to watch: in Illinois, progressive Jewish Democrat Daniel Biss, the grandson of Holocaust survivors, easily won a House seat outside Chicago. On election night, the Anti-Defamation League's Midwest office said it had "serious concerns" about his "approach to antisemitism," pointing to how he handled a pro-Palestinian encampment at Northwestern as mayor of Evanston. Biss hit back, saying the ADL now "traffics in fear, intimidation and extremism." J Street, the liberal pro-Israel group, called the ADL's timing a "shanda," a disgrace. It shows how split the American Jewish establishment is right now.

And Abdul El-Sayed openly welcomed Trump's announcement that US military aid to Israel will end, calling it a case of "broken clock right twice a day."

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech and AI, starting with a sad Israeli story. Explorium, a Tel Aviv data startup, is shutting down after almost nine years and more than one hundred twenty-seven million dollars raised from investors like Insight Partners. It began by enriching companies' data, connecting them to thousands of outside sources to predict things like which customers will leave. When that became a commodity, it made a pivot, a big change of direction, to tools for AI agents, including a prospecting app that drew over three hundred fifty thousand users. Fun fact: one co-founder, Maor Shlomo, left to start Base44, which Wix bought. The founders' goodbye note: "it all just wasn't enough." The lesson: in AI, a whole product category can become cheap almost overnight.

At OpenAI, two numbers. The company says ChatGPT now has one point two billion weekly users, and estimates its search-style questions equal about twenty percent of Google's search volume. That's OpenAI's own estimate, not an audited figure, but it shows who it wants to beat. Less flattering: Reuters, citing five sources, reports that employees warned leaders about security problems even before ChatGPT launched, and OpenAI just hired a security partner whose founders previously probed Anthropic's systems in a bug-bounty test, a paid hunt for security flaws. OpenAI disputes that it neglected security.

And Google says its headcount hit a record, partly thanks to absorbing Wiz, even while it expands buyouts, offers paying employees to leave voluntarily.

## Business story

Our business story is about one of Israel's oldest tech giants, and a lesson in depending on one customer. Amdocs makes the billing software that telecom companies use to charge their customers. It had four point seven billion dollars in revenue last year and about five thousand employees in Israel. One of its biggest customers, for years, is T-Mobile, the American wireless carrier. Now the two are at war. Each has sent the other a notice claiming a "material breach," a serious violation of their contracts, and both threaten to end the deal or sue.

So when Amdocs gave its guidance, its forecast for the coming year, it predicted revenue falling fourteen to eighteen percent, mostly because of T-Mobile. The stock fell forty-four percent in after-hours trading, the trading that happens after the market closes, and hit its lowest level since twenty fourteen. CEO Shimie Hortig insists "the rest of our business remains robust." And CTech reports filings showing that a former CEO sold twenty-two and a half million dollars' worth of shares before the plunge.

Amdocs' escape plan is insurance. It's buying EIS, a San Francisco insurance software company, for three hundred forty million dollars, betting that billing systems for insurers look a lot like billing for phone companies. But it tried diversifying before, buying a video company in twenty eighteen, and that didn't deliver. It already cut about three thousand jobs, ten percent of its workforce, six months ago. The takeaway for any company, and any marketer: when one customer is a big slice of your revenue, your whole story depends on that relationship.

In fashion and beauty, in case you missed it last week, Lady Gaga is now CEO of her own makeup brand, Haus Labs, taking over from co-founder Ben Jones, who left in October.

And a cautionary tale. Nanushka, the Hungarian label known for its minimalist designs and outerwear, has closed every store it had, from New York to London to Budapest, and its website is down. Staff fell from more than one hundred fifty to about twenty, and it has paused wholesale, selling its collections to other stores. Its own leadership blames expanding too fast. The brand hasn't filed for bankruptcy and hopes to return for autumn twenty twenty-seven. Fast growth looks great on Instagram. It's brutal on a balance sheet.

## Art and design

Now art, and the art world's biggest two weeks are coming to New York. The November marquee sales, the auction houses' top evening auctions, start on the sixteenth. Christie's highlight is Kandinsky's "Betonte Ecken" from nineteen twenty-two, estimated at sixty to eighty million dollars, once owned by Solomon Guggenheim himself. Sotheby's has an Edward Hopper at twenty to thirty million, a Mary Cassatt from Armand Hammer's collection, and a Kusama at ten to fifteen million. Christie's expects more than eight hundred million dollars across the week. Market watchers say the real test is the middle, works between two and ten million dollars: are there genuine competing bidders, or just guarantees propping up the prices?

The softness is real: the Art Newspaper reports that four galleries in Vienna are closing, as established collectors turn cautious and younger buyers have limited budgets. Meanwhile, Sydney's new Powerhouse museum, a six hundred forty-one million Australian dollar project, opens Saturday, but awkwardly, with senior executives stepped aside during a misconduct and procurement investigation.

## Travel

Travel now, with a new rule if you're flying to the US. Since Tuesday night, every passenger on a flight to America, including US citizens, must sign a statement that they haven't been in the Democratic Republic of Congo in the past twenty-one days, the virus's longest incubation period, the time between infection and symptoms. It's because of the Ebola outbreak there, which has passed ten thousand cases. Airlines will collect it at check-in or the gate, and can refuse boarding without it. The rule runs until December eighteenth. The CDC says the risk to the public stays low; it's just one more form, so don't be surprised.

Good news for Canada trips: Air Canada brings back direct Tel Aviv to Toronto flights on November thirtieth, four times a week, weeks earlier than planned. But December economy tickets average about one thousand one hundred fifty-five dollars, around thirty percent more than last year. Connecting on British Airways or Lufthansa is roughly fifteen percent cheaper, if you don't mind a longer day.

And if Spain is on your list, after the crash the government is temporarily cutting the number of trains on the Madrid to Seville high-speed line, so book early and check schedules.

## Wellness

And your wellness idea: go to bed a little earlier. A New York University study of almost two hundred adults with prediabetes, blood sugar above normal but not yet diabetes, who slept less than six and a half hours, found that each hour earlier people fell asleep was linked with about one point three pounds of weight loss over eight weeks, regardless of total sleep. It's a link, not proof, but the coaching method is easy to copy: move your bedtime fifteen minutes earlier every few nights, and keep the bed for sleep, not scrolling.
