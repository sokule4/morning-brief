## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Wednesday, October twenty-eighth, and this is your Morning Brief. Israel voted, and the morning after looks like this: Likud is the largest party, nobody has a majority, and the count could take days. Netanyahu used his election-night speech to talk about a "historic, one-time opportunity" against Iran, just as the Wall Street Journal reported that Washington warned Gulf states about a short, intense war after the US midterms. And in lighter news, Jay-Z will headline next year's Super Bowl halftime show. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start with the election, because a lot happened overnight. First, the turnout. By ten last night, when polls closed, seventy-seven point seven percent of eligible voters had cast a ballot, according to the Central Elections Committee, the independent body that runs Israeli elections. That compares with about seventy point six percent at the same hour in twenty twenty-two, and it's the highest at that hour since nineteen ninety-nine. People waited in long lines all day, including in Arab towns, where turnout was described as unprecedented.

Then came the exit polls, the TV channels' surveys of voters leaving polling stations. The first round at ten o'clock looked like a setback for Netanyahu. Channel twelve had the opposition bloc, the parties of Gadi Eisenkot, Naftali Bennett, Avigdor Liberman and Yair Golan, at fifty-two seats, and Netanyahu's bloc at fifty. Channel thirteen had fifty-three to fifty. Kan, the public broadcaster, had fifty-three to fifty. The Arab parties, the Joint List, an alliance of Arab-led parties, plus Ra'am, the Islamist Arab party led by Mansour Abbas, were projected at seventeen or eighteen seats together, a big jump from the pre-election polls.

But as the night went on, the picture shifted toward Netanyahu. Kan's updated exit poll had the two blocs tied at fifty-one each, with Likud rising to twenty-seven seats. And Channel fourteen, the right-leaning channel, was the outlier, giving Netanyahu's bloc sixty, just one short of a majority. Every poll agreed on one thing: Likud, not Eisenkot's Yashar, is the largest single party, with somewhere between twenty-four and thirty seats. Before the vote, almost every poll had Yashar in first place.

Then the real count started, and early on it looked very good for Netanyahu. With about a quarter of ballot boxes counted, at four this morning, the partial results gave the pro-Netanyahu bloc sixty-three seats and the anti-Netanyahu bloc forty-nine. But less than an hour later, with about a third counted, that had narrowed to fifty-nine against forty-six, and it's expected to keep narrowing. Two things to watch. First, the threshold: in the partial count, Ra'am was hovering just below three point two five percent, the minimum a party needs to enter the Knesset, even though exit polls gave it seven seats. If a party falls under that line, its votes are wasted, and the math changes. Second, more than six hundred fifty thousand double-envelope ballots, votes cast outside your home polling station, mostly by soldiers and people displaced by the war, are counted separately and could take until Friday. Official results are due November fourth.

Now the speeches. At Likud headquarters in Jerusalem, Netanyahu said, quote, "Two things have become clear. First, unequivocally, Likud is the largest party in Israel." He mocked the pre-election polls that had Likud at eighteen or nineteen seats. He claimed his bloc won "a clear, decisive majority among the Zionist public," which is his way of saying: if you don't count the Arab parties, we won. And he ruled out any government that relies on Arab parties, which he called "anti-Zionist," saying, "We need a right-wing, national, strong government." He stopped short of declaring victory, saying "we are still waiting for the true results."

Eisenkot told a very different story. At his rally in Tel Aviv he said, "The public voted for change," argued that a worthy leader would accept the result and retire, and opposed going to yet another election. He then sent a sharp message to the heads of the security services, reminding them that Netanyahu runs a caretaker government, a temporary government with no fresh mandate, and said directly: "Netanyahu, don't you dare use Israel's security for your political purposes." That's a reference to the reports this week about Netanyahu pushing for a strike on Iran before the vote. Bennett said his party would do everything, "without ego," to form a Zionist government of "those who serve," meaning people who serve in the army.

So who decides? Here's how it works. Once official results are in, President Isaac Herzog consults every party and picks the lawmaker with the best chance of building a coalition, a majority of sixty-one. He has until November eleventh, and it doesn't have to be the leader of the biggest party. The kingmaker, the small player who can decide who wins, may be Mansour Abbas. He told Channel twelve that, quote, "Everyone is speaking with us, not just from the 'change bloc,'" meaning people from Netanyahu's side have reached out too. He's open to joining an Eisenkot government but admitted "currently there aren't numbers for a government." The problem: several opposition parties have ruled out relying on Arab parties. Why it matters: until someone builds a coalition, Netanyahu stays caretaker prime minister, with all of his powers over war and peace. And if nobody can, Israel could head to yet another election, after five in under four years between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-two.

That brings us to Iran. In the same speech, Netanyahu said Israel faces "a historic, one-time opportunity, along with our friend President Trump, to remove the Iranian threat once and for all." He added, "Iran is weaker than ever, and Israel is stronger than ever." And overnight, the Wall Street Journal reported that the US has told several Gulf Arab countries to prepare for a short but intensive military campaign against Iran after the US midterm elections next Tuesday. According to the report, the US is moving a Patriot air defense battery from Texas and pulling troops away from bases exposed to Iranian fire. The White House declined to comment on "sensitive military plans that may or may not exist," and said Trump "keeps all options on the table."

Meanwhile, the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea lane where about a fifth of the world's oil normally passes, is still mostly shut. Iran's navy said on Tuesday that an oil tanker caught fire after hitting naval mines east of the strait, and claimed the ship had switched off its tracking system. Iran didn't name the ship, and nobody has independently confirmed it. Quick background: the war began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February, the two sides signed a memorandum of understanding, an early non-binding agreement, in June, and the talks then collapsed over shipping through Hormuz. Iran wants the US naval blockade of its ports lifted; the US wants Iran to cut uranium enrichment. Brent crude was around one hundred fourteen to one hundred fifteen dollars a barrel on Tuesday, up about seventy-five percent from a year ago.

And next door in the Gulf, the Houthis, the Iran-backed rebels in Yemen, claimed they hit Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery, one of the kingdom's biggest oil export hubs, with a ballistic missile. Saudi Arabia hasn't commented, and the claim isn't confirmed.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the election is six days away and turnout keeps climbing. After four days of early voting, the city's Board of Elections counted almost two hundred ninety-five thousand voters citywide. Brooklyn leads with about eighty-six thousand, then Manhattan with about eighty-two thousand. Through the first three days, that was roughly seventy-six percent ahead of the twenty twenty-two midterms. Early voting runs through Sunday, and Election Day is Tuesday, November third, from six in the morning to nine at night.

In the governor's race, both candidates made their closing arguments on Tuesday. Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive, held a news conference on the Upper East Side under a congestion pricing sign, the toll for driving into Manhattan below Sixtieth Street, and promised to repeal it, saying "there's no congestion relief here." Governor Hochul, a Democrat, spoke at a hotel workers' union rally and said, "To me, fighting for jobs is everything." She also launched three new TV ads tying Blakeman to Trump, each ending with the line that he'll "always put Trump ahead of you." Trump has endorsed Blakeman. The last big statewide polls this month had Hochul ahead by twelve to fourteen points.

Now a story that's been making New Yorkers angry. A Manhattan grand jury, a group of citizens who decide whether there's enough evidence for a trial, indicted, meaning formally charged, former NYPD detective Victor Romero with second-degree murder. That's an upgrade from the manslaughter charge we told you about on Sunday. Prosecutors say Romero and Koran Irvine, a forty-six-year-old Con Edison electrician driving home from work, nearly collided changing lanes onto the George Washington Bridge. Video allegedly shows Romero tailgating Irvine's car, pulling alongside, and firing through the window. Irvine died. Romero had posted one million dollars bail and is still free, which critics, including Irvine's widow, call special treatment. About one hundred Con Ed workers rallied outside court. His widow, Patricia Irvine, said: "It was murder, and that's it."

Some good news for basketball fans: the Knicks finally won. After an oh-and-three start, the defending champions beat the previously unbeaten Detroit Pistons one hundred eleven to ninety-seven at the Garden. Karl-Anthony Towns had twenty-seven points and twenty rebounds. The win spared them from becoming only the second defending champion ever to start oh-and-four. They're in Chicago tonight.

And get ready for a huge weekend. Saturday is Halloween and the fifty-third Village Halloween Parade, up Sixth Avenue from Canal Street to Fifteenth, starting at seven. The theme is "Body and Soul," and it opens with giant fire-horse puppets. Then Sunday is the last day of early voting and the fiftieth five-borough Marathon. And clocks fall back that night.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, starting with a story that connects New York and Israel. Israel has revoked the travel permits of Brad Lander, the former New York City comptroller who's expected to win a congressional seat next week, along with several American rabbis. These permits are called ETAs, electronic travel authorizations, the online approval visitors need before flying to Israel. They're normally approved in hours for a small fee. Last week, as we reported, Lander and dozens of American Jews joined Palestinians for the olive harvest in the West Bank and were briefly detained by Israeli troops. Now, according to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, more than half of the roughly sixty Jews detained have had their ETAs revoked, with emails giving only "a change in circumstances in your case" as the reason. Rabbi Jill Jacobs, CEO of T'ruah, the rabbinic human rights group that organized the trip, said, "I hope there will be a government that actually cares about its relationship with diaspora Jews." Lander called the move "petty." Israel's immigration authority didn't respond. Why it matters: it's very unusual for Israel to restrict diaspora Jews, and it raises the question of whether visiting is starting to depend on your politics.

Speaking of politics, Jews abroad watched yesterday's vote closely. In New York, Congressman Jerry Nadler told the Forward, "My hope is that Bibi gets shellacked." Governor Hochul told Politico that defending Israel would get "a lot easier" if Netanyahu loses. And the Jerusalem Post spoke to Jewish leaders around the world. Robert Hercz, who heads B'nai B'rith in Norway, said he's "terrified, not only for the future of Israel, but also for how the election will affect Jewry worldwide."

Now a beautiful story from Baghdad. Iraq's first Jewish heritage museum opened last week inside the old Shamash School, a Jewish high school founded in nineteen twenty-eight that had more than nine hundred students at its peak. It shows religious objects, family photos, school certificates and a portrait of the founder, and more than a thousand people visited on opening day, according to Israel's Foreign Ministry. The restoration cost about one hundred fifty thousand dollars, paid for by Iraqi Jews abroad. Last year the site was basically a garbage dump. Some background: Jews lived in Iraq for more than two thousand five hundred years, going back to the Babylonian exile. In the early twentieth century they were almost a quarter of Baghdad's population. Then came the Farhud, a deadly pogrom against Baghdad's Jews in nineteen forty-one, and the mass airlifts to Israel in nineteen fifty and fifty-one. Today, fewer than ten Jews may remain.

And one more homecoming. Two Jewish treasures stolen on Kristallnacht, the Nazi night of attacks on Jews and synagogues in November nineteen thirty-eight, have been returned to Berlin by a synagogue in Arizona. One is a parochet, the curtain that hangs in front of the Torah ark, which an expert dated to the sixteenth century. The other is a Torah wimple, a cloth binder probably made for a baby's brit milah, the circumcision ceremony. After the war, a Holocaust survivor named Moishe Saksenhaus got them from a German man who demanded Crisco, coffee, flour, cigarettes and cigars, and threatened to destroy them otherwise. The family later donated them to Temple Chai in Phoenix. Rabbi Bonnie Koppell handed them over last week, saying, "This time, they are going home." The son, Sol Saksenhaus, said they're being given back "as a sign of love."

## Tech, startups & AI

Next, tech, and let's start with Israeli tech, because it's been a busy week for deals. Israeli chip startup NeuReality is being bought by a US company called Unconventional AI. NeuReality, founded in twenty nineteen by veterans of Intel, Mellanox and Habana Labs, builds infrastructure for AI inference, the stage where an AI model actually answers questions rather than being trained. The buyer is barely a year old but already a unicorn: it came out of stealth last December with a four hundred seventy-five million dollar seed round at a four point five billion dollar valuation, led by Andreessen Horowitz and Lightspeed. Unconventional is building analog AI chips, chips that compute with continuous electrical signals rather than ones and zeros, and is chasing what it calls a thousand-times improvement in efficiency. That's a goal, not a result yet. The price wasn't disclosed; Globes cites market estimates of one hundred fifty to two hundred million dollars, more than investors put in but less than they hoped. About forty Israeli employees in Caesarea and Tel Aviv will join, but some sales, marketing and finance staff will be let go, which is common when a buyer mostly wants the engineers.

Another Israeli company in the spotlight: monday.com, the Tel Aviv work-management software company. Its stock is up eighty-one percent from its April low, and jumped almost eighteen percent last week alone. Why? Two things investors like right now: it's cutting about twenty percent of staff, around six hundred twenty people, to remove management layers, and it just launched voice AI agents that, according to Globes, can hold conversations and even close sales deals. Still, its market value is about four and a half billion dollars, far below more than nineteen billion at its twenty twenty-one peak. Results come November ninth. For you, the lesson is clear: the market is rewarding companies that run leaner with AI, including in go-to-market teams.

Now the giants. Microsoft has offered voluntary buyouts, packages that pay people to leave, to about sixty thousand employees, more than a quarter of its workforce, in the US, Canada, the UK and India. It covers teams like Windows, Xbox and Copilot. Notably, sales and marketing roles are excluded. Microsoft says it isn't announcing sixty thousand layoffs, and hasn't said how many it expects to accept. Its chief people officer, Amy Coleman, said it gives employees "a new choice to make that transition now with support." The backdrop: Microsoft expects to spend about one hundred seventy-five billion dollars this year on capex, capital spending on things like data centers, mostly for AI. Microsoft and Alphabet, Google's parent, report earnings tonight.

OpenAI launched a new model on Monday called GPT six point one Terra, built for what's called computer use: an AI that operates software the way a person does, filling in forms, clicking through screens, editing video. OpenAI says it's more than three times faster than its top models and half the price of its previous mid-tier model, at one dollar per million input tokens, the small chunks of text AI models are billed by. OpenAI still recommends its bigger models for the hardest coding work. Why it matters to you: the boring parts of marketing operations, updating the CRM, moving data between tools, just got much cheaper to automate.

## Cybersecurity & GTM

Now cybersecurity and go-to-market, and a story that has the whole industry talking. A startup called Cathedral, run by former members of DOGE, Elon Musk's government cost-cutting team, says its AI agents broke into Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, in under twelve hours. This was a sanctioned test: Anthropic invites outside firms to try to hack it. Cathedral says it got in through flaws in ordinary third-party business software Anthropic uses, and reached meeting transcripts, salary information and Slack messages. Anthropic confirmed access to "a limited set" of systems and says it closed the gaps. The "twelve hours" figure is Cathedral's own claim. Cathedral raised one hundred sixty million dollars in July at a one point four billion dollar valuation, and focuses on offensive security, meaning attacking systems to find weak spots, for the military. Its report warned that "weaknesses in ordinary enterprise software can compromise the infrastructure of even the most advanced organizations." An unnamed official told the Washington Post, "If a bunch of kids who have just started a company can do this, then potentially, the Chinese aren't far behind." For a security marketer, this is the story you'll hear in every pitch for months: your risk is the SaaS tools you rely on, not just your own code.

Next, a story where cybersecurity and marketing collide, with an Israeli company at the center. Reuters reports that an Israeli ad-tech company, ad-tech being the software that buys and places online ads, called Discover-Tech, used ad auctions to track people using Starlink, Musk's satellite internet. Some background: every time you open a website or app, an instant auction called real-time bidding decides which ad you see, and bidders get data about your device and location. Researchers say Discover-Tech was the number one bidder on Starlink traffic in more than one hundred countries. Trend Micro said it looked like "targeting and tracking infrastructure rather than a commercial advertising operation." Google and Comcast have removed the company from their ad platforms, and it didn't respond to Reuters. Why it matters: the same ad data marketers rely on can be used for surveillance, so expect more regulation of programmatic advertising, the automated buying of ads.

One quick security item. Genesys, a big contact-center software company from California, says hackers got into its internal systems, including its CRM, and likely stole customer names, phone numbers, job titles and business emails. The marketer's angle: a stolen CRM is a ready-made list for spear-phishing, targeted fake emails, aimed at that vendor's customers.

Now two marketing stories. First, Anthropic promised in its Super Bowl ad that Claude would stay ad-free, but its IPO filing, the S-1, shows it's buying a lot of ads. According to Digiday, Anthropic's ad spend jumped from thirty-three million dollars in twenty twenty-four to one hundred forty-seven million in twenty twenty-five, more than Duolingo's entire sales and marketing budget. Nate Elliott of eMarketer said the filing makes clear "they consider their brand one of their greatest assets." The lesson: even the hottest B2B company in the world is investing heavily in brand and launch PR.

And second, publishers are now selling GEO, generative engine optimization, the new version of SEO aimed at getting your brand cited by AI chatbots. The Daily Mail is running a dozen or so tests with brands and plans a formal product early next year. In one test, a personal-finance brand's visibility across target questions went from about fifty to nearly seventy-five percent. One brand found Claude sent only about zero point one percent of its referrals, but about eleven percent of its conversions. The Mail's SEO director, Carly Steven, said, "There's a lot of awareness that there is a gap in the attribution model." Put plainly: AI search traffic is small, but it converts, and nobody is measuring it well yet. That's a skill worth having on your resume.

## Business story

Now our business story, and it's about a bag you probably remember from middle school. In nineteen eighty-two, two neighbors in Fort Wayne, Indiana, Barbara Bradley Baekgaard and Patricia Miller, were stuck on a layover at the Atlanta airport. They noticed that almost everyone's luggage was black or beige, and boring. They put together five hundred dollars, bought fabric at a local store, and sewed the first quilted duffel on a ping-pong table in Baekgaard's basement. Baekgaard later said, "we went back and started the company the next day." They named it after her mother: Vera Bradley.

Baekgaard's daughters took the bags to college, and a sorority house at Michigan State became the first test market. "It just grew from there," she said. The quilted floral print became a staple of the nineties and two thousands. Sales passed five hundred forty million dollars around twenty twenty-one.

Then it lost its way. In twenty twenty-four, the company launched a plan called Project Restoration, a full modernization with new shapes, new materials, a new logo and new store designs. It flopped. Sales didn't move, and longtime fans felt the brand had abandoned the florals they loved. Revenue slid to around three hundred seventy million dollars. In March, a new CEO, Ian Bickley, took over, and he was blunt: "We lost track of what made Vera Bradley special and unique and what customers loved about us."

So here's the twist. The new plan, called Project Sunshine, went the other way: back to the florals, plus shoulder bags and bag charms, and aimed squarely at Gen Z on TikTok and Instagram. In March, a limited run of fifty reissued original handbags sold out in under three minutes. The brand has now sold more than twenty-four thousand items on TikTok Shop, the shopping feature built into TikTok. This summer it posted its first quarterly revenue growth in nearly five years, then a second growth quarter, with comparable sales, sales from existing channels, up nine percent in its direct business.

It's still fragile: the forecast for this year is two hundred fifty-five to two hundred seventy million dollars, far below the peak. But the marketing lesson is a good one for you: when nostalgia is in, don't rebrand away the thing people loved. Lean into it, and meet the new generation where it shops.

## Art & design

Now art and design. In London, the Design Museum has opened "Other Worlds," the first European retrospective, a show covering a whole career, of Es Devlin, the British artist and stage designer behind the London twenty twelve Olympics closing ceremony and giant concert stages. It includes "An Infinite Library," an eight-meter rotating sculpture of more than two thousand books. Her advice is something you could put on your agency's wall: big projects "start in really simple gestures." It runs until April.

And a reality check from the business side. Pace, one of the world's biggest galleries, filed its UK accounts, and turnover there fell to about sixty-eight million dollars, from about one hundred five million the year before, sliding from a profit into a loss. Why it matters to you: the mega-galleries are shrinking, which leaves room for smaller, more flexible players, like an agency.

## Wellness

Your wellness idea today is about your coffee. A new study from Tufts University looked at about thirty thousand American adults and found that people drink about the same amount of coffee as before, around thirteen ounces a day, but the calories in it rose almost forty percent, from about forty-three to sixty a day. The main reason isn't sugar, it's non-dairy creamer, whose use more than doubled. Researcher Yongyi Pan said, "Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense." The practical tip: measure your creamer instead of free-pouring, check the saturated fat on the label, or switch to a splash of milk. The data goes up to twenty eighteen and is self-reported, so treat it as a nudge, not a rule.
