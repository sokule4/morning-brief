Good morning Sophie, and welcome to your Morning Brief. It's Thursday, October eighth. Here are the three big ones. A brand new poll says Israel's election is a dead heat, with neither side able to form a government. The flydubai scare could cost Israelis their easy entry to the United States. And back home, Mayor Mamdani's October seventh statement has managed to anger people on every side. Plus a thirty-billion-dollar OpenAI round, a shipping giant stuck in limbo, and Jeff Koons cashing in his own Picasso. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start with the election, now just nineteen days away. A new Channel 13 poll, published overnight, has Prime Minister Netanyahu's bloc, meaning his group of allied parties, at fifty-five seats. The Zionist opposition bloc gets fifty. You need sixty-one seats in the Knesset, Israel's parliament, to form a government, so as of today, nobody gets there.

The headline inside the poll: Likud and Yashar, the party led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot, are tied at twenty seats each. That's remarkable, because Likud has been the biggest party for years. Bennett's alliance gets twelve, the Democrats ten, Yisrael Beytenu eight. On Netanyahu's side, Ben Gvir's Otzma Yehudit gets eight, United Torah Judaism eight, Shas seven. The two Arab parties, the Joint List and Ra'am, get fifteen between them. And two parties fall below the electoral threshold, the minimum share of votes needed to get any seats, which in Israel is three point two five percent. That includes Blue and White, Benny Gantz's party, once one of the biggest names in Israeli politics.

Here's why the Arab parties matter so much. Both main blocs have ruled out sitting with them. So if the math stays like this, the Arab parties are the kingmakers that neither side wants to call. The Democrats are the exception: they've said they'd include Ra'am in a coalition, meaning the group of parties that together form a government.

On the campaign trail, Eisenkot has challenged Netanyahu to a live televised debate, saying voters deserve answers "free from spin and lies." He also accuses Netanyahu of blocking a state commission of inquiry into October seventh. Likud's answer is to paint Eisenkot as the one hiding from the media. Israel hasn't had a real head-to-head debate like this in a very long time, so don't hold your breath. And a separate poll found that October seventh itself is a central factor for most voters' decisions this time.

Next, the flydubai story keeps growing. Quick reminder: on September thirtieth, a flydubai pilot allegedly attacked his co-pilot and tried to take over a flight to Tel Aviv, before passengers and off-duty pilots took control. This week, the fallout turned political, and personal for a lot of Israelis. According to Israeli TV and Army Radio, Israel could lose its place in the US Visa Waiver Program, the deal that lets Israelis visit America without a visa. Israel only joined in 2023, and it was a huge deal. A US delegation is expected in Israel within days to check whether Israel still meets the security conditions. It went to the UAE first.

What's driving that worry? A string of revelations. A leaked Knesset committee meeting showed about one thousand two hundred and forty aircrew from countries hostile to Israel entered the country in the past year. Reports say that in 2025, the Transportation Ministry turned down an offer from the Shin Bet, Israel's internal security service, to place an officer inside the ministry. And there was money for ten security positions this year, and none of them have been filled. Police are now looking at two aviation security officials, including the man who ran aviation security for nineteen years and reportedly did paid consulting for flydubai and Emirates.

Flydubai has asked to restart flights to Israel. Transportation Minister Miri Regev reportedly said no. New draft rules would require foreign crews to file their details forty-eight hours before a flight, with extra screening for crews from certain countries. Meanwhile, the UAE pulled landing permits for Israeli airlines, which cancelled several flights bringing Israelis home. More than seven thousand have made it back, and the ministry expects everyone home by today. One small piece of good news in aviation: British Airways plans to resume Tel Aviv flights on October twenty-fifth.

Now, the showdown with Britain. Israel gave Britain thirty days to shut its consulate in East Jerusalem, a consulate being a mini embassy, and that deadline runs out today. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar issued the order in retaliation for British sanctions on settlements. Britain's national security adviser held talks with Israeli officials, but there was no deal as of the latest reports. Britain has reportedly threatened to expel up to twenty-seven Israeli diplomats if the consulate closes. Israel has also removed Britain from the US-led coordination center for Gaza, and barred eleven British members of parliament, including Jeremy Corbyn. A former British consul general put it bluntly: "It's not the act of a friend or ally."

On Iran, the war is now in its eighth month, and there's a shift in tone from Washington. Vice President JD Vance told Reuters that to end the war, Iran must make a "meaningful" cut in its uranium enrichment, enrichment being the process that turns uranium into nuclear fuel, or, at high levels, a bomb. Experts noticed that's softer than the old demand of zero enrichment. Secretary of State Marco Rubio still says Iran "cannot have a nuclear weapon, simple, period, end of story," and claims Iran has lost control of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that a fifth of the world's oil passes through. Iran's president calls talks "futile." Meanwhile, ships in the Gulf keep getting hit: a tanker in the strait was struck by a projectile, injuring twelve crew members.

And this got a lot of attention in America. At a rally in Nebraska, President Trump called the war "a small price to pay," and then said: "They can take out a city, let them take out Los Angeles. Let them take out San Diego." The White House says he meant America is fighting so that doesn't happen. California's governor, Gavin Newsom, called it "deranged and dangerous," and the mayor of San Diego answered: "We are not expendable." With the midterms, the US congressional elections halfway through a president's term, about four weeks away, expect to hear that clip a lot.

In Yemen, a follow-up to yesterday. The government has launched what it calls "Dawn of Yemen," an offensive aiming to retake all of the territory held by the Houthis, the Iran-backed group that controls much of Yemen, including the capital Sanaa. The government claims more than eighteen hundred Houthi fighters have been killed or captured. The Saudi-led coalition says it hit eighty-two Houthi targets on Wednesday alone. And the official toll from Houthi attacks on two Saudi airports is now three dead. The UN says more than two hundred thousand people have been displaced.

Russia launched one of its largest attacks of the war on Ukraine overnight into Wednesday. President Zelensky says about seventy missiles plus drones were fired, with energy infrastructure a key target. In the city of Pryluky, a missile hit a five-story apartment building, killing at least fourteen people, including four children. At least twenty people were killed across the country.

And the Nobels. The Chemistry prize went to Henri Kagan of France, who is ninety-five, and Kenso Soai of Japan, for work on how to make just one mirror-image version of a molecule, which matters a lot for medicines. Soai was out shopping when he got the news, and said: "This is the most exciting day in my life." Today at two p.m. Israel time, we get the Literature prize, and tomorrow, Peace.

## New York

Next, back home in New York.

October seventh in the city turned into a fight over the mayor. Mayor Mamdani posted a statement mourning the Israelis killed and the two hundred and fifty-one hostages taken, saying "we grieve alongside our many friends and neighbors who still carry that unthinkable loss." But most of the statement focused on Gaza, using the words "genocide," "apartheid" and "war crimes," and criticizing US weapons shipments to Israel. Governor Hochul, by contrast, lowered state flags and lit landmarks in yellow.

The reaction was fierce. The ADL's Jonathan Greenblatt pointed out that the statement never once used the word "Jews," and said the city deserves a mayor who can honor the dead without exploiting them. Israel's consul general in New York, Ofir Akunis, called it "a disgrace, shame, and insult." Israel's ambassador to the US, Yechiel Leiter, called Mamdani "a defender of Palestinian terrorism." Leiter said that while his own son was on life support after a car-ramming attack in the West Bank.

And here's the twist. Mamdani also got hit from the left. That evening, anti-Zionist protesters disrupted an October seventh mourning event held by a group called Israelis for Peace, which Mamdani attended without speaking. They chanted "Zohran your hands are red." Earlier, hundreds marched near City Hall, angry that he kept Jessica Tisch, who is Jewish, as police commissioner. So he's managed to upset both sides at once.

Two more pieces of context. Council Speaker Julie Menin and Comptroller Mark Levine proposed a permanent October seventh memorial in a Brooklyn garden. And NYPD numbers show that of the hate crimes reported in the first nine months of this year, more than half targeted Jews.

In sports, a rough night for New York. The Yankees were swept out of the playoffs by the Tampa Bay Rays, three games to none, losing Game Three four to three in the Bronx. The heartbreaker: in the sixth inning, Anthony Volpe hit what looked like a game-tying two-run homer, but it was overturned because a fan reached over the wall. It was ruled a double and only one run scored.

The Liberty are also in trouble. They lost to the Atlanta Dream in overtime, a hundred and one to ninety-eight, even with Breanna Stewart scoring thirty-five points. They're down two games to none in a best-of-five series. Game Three is Friday night at nine thirty New York time. They have to win.

A sad and scary story from the Bronx. Early Tuesday morning in Allerton, a twenty-three-year-old mother, Nathaly Ramirez, was pushed from a fifteenth-floor balcony along with her eight-month-old daughter. Nathaly died; reports say she died shielding her baby, who survived and is now stable. Police have named her ex-boyfriend, thirty-one-year-old Angel Carrasco, as wanted, and call him armed and dangerous.

And some lighter city news. Pedestrian deaths in the first nine months of the year hit a record low, according to the Department of Transportation. The city is running its largest-ever set of car-free "Trick-or-Streets" Halloween events through October. And "Dirty Dancing" is becoming a Broadway musical. Nobody puts Baby in a corner, not even on Broadway.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, and a few stories from the anniversary that are worth your attention.

In Israel, Channel 12 aired the final recordings of Amit Mann, a twenty-two-year-old paramedic who was killed at the dental clinic in Kibbutz Be'eri, where she was treating the wounded. Her sister only found the recordings by chance. Magen David Adom, Israel's ambulance service, had seven of her calls; the family knew about four. In her last message to her sisters, Amit said: "They're here, in the clinic. I don't think I'll make it out of here. I love you." Her recordings even helped another widow figure out when her husband died. Now the family is pushing for one government body to collect every piece of October seventh material, sort it by victim, and contact each family. As her sister said: "It belongs to the families." A new law creating an October seventh Memory Authority takes effect in January.

Around the world, the day looked very different place to place. In Sydney, hundreds gathered at Bondi at six twenty-nine in the morning, the exact minute the attack began in 2023. They also remembered the fifteen people killed in the Bondi Hanukkah shooting last December. In New York, there was a quiet commemoration at the JCC in Manhattan, where an eighty-three-year-old woman told a reporter simply, "We should never forget." A few blocks away, hundreds rallied at City Hall chanting "Globalize the intifada."

In culture, a Jewish theater company in Chicago called Arts Judaica cancelled a planned revival of "Women's Minyan," a play by the Israeli novelist Naomi Ragen, who was born in New York. The play is about an Orthodox woman leaving an abusive marriage, and has nothing to do with politics. The artistic director said she was "bombarded" by actors, directors and designers who didn't want to work with a "pro Zionist" writer. Ragen's response: "Are we now back to McCarthyism, where you have to check the political credentials of an artist?" McCarthyism refers to the anti-communist blacklists of 1950s America. This comes a few weeks after an ADL report claiming Jewish and Israeli artists are being systematically shut out. As someone building an art agency, this is a trend worth tracking.

In Pennsylvania, a religion professor at Haverford College, Guangtian Ha, was arrested on federal cyberstalking charges. Prosecutors say he sent about fifty antisemitic messages to a Jewish person from at least four accounts on X, including posting a photo of the victim's mailbox and naming their son. He's on leave and banned from campus, and the college president said she was "heartbroken and furious."

And one more thing worth knowing. A study of more than seven hundred American Jews found that feeling dismissed, especially by universities and community groups, was linked to higher anxiety and depression. Almost half say they now wear visibly Jewish items more often, not less. The researchers' title says it all: "We are not OK." If that's you or someone you love, you're in good company, and it's worth talking about.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI.

The biggest number of the week: OpenAI is reportedly in talks to raise thirty billion dollars at a valuation, what investors say the whole company is worth, of about one point four trillion dollars. That's nearly double what it was worth in February. The money would come from funds in the UAE and from BlackRock, the giant investment firm. OpenAI's annual revenue is reportedly approaching seventy billion dollars. Sam Altman says there won't be an IPO, an initial public offering, meaning a listing on the stock market, this year. The deal isn't done yet. Also from OpenAI this week: it says an internal model found answers to hundreds of unsolved math problems, and posted the proofs publicly.

Now to Israel, and a quote you'll want to remember for interviews. Nikesh Arora, the CEO of Palo Alto Networks, says about forty companies are racing to secure AI agents, AI tools that take actions on their own, and at least twenty of them are Israeli. Seventeen of those Israeli companies have raised about nine hundred million dollars combined, names like Zenity, Noma, Onyx and Reco. Arora's line: "I have cash. I have market cap. So I can go buy them." He also admitted he probably won't pick right nine times out of ten, "but somebody will." Translation: this is Israel's next big cyber category, and a lot of these companies will be acquired.

Speaking of acquisitions, a crazy-fast one. Nebius, the AI cloud company, bought a Tel Aviv startup called Inferize, just ten months old, for a reported one hundred to one hundred and thirty million dollars. Inferize had seventeen employees and hadn't even launched commercially. Its technology cuts what's called "cold starts," the time an AI chip sits idle while a model loads. As the CEO put it, keeping spare chips running "is the price of being ready for demand." Inferize solves that.

The monthly numbers back this up. Israeli startups raised two point seven seven billion dollars in September across twenty-eight rounds. Five mega-rounds, rounds of a hundred million dollars or more, made up almost seventy percent of it. The leaders: Wonderful, an AI customer service company, raised five hundred and fifty million at a five billion dollar valuation. Cyera and Island raised four hundred million each, and Upwind three hundred million. HR company HiBob raised one hundred and sixty-six million, led by Salesforce. And consumer security app Guardio raised forty million at more than a billion-dollar valuation, with over a million paying customers.

In AI land: Anthropic, the company behind Claude, launched Claude inside Google Workspace, as a sidebar in Docs, Sheets and Slides. Meta and a startup called Sierra proposed a standard so businesses can identify AI agents acting for customers, backed by Shopify, Stripe and Walmart.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM, meaning go-to-market, how companies sell and market.

The big warning this week is being called FortiBleed. The FBI and the Secret Service say hackers broke into more than eighty-six thousand firewalls made by Fortinet, the boxes companies use to guard their networks, across a hundred and ninety-four countries. They stole login details, sorted the victims by company revenue, and sold the access to ransomware gangs. Ransomware is when hackers lock your files and demand payment. At least twelve organizations have already been hit. The funny part: the hackers exposed their own server, and that's how the whole operation was uncovered.

From South Korea, a first. President Lee Jae Myung says there are signs that AI agents broke into at least seven financial institutions, including some of the country's biggest banks, exposing the data of at least sixty-eight thousand people. Investigators suspect a Chinese tool. The scary line from the president: hacking with AI is now possible "with ease even without specialized skills." It's being described as the first known case of AI agents hacking a country's financial sector, and it's exactly why those Israeli agent-security startups are raising so much money.

Next, a story that sounds like a movie plot. Zohar Pinhasi, the CEO of a Florida company called MonsterCloud, has been charged in New York. MonsterCloud advertised that it could unlock ransomware victims' files without paying the criminals. Prosecutors say it was secretly just paying the hackers. In one case, it paid about eight thousand dollars in ransom and billed the victim about a hundred and fifty thousand. Overall, prosecutors say it paid more than eight million dollars in ransoms and billed over nineteen million. He's pleaded not guilty. The US attorney said he "re-victimized his clients."

On the money side: Kevin Mandia, the founder of Mandiant, which Google bought, raised two hundred and fifty-five million dollars for his new company Armadin at a valuation of more than two point five billion, just seven months after launching. Armadin uses swarms of AI agents to attack companies the way skilled hackers would, so they can fix the holes first. In one test, twenty-six thousand agents ran about seventeen million attack actions.

Now the GTM story you should know about. HubSpot, the marketing and sales software company that basically every GTM team touches, is cutting about six hundred and sixty jobs, roughly seven percent of staff. It's flattening management and reorganizing teams around customer outcomes, instead of around its product lines. CEO Yamini Rangan says the cuts are "not driven by AI-related efficiencies." Fair enough, but the takeaway for your job search is that sales and marketing orgs everywhere are being reshaped. Roles tied directly to revenue and customer results are the ones companies protect. When you interview, lead with the numbers you moved.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Zim, Israel's eighty-one-year-old shipping giant, which is suddenly making a fortune while its own sale falls apart.

Zim was founded in 1945, before the state even existed, and it's Israel's largest shipping company. It works in more than ninety countries with over thirty-three thousand customers. And it has a special twist: the Israeli government holds what's called a golden share, a special stake that gives it veto rights. It forces Zim to always keep a certain number of Israeli-owned ships available for emergencies, like wartime. When you're a small country surrounded by hostile neighbors, who controls your ships really matters.

In February, the German shipping giant Hapag-Lloyd agreed to buy Zim for thirty-five dollars a share in cash, about four point two billion dollars. The plan was to carve out a smaller "New Zim" of twelve to sixteen ships, owned by the Israeli investment fund FIMI, which would keep the golden share. Zim's shareholders approved it in April. Done deal, right?

Not quite. Israel's defense establishment pushed back, saying the plan didn't protect national security, and that most of the profitable Asia-to-America routes would go to the Germans. Then people noticed who owns big pieces of Hapag-Lloyd: Qatar's sovereign wealth fund, meaning a government investment fund, owns more than twelve percent, and Saudi Arabia's owns about ten percent. At the end of September, the government authority reviewing the deal sent it back to the drawing board.

So Hapag-Lloyd's CEO flew in with a sweeter offer. A new weekly direct route from the Far East, keeping ship management in Israel, investing in Israeli sailors, and lowering the cap on how much any shareholder can own without approval. He says it "will prevent any foreign interference" in Israel's sensitive cargo. Zim's workers aren't convinced; their union chief says it doesn't add a single Israeli ship, and accuses Hapag of stalling until after the election.

And then, on Wednesday, the twist. Zim sharply raised its profit forecast for the year. Its expected operating profit jumped by roughly seventy percent at the midpoint. The stock popped overnight, but it's still trading around twenty-nine dollars, almost twenty percent below the thirty-five dollar offer. That gap is the market saying: we don't think this deal is happening.

The lesson: in Israel, business is never just business. A deal can make perfect financial sense and still die on national security. And sometimes the company you're trying to sell gets more valuable precisely because the world is so unstable.

## Art and design

Next, art and design.

Jeff Koons, the artist behind those giant shiny balloon dogs, is selling about a hundred million dollars of his own art collection at Christie's in New York in November. The star is Picasso's "The Kiss," from 1969, estimated at about forty million dollars. Koons bought it in 2008 for about seventeen million. There's also a Manet of a woman bathing, and a sixteenth-century painting of a man wrestling a bull. These are the old masters, meaning major European painters from before about 1800, that inspired Koons's own "Gazing Ball" series. Together with the Van Gogh sale we told you about yesterday, New York's November auctions are shaping up to be huge.

A real-life art heist in France. Thieves stole a hundred-and-fifty-kilo bronze book cover designed by Salvador Dalí from an art center in the town of Issoire. It was made for a 1958 "Book of the Apocalypse," decorated with gold and precious stones, which its owner once called "the most expensive book in the world." The owner told reporters: "It is the work of a lifetime."

Here's one for the culture crossover file. RM, the leader of BTS, has an exhibition at the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art: a hundred and seventy-eight works covering about fifteen hundred years of Korean art, most of it from his own collection. About fifty thousand people have reserved tickets, and opening weekend sold out. He started collecting after a 2018 visit to the Art Institute of Chicago, when he realized he couldn't name a single Korean artist. His line: "I guess this exhibition is a pretty long introduction of myself." For an art agency, that's the dream: a celebrity collector bringing a whole new audience into a museum.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today. A new Japanese study found that a twenty-minute walk at your own pace gets your digestion moving almost immediately. Within a minute or two of finishing, gut activity rose sharply in every single participant. It was a small study, just twenty-one people, but the idea is easy: after lunch or dinner today, take a comfortable twenty-minute walk. Your stomach will thank you, and so will your head.
