## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Tuesday, November third, and this is your Morning Brief. Avigdor Liberman has picked a side: he'll recommend Gadi Eisenkot for prime minister, with one big condition. The US carried out what it calls "defensive" strikes on an Iranian island, even as Iran offers to speed up a peace deal. And today is Election Day in America, with Democrats ahead in the final polls. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in Israel, where the last big question mark of the week just got answered. Avigdor Liberman leads Yisrael Beytenu, a right-wing secular party that's part of the anti-Netanyahu camp. For days he refused to say who he'd back. On Monday, he said he will recommend Gadi Eisenkot to President Herzog, and that he told Eisenkot so face to face. He also ruled out serving in any Netanyahu government, even in a rotation deal, where two leaders take turns as prime minister, with Liberman going first.

But here's his condition. Liberman says there will be no minority government that depends on Arab parties from the outside. "It won't happen," he said, predicting such a government would be short-lived. He accused the Arab parties of being backed by the Palestinian Authority, which he called "a link we cannot allow." And he didn't hide his frustration with Eisenkot either, calling his campaign "egotistical" and saying the result, forty-eight seats for the opposition's Jewish-led parties, was "far lower than what we expected."

Liberman also wants a different Knesset speaker, the lawmaker who runs the parliament and controls which bills come up for a vote. Right now it's Amir Ohana from Likud. Liberman backs Yair Lapid for the job, which would let the opposition move some laws even before there's a government.

So where does that leave the math? Bennett, Liberman and Yair Golan's Democrats are all now behind Eisenkot, so he's very likely to get the most recommendations when Herzog's consultations start tomorrow. That's what decides who gets the mandate, the first shot at forming a government. Governing is harder. Without the Arab parties, Eisenkot's camp is far from sixty-one. The Joint List's Yousef Jabareen called this "a historic moment" and said his party could support an Eisenkot government from the outside, but won't join it. The Times of Israel points out that if Ra'am voted for Eisenkot and the Joint List abstained, he'd have a majority. That is exactly the scenario Liberman says he won't allow. Meanwhile Netanyahu called cooperation with the Arab parties a "disaster," and his coalition whip, the lawmaker who keeps the government's votes in line, ruled out any rotation deal.

There was also an ugly moment. On Sunday night, a protester outside Eisenkot's home in Herzliya shouted "You murdered your son," about Gal Eisenkot, who was killed fighting in Gaza in December twenty twenty-three, and threatened his wife, Hannah. Eisenkot's party blamed Netanyahu's camp for what it called "rotten, unrestrained political leadership."

Now Iran, where the signals are mixed again. On Monday, US officials confirmed that America struck targets on Qeshm Island, which sits at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz. According to the Wall Street Journal, the island has a big underground missile base, a naval base and a major desalination plant, and these strikes were part of a series of operations the US had kept quiet. Officials called them "defensive," meant to weaken Iran's ability to threaten shipping. Iranian state media reported explosions on the island.

At the same time, Trump is talking peace. Asked if he's planning strikes after today's vote, he simply said, "No," and pointed to falling oil prices as a sign markets expect no new war. Iran, for its part, says it can speed up its peace plan. The foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baghaei, said the seven-step plan can now be done in two days instead of seven, and that "the ball is in the US court." The plan would reopen Hormuz and restart nuclear talks. In return, Iran wants the US to lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, cancel sanctions, guarantee no more strikes, and agree to a full regional ceasefire, especially in Lebanon. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said if those conditions are accepted, the strait opens, and if not, "Iran will not bow down." Qatar and Pakistan are mediating.

The sea is still dangerous, though. UKMTO, the British agency that tracks threats to ships, says a tanker was hit by two unidentified projectiles about seventy-two nautical miles east of Saudi Arabia's Ras al-Khafji. The crew is safe. And The Atlantic reports that Trump, whose advisers promised this war would be over before the midterms, is weighing whom to blame if it isn't, possibly Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth or the top general, Dan Caine. Remember, this war started on February twenty-eighth with joint US and Israeli strikes. Why it matters for us: a deal would mean calmer skies and cheaper fuel, while a new round of fighting would hit Israel first.

In Gaza, the ceasefire keeps fraying. The Palestinian news agency WAFA says Israeli strikes killed five people on Monday, in Khan Younis, central Gaza and Gaza City, and that hospitals received fifteen bodies in forty-eight hours. The Israeli army says it targets militants.

In case you missed it, Spain is mourning after its worst high-speed rail crash in thirteen years. Last Wednesday, two trains heading from Madrid to Seville collided near the town of Urda, killing sixty-nine people. Here's how it happened. A computer failure stopped six trains on the line. Then the control center let them move again with no speed limits, an order the transport minister, Óscar Puente, called "inexplicable." A Renfe train with nearly four hundred passengers slammed into the back of a stopped Iryo train. Puente says controllers watched one train approach the other for seventeen minutes without stopping it. It's the second deadly crash on that same line this year, after one in January killed forty-six. An independent commission is investigating.

And now America, where today is Election Day in the midterms. All four hundred thirty-five House seats are up, and Republicans hold a tiny two-eighteen to two-fourteen majority. In the Senate, thirty-five seats are up and Republicans lead fifty-three to forty-seven, so Democrats need to gain four.

The final New York Times and Siena polls look good for Democrats. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed leads Republican Mike Rogers by eleven. In Texas, James Talarico leads Ken Paxton fifty to forty-three. In Maine, Troy Jackson leads Senator Susan Collins fifty-one to forty-five. Democrats are also ahead in Ohio and Kansas, while Alaska and Iowa are within the margin of error, meaning too close to call. Democrats lead the generic ballot, the "which party do you want" question, by about nine points, and Trump's approval is around thirty-eight percent. Early voting set records in North Carolina, Texas, Georgia and South Carolina. One caveat: polls have underestimated Republicans before, and Speaker Mike Johnson says Republicans are less willing to answer polls.

Some side stories. Elon Musk spent a record four hundred seventy-five million dollars backing Republicans this cycle. Pope Leo, who's from Chicago, voted by mail in Illinois. And on Monday, Trump gave his final speech of the campaign at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, and spent much of it talking about the pool's troubled renovation. Results start late tonight in America, which means early Wednesday morning for us. New York's polls close at four a.m. Israel time.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where it's decision day. The headline race is for governor. Kathy Hochul, the Democrat, is running for a second full term against Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive, the elected head of the county. Most polls give Hochul a double-digit lead. Blakeman isn't buying it. "We're right there, we are neck and neck, we can win this thing if people get out to vote," he said, betting that Republicans will turn out today rather than early.

The closing arguments were all about money. Blakeman promises to cut utility costs in half and to end state income tax for couples earning up to one hundred thousand dollars. He also says that on January first he'd sign an order ending New York's status as a sanctuary state, a state that limits cooperation with federal immigration agents. And he keeps accusing Hochul of a "secret pact" with Mayor Zohran Mamdani to raise taxes. Hochul rejects that. She points to more housing and a nearly four billion dollar investment in public safety, and frames the race as a stand against "Trump-style" leadership. Her line: "I have the ball in my hand, running toward the end zone, but we're not there yet." On Monday, Blakeman ran a final sweep through Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse and Binghamton, ending in Manhattan, while Hochul campaigned in Harlem.

Here's something easy to miss when you vote: flip your ballot. New York City voters have five ballot proposals, changes to the City Charter, which is basically the city's constitution. They would make it easier to put benches, ramps and outdoor cafés in public spaces, simplify paperwork for nonprofits and small businesses that work for the city, and speed up approval of big projects like street redesigns. Mamdani's office says that one alone could speed up street safety projects by at least thirty-three percent. Another would create one central system for building permits by twenty thirty. And the last sets savings targets for the rainy day fund, money the city puts aside for emergencies and downturns.

Next door, Senator Cory Booker faces Republican Justin Murphy in New Jersey, and in Connecticut, Governor Ned Lamont is going for a third term against Ryan Fazio.

Two more city stories. Eight people were charged last Thursday with stealing about seven million dollars from SNAP, the food stamp program. Prosecutors call it a "fraud factory." They say the group used real and fake delis to get people's benefit card numbers and PINs, then drained the cards the moment benefits loaded, leaving low-income New Yorkers with nothing for groceries, and used the money for luxury cars. All three who've been arrested pleaded not guilty.

And sad news for Mets fans. Jerry Koosman, the left-handed pitcher who was an icon of the nineteen sixty-nine Miracle Mets, has died at eighty-three.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now to tech, and one of the biggest deals ever. Marvell, the American chipmaker that helps companies like Amazon design their own AI chips, is buying Lumentum, which makes the lasers and optical parts that move data through AI data centers using light. It's an all-stock deal worth about one hundred fifteen billion dollars including debt, about eleven hundred thirty-two dollars a share, a twenty-four percent premium, meaning above its recent market price. CNBC called it the largest merger ever between two public tech companies. The bet is that the bottleneck in AI isn't only chips anymore, it's connecting them fast enough. It also sets Marvell up against Broadcom, Google's main custom-chip partner. Marvell's CEO, Matt Murphy, said they want to "help shape the next era of AI connectivity," but one analyst called it an enormous bet and asked if Marvell is paying too much. It's expected to close in the first half of twenty twenty-eight.

Next, the IPO everyone's watching: Anthropic, the company behind Claude, plans to list on the Nasdaq, the tech-heavy American stock exchange, under the ticker, the short trading symbol, A N T. The Wall Street Journal reports big investors want a valuation of one point five to one point six trillion dollars, down from the roughly two trillion discussed in recent months, after last week's tech selloff. An updated filing shows seventeen billion dollars in revenue for the quarter ending in September, up from eleven point six billion the quarter before. The roadshow, the series of pitch meetings with investors before the listing, could start next week, with a debut targeted for the week before Thanksgiving. Anthropic also filed in Japan to raise up to three and a half billion dollars from Japanese investors. That would likely be the biggest such raise ever tied to a foreign company's IPO there.

Another AI winner: Palantir, the data analytics company known for its government work, reported revenue of two point three one billion dollars on Monday, up ninety-six percent. For the first time, its sales to American businesses, nine hundred eighty-four million, beat its sales to the US government. CEO Alex Karp said, "I don't believe anyone's had consistent results like this in the history of software AI, ever."

And a sign of how companies are rethinking AI tools. Bloomberg reports Goldman Sachs removed access to AI coding tools, Claude Code and GitHub Copilot, for its bankers and traders, while keeping them for about twelve thousand engineers.

Now Israeli tech. TailorMed, an Israeli digital health company that helps American patients deal with the cost and paperwork of getting their medication, is being bought by the American private equity firm GrowthCurve in a deal Calcalist values at about two hundred fifty million dollars. It was founded in twenty sixteen, raised about sixty million, and has around one hundred twenty employees, fifty of them in Israel. The founders were motivated by serious illness in their own families.

A tougher story: XTEND, the Israeli drone maker, is laying off about ten percent of its roughly two hundred sixty-seven workers. It went public on the New York Stock Exchange two months ago at a one point five billion dollar valuation, through a reverse merger, where a private company merges into an already-listed one instead of doing a traditional IPO. It's now worth about three hundred fifty million, down more than seventy-five percent. And Intel's stock has fallen back to April levels after losing more than half its value in five weeks, worrying its thousands of Israeli workers, who were already shaken by last month's deadly explosion at its Kiryat Gat plant.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with a breach in your world. Revolve, the online fashion retailer behind the Revolve and FWRD sites, says hackers got into its shopping platform. According to state notices, the intruder got in on September eleventh, Revolve discovered it on September twenty-ninth, and it started notifying customers last week. About one hundred eighty thousand people are affected, including more than one hundred twenty-seven thousand in Texas. Stolen: names, addresses, card numbers and expiration dates, for customers who saved a card in their account. In an 8-K, a filing public companies must make with the SEC when something major happens, Revolve said "threat actors gained access to multiple company systems" and used automated tools to pull the data. It doesn't expect a big financial hit. If you've shopped there, check your card statements.

Then a lesson for every marketer. Polymarket, the prediction market where people bet on real-world events, was hit last Thursday through a tool most growth teams use without thinking: a website performance script. Researchers say a script from DebugBear, a company that measures how fast pages load, was swapped for a "drainer" that hijacked logged-in users for about an hour. Seven hundred twenty-six wallets were hit and about two point seven million dollars stolen. Polymarket is paying back three point nine million in total, including users hurt during the trading pause. The median victim lost one hundred seventy-one dollars. It's the second time since June that a vendor's code was used against Polymarket's site. Think about how many tracking pixels and analytics scripts are on your company's website. Each one is a supply-chain risk.

Two more threat stories. Cisco's research team, Talos, found a new infostealer, malware that steals passwords and personal data, called VOLTA. It uses AI models, including Claude and Gemini, to rank victims by how much their stolen data is worth. Hackers are doing lead scoring now, ranking which targets are worth chasing. And Google says it took down a botnet, a network of hijacked devices controlled by criminals, of about one point three million devices, mostly cheap Android TV boxes, and says the box maker sold criminals access.

Now the GTM story of the week, and it's a cautionary one. Perion, the Israeli ad-tech company, meaning technology for buying and placing online ads, is being bought by Affle, an Indian mobile marketing company, for about four hundred twenty million dollars in cash. Perion started in nineteen ninety-nine as IncrediMail, the email app with the animated effects. It later moved into advertising and was worth more than a billion dollars three years ago. Then in twenty twenty-four, Microsoft changed its search advertising terms, and that mattered because search was nearly half of Perion's revenue, and Microsoft made up eighty-five percent of that. Revenue is expected to drop from nearly nine hundred million to about four hundred million. Affle is paying eleven seventy a share, a thirty-four and a half percent premium, and wants Perion's retail media, ads on retailers' own sites and apps, connected TV, meaning streaming on smart TVs, and digital billboards. There's a go-shop period, a window to look for a better offer, until December eleventh. The marketer's lesson: when one partner controls most of your distribution, your whole business sits on their decisions.

## Business story

Time for our business story, and it starts on the Disney Channel. If you were a teenager around twenty ten, you probably know Bridgit Mendler. She appeared on "Wizards of Waverly Place," starred in "Good Luck Charlie," sang in "Lemonade Mouth," and put out a pop album. Then she did something unusual for a teen star. She went to college at USC, in a major she later called "an accident," anthropology. She went on to earn a master's degree at MIT, studied at Harvard Law, where she helped lead the Harvard Space Law Society, and spent time at the FCC, the American agency that regulates communications, where she said she "completely fell in love with space law."

During the pandemic, she started building antennas out of hardware-store parts and pulling down data from American weather satellites. That hobby showed her a real problem. There are thousands of satellites going up, but the ground stations, the antennas on Earth that talk to them, are old, expensive and booked up. As her company put it, the "one-lane rickety road for space data" needs to become "a ten-lane highway."

So in twenty twenty-two she co-founded Northwood Space in El Segundo, California, with her husband, Griffin Cleverly, as CTO, chief technology officer, the head of engineering, and Shaurya Luthra running software. Their seed round, the first real money, was six point three million dollars from Founders Fund and Andreessen Horowitz. In January this year they raised a one hundred million dollar Series B. Then came a nearly fifty million dollar contract with the US Space Force to upgrade the country's satellite control network.

On Monday, Northwood announced a three hundred seventy-five million dollar Series C, a later-stage round for scaling up, at a two billion dollar valuation, led by Andreessen Horowitz and DFJ Growth, plus financing from HSBC. The money goes to hiring and building out the contracts it already has.

Here's what's interesting for you as a marketer. Mendler's fame opened doors, but it also created doubt: people assumed a former actress couldn't be serious about antennas. What won investors over was a sharp, simple story, the highway for space data, backed by real contracts. It's a reminder that a memorable founder story only works when the product proves it.

## Wellness

And your wellness idea for today: protect your head from election news. You've got two elections in your feeds this week, America's today and Israel's coalition fight. Before the twenty twenty-four US election, the American Psychological Association found sixty-nine percent of Americans called the election a significant source of stress. Doctors at Rambam hospital in Haifa wrote some practical advice. Pick set times to check the news instead of refreshing all day, and not right before bed. Before you reply to a provocative post, ask yourself: am I reacting to the facts, or to the alarm they set off? Remember that someone who disagrees with you isn't your enemy. And keep your normal routine, your walk, your dinner with friends. The results will be there when you wake up.
