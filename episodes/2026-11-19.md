Good morning Sophie. It's Thursday, November nineteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. Today in China, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin sit down together for the first time ever, while back here, Naftali Bennett's surprise meeting with Netanyahu has Eisenkot's camp on edge. Anthropic priced the biggest stock market debut in history, and starts trading today. And a Van Gogh with a Nazi-era past just sold for a record one hundred ninety million dollars.

## Israel

We start here at home, one day after Eisenkot's camp took the Knesset speaker's chair, and the day began with a surprise. On Wednesday, Naftali Bennett, who leads the B'Yachad party inside Eisenkot's bloc, walked into Prime Minister Netanyahu's office in the Knesset and stayed for more than two hours, reportedly over lunch. Ynet says it was their first meeting since twenty twenty-one, when Bennett pushed Netanyahu out of power.

Officially, it was a security briefing. The head of the National Security Council was in the room, and Netanyahu's military secretary joined later. But Channel 12 reports the two men were alone for about half an hour. And Bennett didn't tell his partners in advance. Eisenkot's spokesperson said he had no notice, and one source in Eisenkot's camp, quoted by Ynet, called it a "strategic terror attack," accusing Netanyahu of using Bennett to break the bloc apart.

Why would Bennett do this? People close to him say he will not switch sides and will never serve under Netanyahu. His position, as reported, is a unity government, but only with a rotation where Eisenkot goes first as prime minister. And then the line that worried everyone: Bennett "isn't ruling out other scenarios." Likud is clearly trying to widen that crack. Communications Minister Shlomo Karhi said, "Eisenkot is not the only one in the other bloc; there are also Bennett and Liberman." The math explains why: by one count, Netanyahu's fifty-three seats plus Bennett's own faction would reach about sixty, one short of a majority.

A few hours later, the bloc's leaders, Eisenkot, Bennett, Avigdor Liberman, Yair Golan and Yair Lapid, met and put out a joint statement. All of them, it said, agree their top priority is a "national emergency government," meaning a government formed to deal with a crisis, led by Eisenkot, replacing Netanyahu quickly. But when Golan was asked if he trusts Bennett, he dodged, saying only that everyone understands "the magnitude of the moment."

The other big piece of the puzzle is the ultra-Orthodox parties. Channel 13 revealed a document written by Haredi activists and reviewed by senior rabbis and United Torah Judaism lawmakers, listing their price for joining Eisenkot. The core demand: a law exempting yeshiva students from army service, with no enlistment quotas and no sanctions. They want it passed before the government is even sworn in, a blanket pardon for yeshiva students the army now counts as deserters, and an override clause, a provision shielding a law from the High Court, so judges can't strike it down. They even want written sign-off from the attorney general, the army chief and the defense minister.

Here's the background. Since Israel's founding, yeshiva students have largely been exempt from the draft, and that has become one of the most bitter fights in Israeli society, especially after two years of war in which reservists served month after month. The High Court ruled in twenty twenty-four that there's no legal basis for a blanket exemption. Eisenkot's own plan gives draft evaders aged eighteen to twenty-six about six months to sign up through one of three tracks, with sanctions for those who don't. A United Torah Judaism official told Ynet it feels "easier to reach understandings with Liberman" than with Eisenkot. So the gap is huge, and whoever closes it probably forms the government.

Meanwhile, the new Knesset is already moving. On Wednesday it began early votes on bills to repeal laws from the previous government's judicial overhaul.

On the security front, the army and the Shin Bet launched an operation to destroy a tunnel west of the Yellow Line in central Gaza. The army called it an "immediate and direct threat." Residents of three blocks near Bureij and Nuseirat were told to leave, and Palestinian media described the first Israeli armored advance there since the ceasefire of October twenty twenty-five. On the aid side, the Erez crossing, the northern crossing into Gaza, reopened for inspecting aid trucks for the first time since February twenty twenty-five. And Admiral Brad Cooper, the head of CENTCOM, visited Israel to meet army chief Eyal Zamir, with Iran on the agenda.

## World

Now to the world, and the meeting the whole planet is watching. Trump landed in Shenzhen, in southern China, on Wednesday, and Xi Jinping met him at the airport. Xi also held his own meeting with Putin the same day. Today, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, the three leaders hold their first-ever three-way meeting.

What does each one want? Trump wants a win to sell at home. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera say he arrives weakened, after his party's losses in the midterms and the damage from the Iran war. His big ask is nuclear weapons. New START, the last treaty capping American and Russian nuclear arsenals, expired in February. Trump turned down Russia's offer to extend it, because he wants a new deal that includes China. Xi, reportedly, isn't keen on joining a treaty China never helped write.

Putin wants the opposite of isolation: to stand next to the American and Chinese leaders as an equal, despite the war in Ukraine. And Xi wants calm: no return to the tariff war. Remember, American tariffs on Chinese goods once hit one hundred forty-five percent, before a truce last year, now extended to January tenth. Washington also hopes Beijing will quietly push Iran to compromise, though analysts doubt China will take on that job. Expectations are low. One analyst said a handshake without an incident would count as a win.

The Iran war, meanwhile, keeps spreading across the Gulf. Kuwait said its air defenses were fighting off drone attacks, and Iran's army claimed it targeted American drone and missile sites at Kuwait's Ali Al Salem air base. Kuwait called the attacks a "dangerous escalation." Bahrain says it intercepted Iranian attacks too, a day after American jets struck a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Qeshm, an Iranian island in the Strait of Hormuz.

And one number shows what that does to shipping. Hung Cao, the acting Navy secretary, told senators that insuring a single trip through the strait now costs sixty-four million dollars, against about fifty-four thousand before the war. That number hasn't been independently checked, and other estimates are lower, but traffic through the strait is still about sixty-five percent below normal. Overnight, the Emirates' state oil company said one of its ships was hit there, with one crew member missing and eleven hurt.

And in American politics, one more sign of how big the Democrats' midterm night was. In Alaska, a state Trump won by more than thirteen points, Democrat Mary Peltola has been declared the winner of the Senate race over Republican Senator Dan Sullivan. She'll be the first Alaska Native in the US Senate.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where a Brooklyn congressman is about to make history. On Wednesday, House Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries to be the next House Speaker, the leader of the House of Representatives and second in line to the presidency after the vice president. He ran unopposed and won two hundred fifty-one of two hundred sixty-two votes. The full House votes in January, and with the new Democratic majority, he's set to become the first Black speaker. Jeffries has deep ties to New York's Jewish communities and has long been a strong supporter of Israel, but he's grown more critical lately. Last week he called for "dramatic change" on the West Bank, Gaza and the American military aid deal for Israel that expires in twenty twenty-eight.

And Mayor Mamdani faced a tough room at the New York Times DealBook Summit. Andrew Ross Sorkin pressed him on his post on October seventh, which critics said focused more on Palestinians than on the Israelis Hamas killed, and noted that about fifty-three percent of hate crimes in the city target Jews. Mamdani called that number "unacceptable," said he has called October seventh a war crime, and added, "My politics is one that believes that there has to be room to mourn everyone." He also said he's proud to lead "the most Jewish city in the United States of America." When Sorkin asked why he couldn't simply post about the victims that day and criticize Israel the next, the audience applauded.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a new law in Italy. Italy's parliament gave final approval, one hundred forty-seven to ninety-three, to an antisemitism law built on the IHRA definition, a widely used international definition of antisemitism. The law creates no new crimes or penalties. Supporters, including Holocaust survivor and life senator Liliana Segre, say Italy needs it: a Milan watchdog counted nine hundred sixty-three antisemitic incidents last year, up from two hundred forty-one in twenty twenty-two. Critics on the left, including the main opposition Democratic Party, say the definition's examples could be used to silence criticism of the Israeli government. The text itself says political criticism stays protected. It's the same fight playing out on campuses and in parliaments across the West: where criticism of Israel ends and antisemitism begins.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a historic day on Wall Street. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, priced its IPO at eighty-three dollars a share, well above its planned range of seventy to seventy-eight. CNBC reports it raised more than eighty-one billion dollars, the largest IPO on record, ahead of SpaceX's seventy-five billion in June. Reports put the valuation at around one and a half trillion dollars. On top of that, chip and cloud giants including Nvidia, Google and Microsoft agreed to buy twenty-six billion dollars of stock at the same price, in what's called a private placement, a sale of shares directly to chosen investors. Trading starts today on Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T.

The numbers in its filing are staggering both ways. Revenue hit seventeen billion dollars in the third quarter alone. But it lost more than a hundred billion dollars in the first nine months of the year. That's the AI bet in one sentence: enormous growth, enormous spending.

And the mood around AI stocks is nervous. On Wednesday, even after Nvidia reported revenue more than doubling, its shares fell about two percent, and the broader market slid, with the yield on ten-year American government bonds, the interest rate the government pays to borrow, at around five percent. When money costs that much, investors get pickier about paying sky-high prices for future growth.

Closer to home, a warning from Tel Aviv's Institute for National Security Studies. Its new report says quantum technology, computers and sensors built on the strange physics of tiny particles, could erode Israel's military edge. Quantum radar, for example, could one day spot stealth jets. The good news: Israel ranks second in the world, after China, in quantum investment relative to the size of its economy, and Israeli quantum companies raised nearly five hundred million dollars last year. The worrying part: the report says Iran leads the Middle East in the field.

## Business story

Our business story is a comeback at a store you know well. Target, the American retailer, just had its best quarter in nearly five years. Sales rose seven percent to twenty-seven billion dollars, and comparable sales rose five point six percent, the third straight quarter of growth. A year ago, they were shrinking.

What changed? Michael Fiddelke, a twenty-year Target veteran who became CEO in February, says it's many small fixes, not one magic move. Target cut prices on about two thousand more items, and most new holiday items cost twenty dollars or less. It leaned into what it calls "Fun one-oh-one": electronics, gaming, toys and trading cards. It expanded same-day delivery. And after its partnership with Ulta Beauty ended in August, it opened its own Target Beauty Studio in more than six hundred stores.

One footnote: profit got a boost from a tariff refund, money the government paid back on import taxes. But the sales growth is real, and Target raised its forecast for the year. The marketing lesson: Target didn't reinvent itself. It went back to what made people love it, cheap and fun, and said it loudly.

## Art and design

Next, art, and the Van Gogh we told you about on Sunday made history. At Sotheby's in New York on Wednesday night, "Blossoming Chestnut Trees," painted in eighteen ninety, sold for one hundred ninety point four million dollars. That shatters Van Gogh's auction record of one hundred seventeen million, and makes it the fourth most expensive artwork ever sold at auction. The buyer was the veteran New York dealer Bill Acquavella. From the same Argentine family collection, a Cézanne sold for one hundred eleven million.

The sale almost didn't happen. The painting once belonged to Samuel Fischer, a Jewish publisher in Berlin. His heirs said it was sold in nineteen thirty-six under Nazi-era pressure and asked Sotheby's to pull it. Just hours before the auction, Sotheby's announced a settlement between the sellers and all of Fischer's descendants. The terms weren't disclosed. The heirs called the talks "trusting and fair." It's a sign of how the art world now handles these claims: not by ignoring them, but by settling them before the gavel falls.

## Travel

That's down a tiny bit from last year, ending a three-year streak of records, mainly because travel got more expensive. The busiest days on the roads are the Tuesday and Wednesday before Thanksgiving, and then Sunday and Monday coming back. And the most popular international destination for Americans this Thanksgiving is Budapest, ahead of Cancun and Paris.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and what really slows aging, according to Yale. Researchers pooled fifty-one clinical studies and checked them against epigenetic clocks, blood tests that estimate your body's biological age from chemical marks on your DNA. The most consistent winner wasn't a supplement. It was the boring combination: regular exercise plus a healthy eating plan, whether Mediterranean, low-fat or low-carb. Over-the-counter supplements showed no measurable effect. Some prescription drugs, like metformin and semaglutide, the drug in Ozempic, also lowered the scores, but the researchers stress that doesn't mean anyone should take them for aging, and a younger score doesn't yet prove you'll live longer. The takeaway: skip the pricey pills and keep moving and eating well.

## Funding and acquisitions

The money is back today, and the biggest story comes from the company that sets the tone for the whole industry. Palo Alto Networks, the American cybersecurity giant that bought Israel's CyberArk, announced it's buying Applied Compute for about four and a half billion dollars, in an all-stock deal.

What does Applied Compute do? It isn't a security company at all. It's an AI startup, founded just last year by three former OpenAI researchers, Yash Patil, Rhythm Garg and Linden Li. It helps companies take open-weight models, then train them further on their own data and run them. That running part is called AI inference, using a trained model to answer requests. It already has more than a hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue, and customers like Microsoft and DoorDash. The price is striking: earlier this month, Applied Compute raised three hundred fifty million dollars at a valuation of three point two five billion. That round was led by the investor Elad Gil, with Nvidia, Shopify Ventures and HubSpot Ventures joining. Weeks later, Palo Alto is paying four and a half billion. Patil will become Palo Alto's co-chief AI officer, and the research team moves over too. The deal isn't free for Palo Alto: it says it could shave up to one percentage point off its operating margin, the share of revenue left as profit, this fiscal year and up to two the next. It also follows CrowdStrike's deal two weeks ago for Dreadnode, an AI security testing company. The two biggest platforms are now racing to own AI security.

CEO Nikesh Arora explained the logic in one line: "Every enterprise is going to run a mix of open and closed models, and every one of those models needs to be secured."

The deal came with earnings, and they were strong. Revenue rose thirty-nine percent to three point four four billion dollars. Annual recurring revenue from its newer products jumped sixty-six percent to nine point seven three billion. Palo Alto raised its forecast for the year, and the shares rose about nine percent after hours. Rivals rose too: CrowdStrike about five percent, Zscaler about three. Two more numbers you'll want for interviews. Prisma AIRS, its AI security product, is at nearly one hundred seventy million dollars in annual recurring revenue with more than a thousand customers. And Idira, its identity platform built on CyberArk, grew twenty-three percent. So the Israeli acquisition is now a growth engine inside a giant.

Second, a fresh Israeli seed round. VeroSecurity raised an eleven million dollar seed round led by TLV Partners, the Tel Aviv venture fund. It was founded last year by CEO Elad Tessler and CTO Daniel Meirov, both veterans of Unit 8200. It works on what it calls AI security engineering: companies now build their own AI tools and agents, and VeroSecurity turns the security team's rules into controls that are actually enforced while those agents run, blocking actions they're not allowed to take. Eleven million is a healthy seed for an Israeli cyber company, and it plans to grow its go-to-market presence in the US. Notice one more thing: it also runs a community and training site for the new discipline it's naming. That's a marketing move, building the category's community before the category even has a budget line.

## People moves

Now people moves, and today there's a marketing chief. Mitek Systems, a Nasdaq-listed company that does identity verification, checking that a person online is who they claim to be, and fraud prevention for more than seven thousand organizations, named Laura Apel its CMO. She'll run brand, demand generation, product marketing and communications. Where does she come from? Not traditional cyber. She led global marketing for Vonage's API business, part of Ericsson, and before that at Mitto and Telesign, communications and digital identity companies. Her stated goal: "scaling our go-to-market engine." Her CEO, Ed West, praised her ability "to translate marketing into measurable business results." That's the language CMOs are hired on right now: pipeline and revenue, not just brand.

Second, an Israeli company building out its leadership. Huskeys, an Israeli startup that helps security teams see and control the web traffic hitting a company's network, named Eyal Kaldes its chief customer officer, the executive in charge of keeping customers happy and renewing after the sale. He was chief customer officer at HackerOne, which pays hackers to report security flaws, and before that led service for more than thirty thousand customers at PerimeterX. Huskeys raised a twenty-seven million dollar Series A led by Blackstone in September, about five months after launching, bringing its total to thirty-five million dollars, and lists TikTok, Legoland and Hugging Face as customers.

And two moves from SecurityWeek's weekly list. Vanta, the compliance automation company, hired Fredrick "Flee" Lee as CISO; he led security at Reddit through its IPO. And Microsoft hired Allie Mellen, a well-known Forrester analyst, to a senior security research role. Why should a marketer care? Because analyst relations, managing how research firms like Forrester and Gartner see your company, is a big part of cyber marketing, and when a top analyst switches sides, vendors lose a familiar voice in the room.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and two lessons from Palo Alto's night, starting with pricing.

On the call, Arora said that in about six to nine months, Palo Alto expects to start metering agentic traffic, meaning it will measure and charge for the AI agent activity running through its products beyond what a customer has licensed. That's usage-based pricing, where you pay for how much you use, not a fixed number of seats. Why does it matter? Because AI agents don't fit the old model. A company might have a thousand employees but ten thousand agents. If the industry's leader starts charging by usage, every startup will be asked by buyers, and by investors, how its pricing handles agents. A marketer who can explain value in usage terms will be very useful.

Second, the story behind the deal. Palo Alto didn't just buy a product; it bought a narrative: security belongs at the center of how AI is built, not wrapped around it at the end. One analyst, Catharine Trebnick of Rosenblatt, said AI tools are finding vulnerabilities faster than companies can fix them, which pushes customers toward fewer, bigger platforms. That's consolidation, buyers cutting the number of vendors they use. And a commentary on CYBR.SEC.Media recalled a startup CEO's prediction that within two years almost every cyber vendor would claim to do everything. The lesson for a startup marketer: when giants claim everything, your edge is a sharp, specific promise. Huskeys is a good example. It invented a name for its category, Network Edge Security Management, so it isn't lost in a crowded list of web security tools.

And a closing note from Mitek's hire: companies are hiring CMOs from outside cyber, from communications and API businesses, when what they need is a scalable growth machine. Your growth marketing background fits that profile.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, VeroSecurity. A fresh Israeli seed round, founders from 8200, and a plan to build its US go-to-market. It's also creating a community around a new category. Companies at this stage often hire their first marketer to own exactly that kind of story, which is right in your lane.

Second, Huskeys. A Series A led by Blackstone, a new chief customer officer, well-known customers, and a category name it needs to make famous. Customer stories with brands like TikTok are gold for marketing, and someone has to tell them.

Third, Mitek. A new CMO building out brand, demand generation and product marketing usually adds people in the first months. It's a public company in identity and fraud, a space where AI-generated fraud makes the story urgent.
