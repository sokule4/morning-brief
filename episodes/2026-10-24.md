## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, October twenty-fourth, and this is your Morning Brief. Three big ones today. Two Israeli soldiers were killed in a shooting attack near Hebron, and Netanyahu sealed off the city three days before the election. A senior American diplomat says a US strike on Iran could "absolutely" happen this weekend, while oil hits its highest price since March. And tomorrow Brazil chooses its president, in a race that is basically a coin flip. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in the West Bank, because this is the story that shaped Israel's final campaign weekend. On Friday afternoon, two Palestinian gunmen opened fire at the Judea Junction, a military checkpoint on Route thirty-five near Hebron. The army says they had an M16 rifle and a pistol. The firefight lasted more than twenty minutes. Two Israeli soldiers were killed: Staff Sergeant Ron Yonatan Roytman, twenty-three, and Sergeant Yuval Shai Svilem, twenty. Medics say several other Israelis were wounded. Soldiers from the Home Front Command and a company commander who rushed to the scene shot both gunmen dead.

Who were they? Palestinian reports and Israeli officials say they were two twenty-seven-year-old men from Hebron, from the same family. The army chief, Eyal Zamir, said a large amount of ammunition was found in their car, meant for a bigger attack. Hamas praised the shooting as a "heroic operation" and mourned the attackers, but did not claim it. There's no sign of involvement by the Palestinian Authority, the Palestinian government that runs parts of the West Bank.

Then came the response. Netanyahu, together with Defense Minister Israel Katz and the security chiefs, ordered an immediate closure of Hebron, meaning the army seals the roads in and out, and told forces to prepare a full lockdown of the West Bank. He said troops would search house to house and seize weapons, and he told Hamas: "You have no place to hide." Within hours the army began demolishing Palestinian-owned buildings near the site. By Saturday morning, every gate into and out of Hebron was reportedly blocked to cars, and the Palestinian news agency WAFA said dozens of residents were forced out of their homes in some neighborhoods. One mother told the Associated Press she was stopped at a gate while taking her eleven-year-old daughter to a cancer appointment.

Why does this blow up now? Hebron is the largest Palestinian city in the West Bank, with a small Israeli settler community inside it, and it's been a flashpoint for decades. Palestinians see closures and demolitions as collective punishment for the acts of a few. Israel's government sees a terror cell that was stopped just before a much worse attack. And it lands three days before a vote. Far-right ministers pushed hard: Itamar Ben Gvir called for dismantling the Palestinian Authority, and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich called for a campaign in the West Bank "exactly like in Gaza." Netanyahu claimed, without evidence, that Hamas might try to sway the election with attacks. The West Bank and Gaza are expected to be under lockdown Monday and Tuesday for the vote anyway.

Which brings us to the election itself. Israel votes this Tuesday. In case you missed it, the final polls before the legal polling ban all told the same story: Gadi Eisenkot's party Yashar in first with about twenty-two or twenty-three seats, Likud a few behind, and neither bloc anywhere near the sixty-one seats needed to govern. Two parties are hovering right at the electoral threshold, which could swing the whole math. After the attack, Netanyahu canceled all his Friday campaign events. Earlier, someone threw a glass bottle toward his motorcade at Jerusalem's Mahane Yehuda market; nothing was damaged. And a surprising voice weighed in: Ronald Lauder, president of the World Jewish Congress and once a close Netanyahu ally, told Haaretz that Israel would "stand to lose badly" if Netanyahu wins again, and that if he had to pick, he'd pick Eisenkot's Yashar.

Now Iran, and a very tense weekend. Tom Barrack, the US ambassador to Turkey and Trump's special envoy for Syria, told a podcaster that a US strike this weekend could "absolutely" happen, while adding that Trump is still open to a deal and that "the consequence won't be regime change." Trump, asked about it after landing in Florida, said: "That was my ambassador to Turkey? He's a pretty powerful man, I guess. If that's the case, I don't know anything about it." But at a rally that same night he said: "We may do another little Epic Fury." Epic Fury is the name of the American strikes on Iran that began in February.

Meanwhile, the signals keep stacking up. The State Department is cutting staff at its embassies in Jordan, Lebanon, Qatar and the UAE, after a similar order for Saudi Arabia earlier this week. The Director of National Intelligence, the top US spy official, warned in a memo that Iranian plots against Americans have reached "advanced stages." Reuters reports Iran has drawn up plans to hit the undersea internet cables in the Gulf if it's attacked again, which could knock out connections across the region. And this morning Iran's armed forces chief, Ali Abdollahi, said Iran will "under no circumstances" let American warships into the Persian Gulf.

At sea, the Revolutionary Guard now claims two more supertankers hit mines near the Strait of Hormuz, carrying more than four and a half million barrels of oil between them. That's not independently confirmed, and Trump has accused Iran of "making up fake hits" to push prices up. But some of it is very real: an oil slick of about sixty square miles, traced to a damaged tanker, washed onto beaches in the UAE, and residents reported smelling gas. Brent crude jumped three and a half percent on Friday to more than one hundred and seventeen dollars a barrel, its highest since March, and the Dow fell about five hundred and fifty points.

Diplomats are scrambling. Foreign ministers from Turkey, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Germany, France, Britain and Italy met in Istanbul to push for talks and a reopened strait. Turkey warned a new attack would widen the war. And Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, will fly to Moscow next week to meet Vladimir Putin. Why it matters for you: any US strike would likely mean Iranian missiles toward Israel again, right around election day. Keep your phone alerts on this weekend.

One more big vote, and it's tomorrow. Brazil, Latin America's largest economy, holds a presidential runoff, a second-round vote between the top two candidates. On one side, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, eighty, a leftist going for a fourth term. On the other, Flávio Bolsonaro, forty-two, a right-wing senator and the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is under house arrest serving a twenty-seven-year sentence over his attempt to overturn the twenty twenty-two election. Flávio surprised everyone by finishing first in round one, and he's run as his father's heir: tough on crime and drug gangs, lower taxes, close to Trump. Lula is running on the economy and workers' rights; Congress just approved two paid days off a week, one of his priorities. The final Datafolha poll, out Thursday, has them tied at fifty-fifty in valid votes. Flávio's lead has shrunk for two straight weeks. If Flávio wins, Trump gains another close ally in the region; if Lula wins, he starts a fourth term at eighty.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where it's voting day one. Early voting, which runs through November first, opened this morning, and today is also the last day to register. The headline race is governor: Kathy Hochul, the Democrat, wants a second full term; Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive and a close Trump ally, is challenging her. Hochul has led every public poll, and she has a huge money edge, about nineteen million dollars in the bank through September against roughly one hundred seventy-eight thousand for Blakeman. His pitch is tax cuts: no state income tax for single people earning under fifty thousand dollars, a ten percent cut for everyone under two hundred fifty thousand, and a promise to cut electric bills "in half" on day one, which experts call unrealistic. Hochul's pitch is her record, like universal free school meals, and tying Blakeman to Trump and to recent ICE violence. In polls, cost of living is the number one issue, by far.

Speaking of ICE, Mayor Mamdani set off a firestorm on Friday with a Spanish-language video telling New Yorkers their rights if immigration agents show up. His advice: ICE can't enter a private home without a judicial warrant, meaning one signed by a judge, so check for the words "United States District Court" and a judge's signature. If it's not there, keep the door closed and say you don't consent. You can stay silent, ask if you're free to go, and talk to a lawyer before signing anything. Republicans were furious. Senator Rick Scott said the mayor is "openly helping illegal aliens evade ICE," and Long Island Congressman Nick LaLota said, "NYC is becoming more like Paris every day." Some commentators called it "sedition." Mamdani's side says this is simply what the Constitution already guarantees.

In schools, Chalkbeat got its hands on the list of thirty-eight education tech products the city flagged when Mamdani restricted AI that students use directly. The biggest casualty is Amira, an AI reading tutor used in more than two hundred city schools, which was banned outright. Some companies are pushing back: the textbook giant HMH says none of its flagged products have AI that students use, and the translation tool Wordly says it doesn't use generative AI at all. If you work in ed-tech marketing, this is the new reality: your product's "AI" label can now get you banned.

And a weird one connecting New York to world news. A tanker carrying forty thousand metric tons of Russian diesel is on its way to New York harbor. It's the first shipment under a deal Trump announced to import Russian diesel, as fuel prices climb with the Iran crisis. Trump also invoked an emergency production law this week and put one hundred fifty million dollars into boosting American gas and diesel refining.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, and a story of stubborn light. On Wednesday, a twenty-six-year-old Chabad man named Yosef, Chabad being the Hasidic movement known for outreach, was stabbed on Forty-Seventh Street in the Diamond District, where he regularly helps people put on tefillin, the small leather prayer boxes Jewish men wear. Doctors at Bellevue found no internal bleeding, and he didn't need stitches. Police are investigating it as a possible hate crime; the suspect is described as a woman in her forties, and no arrest yet. The next day, Yosef was back on the same street, same routine. He told the Jewish Telegraphic Agency: "We need more mitzvahs in the world, we need more light in the world. We can't allow some hate to stop us."

Behind stories like that is a big bill. A UJA-Federation of New York survey of thirty-nine synagogues, schools and community centers found their security spending has more than doubled since October seventh. The median went from fifty thousand dollars a year to about one hundred fifty-two thousand. That's money that used to go to programs, teachers and youth trips.

In Israel, one of the most interesting small parties in this election is called the Haredi Public, a new ultra-Orthodox party that actually supports drafting ultra-Orthodox men who aren't full-time yeshiva students into the army. That's close to heresy in the Haredi political world. Its rabbinic committee is chaired by Yehoshua Pfeffer, a British-born rabbi in Jerusalem. The platform: add secular studies like math and English to Haredi boys' schools, make it normal for Haredi men to work, and use incentives, not arrests, to get people to enlist. Two of its top four candidates are women, which the established Haredi parties would never do. The background: in twenty twenty-four Israel's High Court, the country's top court, struck down the old blanket exemption for yeshiva students, and since then most draft-age Haredi men have still refused to serve. Reservists, some with more than six hundred days in uniform since October seventh, are exhausted. The party is polling under the threshold, so it probably won't get in. But Pfeffer says it will run again.

And a lovely history story. Writer Zachary Kranc has shown that Oscar Levis, a Black pitcher who starred in the Negro Leagues and in Cuba in the nineteen-twenties, was Jewish. Levis was born in Jamaica; his father, Amos DaCosta Levy, came from Jamaica's Sephardi Jewish community, Jews whose roots trace back to Spain and Portugal. Kranc dug through synagogue records and family testimony, and found old newspapers that called Levis a "Spanish Jew." Levis threw the first no-hitter in Cuban baseball history in nineteen twenty-four, and later ran Chick's Bar and Grill in Harlem for thirty-five years. By Major League Baseball's official stats, he's now among the best Jewish pitchers ever. Kranc's line: "He was Black and he was Jewish."

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, starting with an Israeli crypto heavyweight. Fireblocks, the Israeli-founded company that lets banks and exchanges store and move digital assets safely, is laying off about sixty to seventy of its roughly nine hundred employees, about seven percent, including dozens in Israel. This comes right after reports that Visa was looking at buying Fireblocks for around two and a half billion dollars. Fireblocks' answer was memorable: "This is news to us, and not true at present time." Notice that "at present time." For context, Fireblocks was valued at eight billion dollars in early twenty twenty-two, so even if a Visa deal happened, two and a half billion would be a big step down. It's also pushing into stablecoins, crypto tokens pegged to the dollar, which helps explain why a payments giant like Visa might be interested.

A sharp piece of analysis from Calcalist this week asks a question every Israeli should care about: is Israel's economy booming, or is Nvidia? Here's the idea. A growing share of what Israel counts as its economic output is what economists call "production abroad," products designed in Israel, like Nvidia's chips, manufactured by contractors overseas and sold abroad. That slice has jumped from under two percent of the economy in twenty twenty-three to almost six percent this spring. With it, Israel's growth forecast for this year is five point six percent. Without it, three point four. The Bank of Israel governor, Amir Yaron, says the lower number "better represents economic activity." In plain words: the headline numbers look great, but life for most Israelis at home isn't improving that fast, and the profits from chips mostly go to foreign shareholders.

In funding, Modelyo, an Israeli startup founded by a reserve lieutenant colonel from special operations and two encryption experts, raised about seventeen million dollars, led by the US fund OlivTree. It lets companies use cloud services and AI agents while their data stays encrypted, even while it's being processed. That's called confidential computing, keeping data locked even while it's in use. The big fear it sells against: handing your company secrets to an AI you don't control.

From Silicon Valley, Tesla reported its third quarter. Revenue was flat at about twenty-eight billion dollars, but profit fell forty-three percent, as spending on research jumped sixty-nine percent. Where's that money going? Robots and robotaxis. Tesla has started producing its Optimus humanoid robot in California, its Cybercab robotaxi has joined its fleet in Austin, and it says it will start selling Cybercabs by the end of the year. Investors are basically being asked to pay for the future while the car business stalls.

And in AI products, Anthropic launched a research preview this week of something called Claude Duet, an assistant that sends you a morning summary across apps like Gmail and Slack, and can do tasks on its own computer in the cloud. Notably, it has to ask your permission before sending messages, paying, signing documents or deleting anything. That "ask first" design is becoming the industry standard, and you'll see why in a minute.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cyber and go-to-market. First, a funding round with a marketer's fingerprints on it. Mave, an Israeli startup founded last year, raised a twenty-eight million dollar Series A led by Red Dot Capital Partners. Mave uses AI to run security investigations automatically across all the tools a company already has, without moving the data into one central place, and it logs every step so a human can check its work. The CEO, Raziel Oren, is a Unit 8200 veteran who previously led go-to-market at Wing Security. So a GTM leader became a founder, and the company says revenue grew more than four hundred percent in six months. His pitch line is good copy: "Attackers do not wait. Every pivot adds delay, and every delay creates risk."

Second, the AI agent problem moves into your bank account. Axios reports that major US banks are spotting outside AI agents inside customer accounts, logging in with the customer's own password, and sometimes doing things the customer apparently didn't ask for. Banks often can't tell whether the human told the agent to do it. Morgan Stanley is now blocking third-party agents, while JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are restricting them, and most banks are moving toward read-only access. For anyone marketing AI agents to financial companies, "permission and audit trail" is now the first slide, not the last.

Third, a job posting that should make every writer and marketer smile. Anthropic is hiring an executive communications writer, someone to draft speeches and messaging for its top leaders, and the pay goes up to three hundred forty-five thousand dollars a year. The posting asks for someone who'll challenge weak ideas and unsupported claims, with "a low ego" and "epistemic humility," meaning knowing what you don't know. This at a company whose finance chief says AI writes ninety percent of its code. The lesson: AI can write, but companies still pay a premium for human judgment about what to say.

Fourth, good news on early-career hiring. While much of Israeli tech froze junior hiring, Salesforce says twenty twenty-six is its biggest year ever for junior hires in Israel, mostly engineers and product managers. Its recruiting director, Einat Frish, said: "We never stopped investing in our talent." The logic is smart: if nobody hires juniors today, there are no mid-level people in five years. Another data point: enrollment in computer science degrees in Israel fell about seven percent this year, while AI and data science programs grew.

And a quick update on the PHP security flaw we told you about this week. Governments from the US to Japan have told website owners to patch fast, and Wikimedia and the BBC took parts of their sites offline as a precaution. Experts expect more attacks in the coming weeks.

## Business story

Now our business story, and it's a kibbutz tale with a painful twist. It starts in the mid-nineteen-eighties at Kibbutz Yizre'el, a small community in the Jezreel Valley founded by former Palmach fighters after Israel's War of Independence. A kibbutz member who had come from South Africa knew an engineer back in Johannesburg who'd built a prototype robot that cleans swimming pools. The kibbutz pooled its money, bought the prototype and the rights, and had it flown to Israel. That became Maytronics, and its Dolphin pool robots went on to clean pools from Paris municipal swimming pools to Disney resorts in Orlando.

In twenty twelve came the famous moment. An American pool company, Hayward, offered to buy the kibbutz's stake at a value of about three hundred forty-five million shekels for the whole company. Even the company's CEO and chairman supported the deal. But the kibbutz members voted no. And for a decade, that looked like genius. Maytronics, already listed on the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange since twenty-oh-four, dominated its market, and by twenty-seventeen its income made up about eighty-three percent of the kibbutz's yearly earnings, funding members' stipends, housing and even a music school. Then the pandemic hit, everyone was stuck at home building backyard pools, and in November twenty twenty-one Maytronics was worth about nine point one billion shekels, around three billion dollars.

Then it all reversed. Pandemic demand faded and left warehouses full of unsold robots. Chinese competitors started selling pool robots for around five hundred dollars, while a Dolphin cost up to twenty-four hundred. Maytronics was slow to launch a cheaper model, and slow to move into online and direct-to-consumer sales, meaning selling straight to buyers instead of through stores and distributors. Add Israel's long reserve duty disrupting production, and a strong shekel hurting exports. Last year it lost two hundred twenty-two million shekels.

And now the deal on the table. The investment fund FIMI wants to put in two hundred million shekels, about sixty-five million dollars, for half the company. That values Maytronics, before the new money, at about two hundred million shekels. Remember the offer the kibbutz turned down in twenty twelve? Three hundred forty-five million. The two sides signed a memorandum of understanding, an early non-binding agreement, and shareholders still have to approve it. If it closes, the kibbutz would keep twenty-eight percent. Calcalist estimates each member's share of the stake is now worth about three hundred thousand shekels, down from around seventeen million at the peak.

Why this matters to you as a marketer: Maytronics had the beloved brand, the premium product and the market lead. What it didn't have was a fast answer when cheap competitors and online buying changed how customers shop. Brand love doesn't protect you if your channel and your price point fall behind.

## Celebs, culture and fashion

Next, celebs, culture and fashion, starting with Swifties on high alert. Today is exactly twenty years since Taylor Swift released her first album, "Taylor Swift," on October twenty-fourth, two thousand six, the one with "Tim McGraw" and "Teardrops on My Guitar." Swift bought back the rights to her whole catalog last year and has said she already re-recorded the debut album and would release it "when the time is right." Re-recorded versions are called "Taylor's Version," the copies she fully owns. This week her website updated its archive section to celebrate twenty years, with early music videos and her old awards, and fans are convinced an announcement is coming. To be clear, nothing is confirmed yet. But if your feeds blow up today, now you know why.

At the movies this weekend, besides "Clayface," which we mentioned yesterday, there's a lot. Two new openings: Sacha Baron Cohen directs for the first time in "Ali G: Who Iz I?", where Ali G finds out he's going to be a dad and heads to America to win back his fame. Jenna Ortega plays a robot companion in Taika Waititi's "Klara and the Sun," based on Kazuo Ishiguro's novel. On Broadway, Billy Crystal's one-man show "860" officially opened this week at the Imperial Theatre. It's named after the house number of the Pacific Palisades home he lost in last year's fires, and it's about family, luck, loss and how laughter gets you through. It runs through January third.

Now a fashion trend with a marketing angle: celebrities with C-suite titles. Dwyane Wade, the NBA legend, was just named "Chief Performance Officer" for Revolve's men's businesses, Revolve Man and the luxury site FWRD Man. He'll advise on which products to carry, brand marketing and partnerships, and be the face of the menswear push. That comes a week after Revolve made Kendall Jenner global creative director of FWRD. Is it a real job or a fancy endorsement deal? Usually somewhere in between. The title signals to customers that the star actually shapes the product, which makes the marketing feel more authentic than a simple ad.

A real executive move: Hector Muelas, the Spanish-born creative executive who led brand creative at Tiffany, is taking over as chief brand creative officer at Louis Vuitton, the person who guards how the brand looks and feels everywhere, from ads to stores. And a new name to know: Zane Li, a Chongqing-born, New York-based designer who founded the womenswear label Lii in twenty twenty-four, won this year's CFDA and Vogue Fashion Fund, the American fashion industry's big prize for young designers, which comes with three hundred thousand dollars and mentoring. It's his third major prize this year, after the Karl Lagerfeld Prize at the LVMH Prize in Paris. His honest quote: the business side is "the difficult part."

## Art and design

Next, art and design, and Paris is shutting its doors today. Because of protests, the Louvre, the Musée d'Orsay, the Orangerie and the Picasso Museum are all closed this Saturday, along with the Fondation Louis Vuitton, the Pinault Collection, and galleries including Gagosian and Hauser and Wirth. France has been shaken by protests over the cost of living and in support of striking high school students, with looting in cities like Lyon and Limoges last weekend. Art Basel Paris already closed early on Friday night, cancelling its public weekend days.

Before the shutdown, though, the market had a huge night. On Thursday at Sotheby's Paris, a giant late painting of irises by Claude Monet, finished in nineteen twenty-five, a year before he died, and never shown in public before, sold for about fifty-six point two million dollars, according to ARTnews. That's a new record for any painting sold at auction in France. It came from the collection of Jeanne Schlumberger, an oil-fortune heiress who kept it hidden from almost everyone. It's also the largest painting from Monet's iris series ever offered for sale.

And closer to home, tomorrow the Tel Aviv Museum of Art opens "Black Box," a retrospective, meaning a show covering an artist's whole career, of the photographer Alex Levac, who has shot for Haaretz since nineteen ninety-three. About two hundred photos: the intifadas, meaning the Palestinian uprisings, the Oslo Accords, the nineteen-nineties peace deals, and Rabin's assassination, plus the original negative of his famous nineteen eighty-four photo of a captured bus hijacker who was alive, contradicting the official story. Opening two days before a historic election, it's a sharp mirror for Israeli society. It runs until June.

## Wellness

For wellness, one easy idea for the weekend. Researchers at the University of Texas at Austin tracked young adults wearing Fitbits for months, and found that people who were active on more days of the week got more non-REM sleep, the deep, restoring stage of sleep. As little as ten minutes a day of moderate to vigorous movement, the kind where you can still talk but you're breathing harder, was linked to better sleep, better mood and less stress the next day. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. But the practical takeaway is simple: instead of one big weekend workout, try a brisk ten-minute walk every single day.
