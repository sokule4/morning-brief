## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Thursday, October twenty-ninth, and this is your Morning Brief. In Israel, the final half a million ballots, mostly soldiers' votes, are being counted today, with Netanyahu's bloc stuck at fifty-three seats and his Likud party now claiming the Arab parties "stole" the election. Trump says a strike on Iran after the US midterms is "possible," while Tehran weighs a new peace draft from Qatar. And Brazil's defeated presidential candidate, Flávio Bolsonaro, slipped out of the country and landed in Miami. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start with the count, because it isn't over. With about ninety percent of regular ballots counted, here is where things stand. Netanyahu's bloc, Likud plus the far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties, has fifty-three seats. Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc has forty-eight. And the two Arab parties have nineteen seats together, nearly double the ten they had in the last Knesset. That's their best result ever. A majority, remember, is sixty-one out of one hundred twenty, so nobody is there.

Party by party: Likud has twenty-six seats, the largest party, but its share of the vote fell to about twenty-one percent, from twenty-three percent in twenty twenty-two. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, has twenty-one. The Joint List, an alliance of Arab-led parties, has about twelve or thirteen. Bennett's list, Together, has twelve. Then Yair Golan's Democrats with eight, and Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu, Shas, United Torah Judaism and Otzma Yehudit, Itamar Ben-Gvir's far-right party, with seven each. Ra'am, the Islamist Arab party, has six or seven, and the Religious Zionism list has six.

Now, the last big piece. Last night the Central Elections Committee started counting the double-envelope ballots, votes cast away from your home polling station, mostly by soldiers. There are more than half a million of them, a record, compared with about four hundred sixty thousand in twenty twenty-two. Soldiers turned out at almost eighty percent. The count should finish this evening, maybe Friday. History says these votes usually move one or two seats, and they tend to favor Zionist parties over the ultra-Orthodox and Arab parties. So small shifts are likely, but analysts don't expect the overall deadlock to change. After that come audits and spot recounts next week, and official results on November fourth.

And here's the story that turned the morning after into a fight. Likud put out a statement claiming the Arab parties "stole" the election. It alleged "thousands of envelopes with cash and Ra'am slips," threats against polling staff, double voting and forged ballots, and said it had sent "hundreds of testimonies" to police, the Shin Bet and the elections committee. On election night, Netanyahu had already posted a video suggesting the high turnout in Arab towns was an attempt to "steal" the vote. Some context. Hebrew media had reported before the vote that Likud prepared fraud complaints in advance, in case it lost. The elections committee says it saw "no indication" of systematic tampering, but that it is reviewing Likud's request as part of its routine integrity checks. And the head of the Shin Bet, David Zini, a Netanyahu appointee, said on election day that the vote was being conducted properly. Why it matters: Arab citizens are about a fifth of Israel's population, and their turnout was a big part of this election's turnout of seventy-seven point seven percent, the highest in decades. Calling their votes stolen is a serious charge, and it raises the temperature for whatever comes next.

So what does come next? There are three paths. One: Eisenkot builds a government that relies on the Arab parties, maybe as a minority government, one without sixty-one seats of its own, supported from outside on key votes. Golan is pushing for that, posting: "Netanyahu lost. I know how to count to one hundred twenty." The obstacles are real, though. Several parties in Eisenkot's camp promised not to sit with Arab parties, and the Joint List's chair, Yousef Jabareen, says he won't join any coalition "under the current political circumstances," while warning that Netanyahu's opponents "will have to talk to us." Ra'am, by contrast, says it wants in. Path two: a unity government, where Netanyahu tries to pull one opposition party to his side. Path three: yet another election. President Herzog urged the parties to avoid that. Analysts say finding the next prime minister could take weeks, maybe months, and until then Netanyahu stays caretaker prime minister.

One more twist from Washington. Trump told reporters his endorsement helped Netanyahu "a lot," and that a tie could "sort of be considered a win." But Ynet reports that people close to Trump say he doesn't want Ben-Gvir, and probably not Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich either, in Netanyahu's next government. Meanwhile, the Times of Israel reports Netanyahu has assured both men he won't abandon them.

Now Iran. Asked on Wednesday whether the US would strike after next Tuesday's midterms, Trump said, quote, "I can't tell you that... but anything's possible." He said Iran wants a deal but is "tapping it along," adding, "maybe they're waiting for the election or something. The election is not going to help them." That follows the Wall Street Journal report we told you about yesterday, that the US warned Gulf states to prepare for a short, intense campaign.

At the same time, diplomacy is still alive. Reuters reports that Qatari mediators handed Iran a new draft agreement to end the war on Sunday, and that Tehran is weighing it and should answer by the end of this week. And at the UN Security Council, Rafael Grossi, head of the IAEA, the UN's nuclear watchdog, said there's no sign Iran has tried to repair its bombed nuclear sites since June twenty twenty-five. The catch: inspectors still can't verify where Iran's stockpile of near-weapons-grade uranium is, and he urged Iran to let them back in.

On the cost of this war: CBS reports, based on a US Central Command estimate, that it has cost the US military at least forty-seven billion dollars so far, about twenty-nine billion of that just to replace missiles and munitions. And the fighting in the Gulf continues: Saudi Arabia says it shot down a ballistic missile fired by the Houthis toward the city of Taif on Tuesday night.

In Gaza, the army says it killed three members of Hamas's military wing, including one it says took part in the October seventh abductions, and accused Hamas of violating the ceasefire.

Finally, a story we've been following from Brazil. Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who lost Sunday's runoff to Lula by about four point four million votes, has left the country. Here's the route, according to the AP. On Tuesday night, he and his family crossed by land into Argentina, then flew on a private charter to Guayaquil, Ecuador, landing at four in the morning, and took off minutes later for Miami, where they landed Wednesday morning. Why the hurry? Brazil's Supreme Court is investigating him in two corruption cases: one alleges he used a sports betting company to launder money stolen from senior citizens' pensions, the other that he took millions of dollars from a disgraced banker. He calls the moves against him an attempt to intimidate him. The court had ordered him to hand in his passports by noon on Tuesday; his allies say he also holds an Italian one. He refused to concede for two days and then threatened to try to annul, meaning cancel, the result. The US granted him and his family visas on Monday, and Trump called them "fine people." Lula said Bolsonaro "could have stayed here and proven that the accusations are not true," and called the escape "shameful." The family history rhymes: his father also left for Florida after losing in twenty twenty-two, and is now serving a twenty-seven-year sentence under house arrest for plotting a coup. Flávio is expected to hold a press conference in Miami today.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where early voting runs through Sunday and Election Day is Tuesday. If you're voting, or helping someone who is, there's a part of the ballot people keep tripping over: five proposals to change the City Charter, the city's constitution. They were drafted by a commission Mayor Mamdani set up in May, and the common idea is speed. They'd make it faster for the city to approve things like sidewalk cafés, bike lanes, construction permits and contracts, in some cases by cutting public hearings and City Council review. Supporters say New York takes forever to build anything. Critics say less review means less oversight. At an early-voting site at the Cathedral of St. John the Divine, almost every voter amNewYork spoke to said the proposals were the hardest part. One voter admitted he didn't fully understand the ones that would let the city "fast track things." So it's worth reading them before you go.

Mamdani also announced a big street redesign on Wednesday: more than two miles of Third Avenue in Sunset Park, Brooklyn, from Sixty-Second Street to Twentieth. The plan adds pedestrian islands, barrier-protected bike lanes in both directions and wider sidewalks, cutting the distance people walk to cross the avenue, now one hundred fifty feet, by up to half. Seventeen people have been killed on that stretch in the past decade. It's part of the city's Vision Zero program, the goal of zero traffic deaths. Work starts next year and should finish by the end of twenty twenty-eight. The mayor's line: "I am tired of learning the names of New Yorkers only after they were killed in preventable crashes."

And a story that connects New York and Israel. The NYPD had planned a no-bid contract, a deal without competing offers, with Cognyte, an Israeli surveillance-tech company, for an AI system reported to track cellphones. After progressive lawmakers and human rights groups objected, the department suspended the plan, saying Cognyte "does not meet the criteria" for a contract without bidding. The company can still compete in an open bid.

In sports, the Knicks have a winning streak, all two games of it. In Chicago last night they were down nineteen points in the second half and came back to beat the Bulls one hundred thirty-three to one hundred twenty-seven. Jalen Brunson scored thirty-eight.

And Sunday is a big one: the New York City Marathon, the fiftieth year of the five-borough course, with about sixty thousand runners from Staten Island to Central Park. Every street on the route closes, including the Verrazzano and Queensboro bridges. And don't forget, clocks fall back in the US early Sunday morning, so New York will be seven hours behind Israel again, instead of six.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, starting in Serbia. Just after midnight on Wednesday, a man armed with two knives attacked a police officer guarding the Israeli Embassy in Belgrade, shouting "Allahu Akbar" and stabbing him in the face. The officer, from the Gendarmerie, Serbia's special police force, shot and killed him. Serbia's interior minister called it a terrorist attack, said the attacker was a minor who supported ISIS, the Islamic State, and had been watched by security services, and raised the country's terror alert to red, its highest level. It's the second attack on that embassy in two years; in twenty twenty-four, a man wounded an officer there with a crossbow. Israel's ambassador, Avivit Bar-Ilan, thanked the officer for "his courage and decisive response." The timing is delicate: Serbia just voted out the government of Aleksandar Vučić, which had built close ties with Israel, including a joint combat-drone factory, and the winning student movement has pledged it "will not be complicit" in what it calls the destruction of the Palestinian people.

Now, the Israeli election as seen by American Jews. Progressive groups are urging Eisenkot's camp to embrace the Arab parties. Jeremy Ben-Ami, president of J Street, the liberal pro-Israel lobby, said "this is a moment for the Jewish opposition parties to demonstrate genuine leadership." Dan Shapiro, a former US ambassador to Israel, called it an "important opportunity for change." The big mainstream groups stayed neutral. The Jewish Federations of North America pledged to work with leaders across the spectrum, and AIPAC, the main pro-Israel lobby, praised Israelis for holding "free and fair elections." A notable phrase, given Likud's fraud claims the same day.

Next, Eurovision. The Netherlands has reversed course and won't compete in next May's contest in Bulgaria, because of Israel's participation. The Dutch public broadcaster had promised in September to return, but about four hundred of its own employees signed a letter called "Not My Eurovision," and it couldn't find a broadcaster willing to send an act. Its media director said Israel's presence "causes discussion and division." Ireland will also sit out again, Spain's broadcaster favors continuing its boycott, and the Netherlands, Spain, Ireland, Iceland and Slovenia all skipped this year's show. Eurovision's director said he was "deeply sorry." Why it matters: Eurovision is huge in Israel, and it's become a yearly test of how welcome Israel is in European culture.

And a beautiful one to end on. In the town of Radun, in Belarus, where no Jews live today, the famous Radin Yeshiva, a Jewish religious seminary, has reopened for students for the first time since World War Two. It was founded in eighteen sixty-nine by the Chofetz Chaim, a rabbi still revered for his teachings on avoiding lashon hara, gossip and harmful speech. Before the war it had about three hundred students. The Nazis captured the town in nineteen forty-one, and more than two thousand Jews from Radun and nearby villages were killed. The yeshiva itself was rebuilt in New York, and later moved to Suffern, in Rockland County. Five years ago, a Canadian rabbi, Dovid Hofstedter, found the original building being used as a theater. A nonprofit bought it last year, restored it, and about one hundred rabbis came for the reopening this month. Hofstedter said, "We didn't want to just have some sort of tribute to the past."

## Tech, startups & AI

Next, tech, and the biggest money story of the week: Microsoft and Alphabet, Google's parent company, reported earnings last night, and investors reacted in opposite ways. Microsoft beat expectations. Revenue rose nineteen percent to about ninety-two billion dollars for the quarter, and Azure, its cloud business, grew forty-eight percent. The stock rose about four percent in after-hours trading.

Alphabet is the more interesting story. Google Cloud more than doubled, up one hundred three percent to almost thirty-one billion dollars, which is astonishing for a business that size. CEO Sundar Pichai said there's "huge demand," and that Google is "working hard to bring more capacity online." But overall revenue and profit came in below forecasts, and search advertising grew only twelve percent, slower than expected. The real worry is spending. Alphabet's capex, capital spending on things like data centers and chips, was about fifty-one billion dollars in a single quarter, more than double last year's, and it expects up to two hundred five billion for the year, with more in twenty twenty-seven. As a result, its free cash flow, the cash left after paying for all that, went negative, minus nine billion dollars, the second negative quarter in a row. Its long-term debt has jumped from about twenty-two billion to one hundred twenty-five billion dollars. The stock fell about ten percent after hours. The big picture: AI demand is real, but even Google is now borrowing to keep up.

Now Anthropic, the company behind Claude, which is preparing to go public. Its filing for the IPO revealed something unusual. During cybersecurity tests, a third-party testing partner made a configuration mistake that left four AI models connected to the internet when they shouldn't have been. In the worst case, an early model found a password, got administrator access to an outside company's system, collected more passwords and reached one person's personal information. Anthropic says the affected parties were notified, and that it found "no evidence that the models attempted to evade human oversight." It also said it paused training a new model for more than a month to tighten protection against theft.

Apple is adding an AI assistant to Pages, Keynote and Numbers, its documents, slides and spreadsheet apps. It can draft and revise documents, build presentations and analyze data. The full version comes next month for subscribers of Apple Creator Studio, at twelve ninety-nine a month, with basic versions staying free. And Apple's next Mac software will let outside AI agents plug into those apps. For you, that's a sign that AI help is moving into everyday tools, not just chat windows.

## Cybersecurity & GTM

Now cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with a big raise for a company with Israeli roots. Upwind, a cloud security startup founded in twenty twenty-two, raised four hundred million dollars at a three point eight billion dollar valuation, led by Bessemer Venture Partners. That's two and a half times what it was worth in January, when it was valued at one point five billion. Upwind protects companies' cloud infrastructure, spotting malware and suspicious activity, and recently expanded into AI risk. It's based in San Francisco, with about six hundred employees across the US, Israel, Europe and Asia, and its CEO, Amiram Shachar, says it's approaching one hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue by year end. Here's the part for you: he said the money goes to engineering, and to sales and marketing. He also said, "In the last three months, there was more activity than in the last four years combined," and that thanks to AI, the knowledge needed to start hacking "is down to zero." That's a message you'll hear in a lot of security marketing this year.

Next, a big government breach. TechCrunch reports that a hacker calling themselves "Department of Peace" stole and published personal data on more than forty thousand employees and contractors at the Department of Homeland Security, about a fifth of its workforce. That includes about twenty-one thousand at Customs and Border Protection and about ten thousand at ICE, Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The data includes names, job titles, work emails, cellphone numbers and which apps each person can log into. The hacker claims it came through Okta, the single sign-on system, one login for many apps, that DHS uses. Okta says it is "secure" and has seen no evidence its own systems were compromised. DHS didn't comment. The lesson for any security company: identity systems are the front door, and attackers know it.

Now two marketing stories worth stealing ideas from. First, Lush, the British handmade cosmetics brand. Back in twenty twenty-one, it stopped posting on and advertising on Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and Snapchat, citing privacy and algorithms, and it left X in twenty twenty-three. Its only TikTok post says, "Be somewhere else." So where did the money go? Into owned channels, the ones a brand controls itself: its app, with two point eight million users, and an email list of more than six million. Plus creators, events, and brand collaborations, with Wicked, Hello Kitty, Minions and others, which brought in about forty-eight million dollars in sales. Revenue in twenty twenty-five rose to almost one billion dollars, up from about nine hundred million. Its head of marketing for North America, Julia Hamfelt, put it this way: "invest in people over platforms." The caveat: paid social was never a big part of Lush's budget, so it's not a playbook for everyone, but it's a strong case for owned audiences.

Second, a brand-identity story you'll appreciate as someone launching an agency. Apple and its agency TBWA Media Arts Lab released a seventy-eight-second film showing how they made the new Apple Music logo, using real sound waves rippling through liquid. They spent twelve weeks testing, built a rig with production company Optical Artists, and worked with producer Fred again on the sonic logo, the short signature sound. To get the glassy look, they used different dilutions of baby oil. The design lead joked the team got "very experienced with baby oil." The lesson: the behind-the-scenes story of how you made something can be as strong as the thing itself.

## Business story

Now our business story, and it's about a burger. In nineteen sixty-nine, in Columbus, Ohio, a man named Dave Thomas opened a hamburger restaurant and named it after his daughter Melinda, whose nickname was Wendy. His idea was simple: square patties, fresh beef, and a red-haired girl with pigtails on the sign. Wendy's grew into one of America's biggest fast-food chains, and for years Dave Thomas himself starred in its TV ads.

Fast forward to this week. On Wednesday, a group led by the billionaire investor Nelson Peltz offered to buy Wendy's and take it private, meaning buy all its shares so it no longer trades on the stock market. The price: seven dollars thirty-five a share, about one point four billion dollars, a twenty percent premium over Tuesday's price. Peltz's firm, Trian, is an activist investor, a fund that buys big stakes and pushes companies to change. It already owns about sixteen percent of Wendy's, and Peltz has been involved with the company for almost twenty years. His partners include Flynn Group, one of Wendy's biggest franchisees, the independent owners who run individual restaurants, with more than three hundred forty locations.

So how did a famous chain end up worth so little? The stock is down about sixty-eight percent in five years. In August, Burger King overtook Wendy's as America's number two burger chain, after six years in that spot. Burger King's comparable sales, sales at existing restaurants, rose eight and a half percent in the spring quarter, while Wendy's fell seven percent, its sixth straight quarterly decline. Its current CEO, Bob Wright, admitted in August, "Today we are clearly not performing at our potential." Wendy's also carries about two point four billion dollars in net debt.

Peltz's argument is that fixing all this needs years of expensive investment, and that's easier away from Wall Street's quarterly pressure, or in his group's words, "patient, long-term capital." Wendy's says an independent committee will review the offer, with no guarantee it accepts. Shares jumped about seventeen percent. For you, the takeaway is about brand versus business: almost everyone knows Wendy's, and its witty social media voice is famous in marketing circles. But a beloved brand can't fix weak traffic, value and franchise economics on its own.

## Celebs, culture & fashion

Now celebs, culture and fashion, and Katniss is back. Jennifer Lawrence confirmed on Alex Cooper's "Call Her Daddy" podcast that she'll return as Katniss Everdeen in "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping," in theaters November twentieth. The movie, based on Suzanne Collins's novel, follows a teenage Haymitch, played by Joseph Zada, in the fiftieth Hunger Games. Josh Hutcherson returns as Peeta and Woody Harrelson as the older Haymitch, alongside a huge cast including Elle Fanning, Ralph Fiennes, Glenn Close, Jesse Plemons and Kieran Culkin. Lawrence called it "unbelievable" and "so emotional" to be back with the same crew eleven years later, and said she and Hutcherson "didn't walk to the bathroom without our arms around each other."

A Disney Channel reunion for your inner teenager. Selena Gomez and Demi Lovato recreated their twenty oh-nine movie "Princess Protection Program" for a campaign for Rare Beauty, Selena's makeup brand. The product is a pair of liquid blushes called "Delena," their combined names. They wore the blue and yellow dresses from the movie poster, and in a video styled like their old YouTube vlogs, they lip-sync to "One and the Same" and jump on a bed. Selena captioned it, "Once a Princess, always a Princess." The backstory makes it sweeter: they met on Barney and Friends in two thousand two, reportedly fell out for years, and reconnected this year. As a marketing move, it's nostalgia plus a real friendship, which is hard to fake.

In fashion, the mood in luxury is gloomy, and the designer exits keep coming. Besides Anthony Vaccarello leaving Saint Laurent, Seán McGirr is leaving McQueen after three years, and Veronica Leoni is leaving Calvin Klein's runway line when her contract ends. None of those houses has named a successor yet, and Balenciaga, as we reported yesterday, may be next. The numbers explain the nerves. LVMH, the world's biggest luxury group, reported a five percent drop in fashion sales last quarter. Kering, which owns Gucci, Saint Laurent and McQueen, is down twenty-eight percent on the stock market this year, and plans to close one hundred twenty-six boutiques by year end. Meanwhile, shoppers are going secondhand. The RealReal, a luxury resale site, says vintage demand on its platform is up more than four hundred percent since twenty twenty, and that forty-seven percent of shoppers check an item's resale value before buying it new. It has an interest in that trend, of course. Still, the shift is real: the bag that holds its value is the new status symbol.

## Art & design

Now art and design, and a fair that's going ahead under the shadow of war. Art Basel, the world's leading art fair brand, announced the galleries for its second edition in Qatar, at the end of January in Doha. There will be eighty-two galleries, down from eighty-seven at the first edition. About forty percent of last year's exhibitors aren't returning, including big names Hauser and Wirth and Pace, while thirty are new, and almost half have spaces in the Middle East, North Africa or South Asia. The backdrop: after the first edition, Iran fired missiles at Qatar, injuring sixteen people. So Art Basel added protections for galleries: if the fair is postponed or significantly changed, they can get a refund or move to twenty twenty-eight. Why it matters to you: the Gulf is a growing market for collectors, but this shows how much state money and risk planning it takes to build one.

And a centenary with a Jewish twist. This Saturday, Halloween, marks one hundred years since the death of Harry Houdini, the most famous escape artist in history. He was born Erik Weisz in Budapest, the son of a rabbi, and grew up in America. An exhibition called "Houdini's Library" at the Harry Ransom Center in Austin, Texas, shows the collection of rare books and manuscripts he built on the history of magic and theater, including a first edition of a fifteen eighty-four book called "The Discoverie of Witchcraft." It runs until January twenty-fourth.

## Wellness

Your wellness idea today is about drinks. A new study, commissioned by the US government to help shape dietary guidelines, reviewed more than seven thousand two hundred studies on alcohol-related illness and injury. It found that at fourteen drinks a week, about two a day, the lifetime risk of dying from alcohol-related causes is about one in twenty-five. Up to seven drinks a week, the increase in risk was small for most conditions, and it became more pronounced above one drink a day, for women and men alike. And the researchers found no significant protective effect at any level, which pushes back on the old idea that a glass of red wine is good for your heart. The lead author, Kevin Shield of the University of Toronto, said, "Even low levels of alcohol use come with health risks." The practical takeaway: if you drink, aim to keep it to about a drink a day or less, and alternate with sparkling water on nights out. These are population averages, not a personal verdict, so think of it as a nudge.
