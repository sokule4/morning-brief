Good morning Sophie, and welcome to your Morning Brief. It's Friday, October ninth. Here are the three big ones. Netanyahu's former military secretary has broken his silence, and claims records from the morning of October seventh were changed. Britain's consulate in Jerusalem is officially out of business, and twenty British diplomats are packing up. And President Trump says America won't strike Iran before its own elections in November. Plus record low shootings in New York, a sixty-seven-million-year-old dinosaur heading to auction, and why your morning coffee might be messing with your sleep. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start with the election campaign, and the biggest political bombshell of the week. Major General Avi Gil was Prime Minister Netanyahu's military secretary on October seventh, the general who connects the prime minister to the army. On Thursday, for the first time, he went public.

His main claim is explosive. Netanyahu gave a fifty-five-page account of that morning to the state comptroller, the official watchdog who audits the government. Gil says that account is, quote, "part of the truth, but not the whole truth." He alleges that Netanyahu's chief of staff, Tzachi Braverman, told a stenographer to change the recorded time of Gil's first phone call with the prime minister that morning, reportedly from six forty to six twenty-nine, the exact minute the attack began. Gil called it part of a "criminal pattern of behavior." To be clear, none of this has been proven.

Netanyahu hit back hard. He said Gil knew about the coming attack, "like others," and "chose not to wake the prime minister up in time." He called the interview "an attempt to obscure the simple and painful truth about his failure."

The opposition jumped on it. Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief now leading the Yashar party, asked the attorney general, the government's top legal officer, to investigate whether Netanyahu misled the comptroller. And he wrote that "nothing will save him from the state commission of inquiry we'll establish after the election." A state commission of inquiry, meaning an independent investigation led by a judge, is the thing Netanyahu has resisted for three years. Avigdor Liberman went even further, calling for an investigation on charges of treason.

Why it matters: the election is in eighteen days, and October seventh accountability is the issue that just won't go away.

And the polls? Honestly, they're all over the place. Yesterday we told you about a poll with Likud and Yashar tied. Two more came out since. An i24 News poll has Likud way ahead at twenty-six seats, and Netanyahu's bloc, his group of allied parties, at sixty, just one short of a majority. But a poll for Zman Yisrael, the Hebrew sister site of the Times of Israel, has the opposite: Yashar first with twenty-four, Likud at twenty, and Netanyahu's bloc at only forty-seven. Same week, very different pictures. The safe takeaway: nobody knows, and every seat in the Knesset, Israel's parliament, is up for grabs.

Next, the showdown with Britain reached its deadline on Thursday, and here's how it ended. Israel had given the British thirty days to close their consulate in East Jerusalem, a consulate being a mini embassy, which has historically served Palestinians. Israel did this in retaliation for British sanctions on settlements, including a ban on settlement imports.

So what happened? A kind of messy half-closure. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar says the consul general and nineteen other diplomats, twenty people in total, are leaving Israel. Seven staff can stay in the building, which Britain owns, with what Saar called "a very limited scope of activity." Saar said: "It is not possible to act against Israel without Israel taking appropriate measures in response."

Britain sees it differently. It has renamed the building the "UK Mission, Jerusalem," and Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband says Britain doesn't accept that Israel has the legal right to close it, and that "we retain our long standing presence." Importantly, Britain says it won't retaliate while talks continue. Worth noting: France and Canada joined those sanctions too, but Israel hasn't gone after them.

Now Iran. The war is in its eighth month, and on Thursday President Trump posted this on Truth Social: "We are having productive discussions with the Islamic Republic of Iran." And: "We will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections," the US congressional elections, on November third.

Here's why that's interesting. Earlier this week, Axios reported that the Pentagon had told its forces to get ready to restart large-scale strikes on Iran, possibly even before Israel's election. And Channel Twelve reported that senior American officers told Israel's army chief, Eyal Zamir, to expect a "massive" attack within three weeks. Zamir reportedly warned that a strike like that, and the Iranian missile fire it would bring down on Israel, could force Israel to postpone its election. So Trump's post may be real diplomacy, or meant to keep Iran guessing.

On the diplomatic track: Trump rejected Iran's latest offer, which would have reopened the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway a fifth of the world's oil passes through, within seven days, but with conditions. Iran's head of atomic energy said enrichment, turning uranium into nuclear fuel or bomb material, is something "we will not abandon in any way." And the US Treasury sanctioned seventeen more ships from Iran's shadow fleet, meaning the hidden tankers Iran uses to sneak oil past sanctions.

Tomorrow marks one year since the Gaza ceasefire began. Quick check on where things stand. Hamas has not disarmed. Israel's army now controls about two-thirds of Gaza, up from about half when the ceasefire began. The Hamas-run health ministry says at least fourteen hundred Palestinians have been killed since the ceasefire, and the army says five Israeli soldiers have been killed. On Thursday, the army said it killed a man who crossed the Yellow Line, the boundary between the Israeli-held and Hamas-held parts of Gaza.

And sad news from the north. An Israeli soldier was killed and three were wounded when their military Humvee overturned in southern Lebanon on Thursday evening.

Quickly around the world. In Ukraine, a Russian glide bomb, a heavy bomb with wings that glides to its target, hit two passenger buses in the city of Kramatorsk on Thursday. At least thirty people were killed. President Zelensky said, "This strike will not go unanswered."

And the Nobels. The Nobel Prize in Literature went to Anne Carson, the Canadian poet, essayist and translator. She's seventy-six, she's a classics scholar, and she's famous for books that mix ancient Greek myths with very modern, playful writing. She's only the nineteenth woman to win the literature prize. And the big one, the Nobel Peace Prize, gets announced today at noon Israel time in Oslo. Trump has openly lobbied for it, saying it would be a "great discredit" if he doesn't win. We'll know by lunch.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, and some genuinely good news on crime.

The NYPD released its numbers for the third quarter, July through September, and they're historic. Murders were down almost twenty percent compared to the same period last year, from ninety-one to seventy-three. Shootings were down almost twenty-three percent. Major crime overall fell more than nine percent. Mayor Mamdani said the city had "the fewest shooting incidents and victims of any September" on record.

But there's a darker number inside the report. Hate crimes are up almost thirteen percent this year. Anti-Jewish hate crimes are at two hundred and forty-five so far this year, about the same as last year. Jews are about ten percent of the city's population, but were targeted in more than half of all hate crimes. Tisch called antisemitic hate crimes the city's "most striking trend." Anti-Muslim incidents also nearly doubled, from eighteen to thirty-two.

Staying with the mayor and the October seventh fallout, there's one new piece. On Thursday, at a press conference in Bushwick, Mamdani stood by how he handled the anniversary. He said he was "proud to attend" the vigil where protesters called him a traitor, and added: "As for those protesting me, I am the mayor of New York City, and this is what I signed up for." He didn't directly answer whether he'd change his statement. And City Council Speaker Julie Menin quietly broke with him, saying October seventh "should focus on the events of October 7, not on the aftermath." She didn't name him, but everyone knew who she meant.

Now, something you'll notice next time you ride the subway. Fare evasion, people jumping the turnstiles, dropped an average of thirty-five percent at twenty stations testing new modern fare gates. At some stations the drop was huge: seventy-two percent at Hoyt Street and at Nostrand Avenue in Brooklyn. The plan is seventy stations by the end of next year, and a hundred and fifty eventually, with one point one billion dollars set aside. And the new gates are wider, which is a big win for wheelchairs and strollers.

A heartbreaking story from Harlem. On Wednesday evening, a fifteen-year-old boy, Jihad Pack, was shot and killed outside the King Towers public housing complex on West a hundred and fifteenth Street, where he lived. A fifty-seven-year-old bystander was hit in the hand by a stray bullet. Police have a person of interest in custody, with charges pending.

Sports. Tonight is do-or-die for the Liberty. They're down two games to none to the Atlanta Dream in their best-of-five series, after losing Game Two in overtime despite thirty-five points from Breanna Stewart. Game Three is tonight at Barclays Center, seven thirty New York time, which is two thirty in the morning in Israel. Lose, and their season is over. One thing in their favor: Atlanta's top scorer, Allisha Gray, is out with a hand injury.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting with a frightening story from Minnesota.

The FBI says it stopped an eighteen-year-old from carrying out a mass shooting at the Mall of America, and that he chose the mall because he believed its owners were Jewish. The suspect, Sheikhdoon Abdullahi Mohamud, from Richfield, Minnesota, allegedly pledged allegiance to ISIS, the Islamist terror group, in a video in September. Prosecutors say he planned to attack on October twenty-fourth, during a Hmong arts festival, and hoped to kill thirty to sixty people. On Tuesday, an undercover FBI agent sold him an AK-47 that didn't actually work, plus ammunition, for six hundred dollars, and he was arrested right after. A plot stopped, but a chilling one.

Next, a more hopeful data point. A big survey from the Jewish Federations of North America found that thirty-eight percent of American Jews say they've become more involved in Jewish life in the three years since October seventh. They call this group "Surgers." Most turned to synagogues, with Chabad second. And these people feel deeply connected to Israel: ninety-three percent of them say they're emotionally attached to it. Interestingly, the top thing that keeps people from participating more is Israeli politics. The federation's Mimi Kravetz said, "These aren't people who are walking away." In other words, the post-October seventh Jewish revival isn't fading, it's become the new normal.

In Europe, the anniversary protests were smaller than in past years, but still tense. In Berlin, about seven hundred people marched, including Greta Thunberg, and more than thirty were arrested. In London, eleven people were arrested, five of them outside the offices of the Board of Deputies, the main body representing British Jews, on suspicion of inciting racial hatred. But heads up: a big march is expected in London tomorrow, with around ten thousand people, and Israel's Diaspora Ministry expects it to be the biggest day of protests this year.

Back in New York, something quietly moving. At the Museum of Jewish Heritage in lower Manhattan, there's a new sculpture called "Rooted." It's a six-foot clear acrylic tree trunk, and visitors drop in the hostage dog tags and yellow pins many of us have worn for years. It was inspired by the hostage posters created by two Israeli artists, Dede Bandaid and Nitzan Mintz, and a photo of little Kfir Bibas was placed inside. Bandaid said, "I don't think as a community or as a country that the circle is closed." But one visitor said, "It feels like a sense of closure." It's on view until October twenty-sixth. If you still have your dog tag, it's a meaningful place to leave it.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting in Israel.

The coolest Israeli AI story this week comes from Amnon Shashua, the co-founder of Mobileye, the self-driving car technology company Intel bought. His newer startup, doubleAI, says its AI system solved part of a math problem that had been open since nineteen eighty-eight. It's about graphs, the network kind, not charts, and the AI found a new construction that beat the previous best result. The proof was checked by a computer verification system, so it's not just the AI's word. And the whole thing cost under two thousand dollars in computing. Shashua calls his approach "artificial expert intelligence": instead of a chatbot that knows a bit about everything, an AI that's truly expert at one hard thing.

Now a story that matters for anyone job hunting. HoneyBook, the business software company for freelancers and small businesses, with a big team in Israel, is laying off about fourteen percent of its staff, around thirty to thirty-five people, most of them in Israel. Here's the interesting part: HoneyBook isn't in trouble. It has hundreds of millions of dollars in the bank. The company said the move is about "aligning our structure and workflows with the AI era," and is "driven by financial strength and strategic focus." Translation: healthy companies are now cutting roles because they believe AI can do more of the work. Smart interview question: how is AI changing the shape of your team?

On the same theme, Oracle. Globally, Oracle has cut more than twenty-one thousand jobs, about thirteen percent of its workforce, including about six thousand in sales and marketing. Where's the money going? Into AI data centers. Oracle plans to spend ninety to ninety-five billion dollars on infrastructure next year. But Calcalist found that Oracle Israel, with about four hundred to five hundred people, hasn't had major layoffs, and is actually hiring, with thirty-three open roles, many in its cloud business. So, a pocket of opportunity right here.

A cautionary market story. NextVision, the Israeli company that makes tiny, powerful cameras for drones, and one of the hottest defense-tech stocks in the country, fell twenty-four percent in two days this week. The trigger: Fidelity, the giant American investor, sold most of its stake, about four hundred and twenty-six million dollars' worth, at a discount. NextVision's market cap, the total value of all its shares, is now about five point seven billion dollars, roughly half its peak in March. And investors noticed that the founders have sold more than half a billion dollars of their own shares since twenty twenty-three. One investor told Calcalist: "Investors don't really know what's going on."

Globally, a few things worth knowing. Google launched a free app for Mac called AI Edge Foresight, which records and summarizes your meetings, even in-person ones, and works completely offline. It's basically a free competitor to Granola, the AI note-taker a lot of sales teams love. If you sell software, note-taking and call-recording tools are becoming free features, which changes the market for them. Manus, the AI agent company, raised more than five hundred million dollars in its first round since splitting from Meta. And Nous Research hit a one point five billion dollar valuation, what investors say the whole company is worth, and launched AI agents, AI tools that take actions on their own, for business users.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM, meaning go-to-market, how companies sell and market.

Let's start with the Israeli round of the week. Rein Security raised twenty-five million dollars in a Series A, a startup's first big institutional funding round, led by Glilot Capital. That brings its total to thirty-five million. Rein protects AI agents while they're actually running, which the industry calls "runtime" security. Imagine an AI shopping assistant on a retailer's website. Rein watches what it does in real time and stops it if a hacker tricks it into doing something bad. The company says revenue is up eight times since it launched in January. Here's the GTM angle: Rein has thirty-one people, with research and development in Tel Aviv and an office in New York. That's the classic Israeli cyber setup. Build in Tel Aviv, sell in America. And with fresh Series A money, the American sales team is usually where the hiring happens.

From Amsterdam, Hadrian raised forty million dollars in a Series B, its second big round. Hadrian does what's called offensive security, meaning it uses AI to attack companies' systems the way a hacker would, to find holes before the bad guys do. Traditionally that's done with a pentest, short for penetration test, a hired team trying to break in. Hadrian says eighty-seven percent of organizations still do those by hand. Their pitch: "AI can work much faster than any human offensive security team." The money goes to expanding in the US and Europe, which again means sales hiring.

Now, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, launched a three-level program for cybersecurity teams that want to use its most powerful AI models for security work. The first level is for defenders, people investigating attacks. The second is for red teams, the ethical hackers who test defenses. And the third is for very sensitive systems, reviewed together with the US government. Anthropic says partners in its earlier program found at least a hundred and twenty-nine thousand verified software vulnerabilities between April and July. Why it matters for your world: the big AI labs are now selling directly to security teams, which means every AI security startup has to explain why it's needed on top of that.

Two breaches that show how attacks really work today. First, ASOS, the British online fashion retailer. Hackers broke into an employee's account by "impersonating a trusted contact." That's social engineering, tricking a person instead of hacking a computer. They then sent a fake push notification to ASOS app users, and got access to customers' names and contact details. ASOS says payment cards and passwords weren't touched. But the stock still fell more than ten percent. That's a powerful sales story: a breach can cost you a tenth of your company's value in a day.

And two GTM moves to know. BlueVoyant, a security services company, hired John Hultman as its new CRO, chief revenue officer, the executive in charge of all sales. He came from Druva and Cohesity. When a company hires a big-name CRO after what it calls a "strong third quarter," it's usually gearing up to grow its sales team. And SecurityWeek counted thirty-nine cybersecurity acquisitions in September, including Upwind buying the Israeli AI security startup Aegis for a reported thirty million dollars in stock.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: At-Bay, the Israeli-founded cyber insurance company that went from a billion-dollar unicorn to a much smaller sale, and why its founder isn't sorry.

It starts in twenty thirteen, with the massive hack of Target, the American retailer. More than a hundred million customers were exposed, and over eighty million credit cards stolen. An Israeli named Rotem Iram was working on the fallout, and had an idea. Companies were buying insurance against cyber attacks, but the insurers didn't actually understand cyber risk. What if you built an insurance company that did?

In twenty sixteen, Iram and Roman Itskovich, who met at the consulting firm McKinsey, founded At-Bay in Palo Alto, with a development team in Israel. The twist was the business model. At-Bay wouldn't just sell you a policy and wait for you to get hacked. It would scan your systems, warn you about weaknesses, and help you fix them, because a customer who doesn't get hacked is a customer who doesn't cost you money.

At first, it was a hard sell. In twenty nineteen, At-Bay sold about one million dollars of insurance. In twenty twenty, about twenty-five million. By March twenty twenty-one, it was on track for seventy million a year, ten times the year before. That's a run rate, meaning what you'd earn in a year at your current pace. And in mid-twenty twenty-one, At-Bay raised money at a one point three five billion dollar valuation. A unicorn, meaning a startup worth over a billion dollars. In total, it raised three hundred and eight million dollars.

Then the world changed. Iram puts it perfectly: "We founded the company during a period when the cost of capital was zero." Then, "Interest rates jumped from zero to five percent, and the funding tap was turned off." Insurance businesses need a lot of money to grow, so this hit hard.

At-Bay kept going, and in July this year, it had its first profitable month. But in August, it agreed to be bought by Munich Re, one of the biggest insurance companies in the world, for five hundred and seventy-five million dollars. That's less than half its peak valuation.

So is that a failure? Iram doesn't think so. Today At-Bay has about forty thousand customers, and a quarter of them also pay for its security services. Munich Re has committed to growing the Israeli development center, and plans to start selling At-Bay's insurance in Israel. In his words: "So, we had to accept reality as it was and move toward a sale." And: "In a startup, you're building a ship, but you can't control the ocean waves."

He also has a warning for the industry: "The AI storm could easily topple smaller players," because AI will dramatically increase the number of attacks.

Two lessons for you. One, bundling is a powerful GTM strategy. At-Bay didn't just sell insurance, it sold insurance plus protection, which made it different from every other insurer. And two, a lot of the companies valued as unicorns in twenty twenty-one are now being sold for less. That's not always failure. Sometimes it's the smart move. And when a giant like Munich Re buys an Israeli company and promises to expand here, that's a hiring signal.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, and this one is fun.

Sotheby's is selling a dinosaur. Her name is Jodi, and she's a Nanotyrannus, a smaller cousin of the T. rex. She's about sixty-seven million years old, about two meters tall and six meters long. A Montana rancher named Jodi Pierson found her in twenty nineteen, which is where the name comes from. She's one of only three Nanotyrannus skeletons ever found, and the first ever offered for sale. The estimate is six to nine million dollars, at a special auction in New York on October twenty-seventh, and she goes on view at Sotheby's new Breuer building, the old Whitney museum, on October thirteenth. Here's the scientific drama: for decades, scientists argued whether Nanotyrannus was its own species or just a teenage T. rex. Last year, a study concluded it's a separate genus, a distinct group of animals. Sotheby's expert said, "this debate has been settled."

Next, a report you'll want to know about as someone starting an art agency. The Art Basel and UBS survey of collectors came out on Thursday. They surveyed thirty-one hundred high-net-worth collectors, meaning very wealthy people, and the big finding: Gen Z spent more on art than any other generation, more than twice as much as older collectors. And Gen Z made up nearly half of buyers of works over a million dollars this year. A UBS economist said the Great Wealth Transfer, trillions of dollars passing from boomers to their kids, "is well underway." And twenty-two percent now use apps or AI tools to research art, up from four percent two years ago. And the youngest collectors are the most private. The lesson for your agency: young buyers do their homework, they find art online, and they don't want to be loud about it.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today. A new review of thirty-two sleep studies found that even a hundred milligrams of caffeine, about one cup of coffee, can reduce your deep sleep, the most restful stage, called slow-wave sleep. In one study, a big dose six hours before bed cut deep sleep by almost a third. And in a small study, even coffee drunk right after waking up still cut total sleep that night. Some people are just more sensitive, partly because of their genes. So the practical idea: have your coffee early, and if you're sleeping badly, try a week with one cup less and see how you feel.
