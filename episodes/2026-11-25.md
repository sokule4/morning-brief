## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Wednesday, November twenty-fifth, and this is your Morning Brief.

Here are today's three biggest stories. Avigdor Liberman signed the first coalition deal with Gadi Eisenkot, and on the same night Yair Golan's party walked out of talks. Nvidia brought back Jensen Huang as CEO, and its stock had its best day in more than twenty years. And in Yemen, the Saudi-led coalition told civilians to leave the port of Hodeidah, and the Houthis are threatening Saudi ports in return.

## Israel

We start here at home, where Tuesday night brought good news and bad news for Gadi Eisenkot, all within a few hours.

The good news first. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, said that he met Avigdor Liberman, the leader of Yisrael Beytenu, Israel Is Our Home, on Tuesday, and that the two agreed to sign a coalition agreement. Later that night Yashar said the deal had been signed. Reuters calls it the first such alliance since the October election. That matters because Liberman has been the hardest partner for Eisenkot to pin down. He brings seven seats, and his main goal, he has said, is to remove Netanyahu. But he also has red lines on both sides. He opposes a government that relies on the Arab parties, and he opposes a unity government with Netanyahu. So getting his signature locks in one piece of the puzzle.

Now the bad news. At almost the same time, Yair Golan, who leads the Democrats, the left-wing party with eight seats, announced he was halting coalition talks with Yashar. According to Reuters, his reason is that Likud and Yashar have been making progress toward a unity government, while talks on what's called a change government, a government without Netanyahu's bloc, are stuck. Golan's own preference is the opposite of Liberman's: a government without Netanyahu that leans on the Arab parties.

So you can see Eisenkot's problem. His two partners want opposite things. Jewish Insider describes three tracks running at the same time. Track one is a unity government with Netanyahu, which the outlet calls the least likely. Track two is a change government that includes the ultra-Orthodox parties, the road Liberman is pushing. And track three is a minority government, one without a majority of its own, that survives because the Arab parties don't vote against it. Eisenkot reportedly sees that as his most likely fallback.

The ultra-Orthodox piece moved too. The Jerusalem Post reports that Yaakov Asher of United Torah Judaism got the green light from his rabbis to keep talking with Eisenkot. Reports say Eisenkot's proposed draft law would drop enlistment quotas and keep exemptions for yeshiva students, the men who study religious texts full time. Netanyahu, meanwhile, reportedly failed in his attempts to hold talks with United Torah Judaism lawmakers, according to Times of Israel. And Bezalel Smotrich, the far-right finance minister, warned the Haredi parties against joining any government Eisenkot leads.

Likud and Yashar are set to meet again today. What's next: the clock. Eisenkot's mandate runs out around December sixth, and the president can add fourteen more days.

The new Knesset is also already acting like it's in charge. On Tuesday, the Arrangements Committee, which organizes the Knesset's work before a government exists, voted thirteen to nine to fast-track a bill by Orit Farkash-Hacohen to set up a state commission of inquiry into October seventh. That's the most powerful kind of investigation in Israel, led by a judge. Netanyahu's government has long resisted one.

On security and society, two stories about settler violence in the West Bank. The Shin Bet reportedly criticized Defense Minister Israel Katz for refusing to use administrative detention, holding someone without charges or trial, against violent settler extremists. Katz stopped using that tool against settlers in twenty twenty-four. And Supreme Court President Isaac Amit condemned the recent attacks and ordered the state to send the court a report by December third.

One sobering number, from the Defense Ministry: more than thirty-one thousand five hundred wounded soldiers and security personnel have asked for treatment since October seventh. About sixty-two percent, around nineteen thousand six hundred people, are dealing with psychological injuries.

And from Washington, a reality check on aid. Mike Huckabee, the US ambassador to Israel, said he's "not sure" American military aid can stop suddenly by the end of this year, despite what President Trump promised on Election Day. The current ten-year aid agreement runs through twenty twenty-eight, and Huckabee said it can't happen "unless Congress were to act."

## World

Now to the world, and the Iran war's front line in the Red Sea. The Saudi-led coalition that fights the Houthis in Yemen ordered civilians to evacuate the port of Hodeidah "for their safety." Hodeidah is the Houthis' main port and the entry point for much of Yemen's food and fuel, so an evacuation order usually means strikes are coming. The Houthis answered fast. Their military spokesman, Yahya Saree, warned that if Hodeidah is attacked, they'll target Jeddah, Saudi Arabia's biggest Red Sea port, and other Saudi ports. The Saudi-led coalition also reported what it called a "terrorist attack" on Tuesday on the tower of the Public Investment Fund, Saudi Arabia's state investment giant, in Riyadh's financial district. The same tower was hit by Houthi cruise missiles last week.

Why does this matter? Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is already crippled by the war, and the Red Sea is the other big route for oil and goods between Asia and Europe. If Saudi Red Sea ports become targets, the world loses its backup route.

Iran's message is getting broader too. The head of the Revolutionary Guard, Major General Ahmad Vahidi, warned that future wars "could extend beyond the region," and he urged Iran's neighbors to rethink their security ties with the US.

On the diplomacy side, Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio each met Qatar's prime minister at the White House on Monday. Qatar is still the main go-between with Iran. According to the 10 Things news briefing, Netanyahu has been urging Trump to approve new strikes on Iran, but US officials say any action is delayed for at least a week, and Trump would prefer to wait until after New Year. Huckabee added that Trump is open to renewed military action if Iran doesn't meet American demands. And the Wall Street Journal reports the US is investigating Iran as the prime suspect in cyberattacks on as many as fifty oil-carrying ships.

In Ukraine, an overnight Russian attack on Zaporizhzhia, in the southeast, hit an apartment building, killing two people and wounding forty, including four children. And Western allies are warning that Russia could launch a nuclear-armed satellite as early as December. The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, dismissed the reports. Next door, Estonia plans to close its land border crossings with Russia from December first for at least six months, and Moscow accused it of hostile acts that risk "open war."

And in US politics, a few headlines. President Trump said he'll sign a directive telling federal prosecutors to seek the death penalty in child murders and some other killings. Democratic Congressman Jimmy Gomez of California said he will resign next week, after the House Ethics Committee, the panel that investigates lawmakers' behavior, found "substantial evidence" of unwelcome sexual conduct. And a federal appeals court is letting Trump try again to move his twenty twenty-four Manhattan hush money conviction into federal court.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where everyone is looking at the sky before tomorrow. It's the one hundredth Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, and there's a high wind watch, a warning that dangerous winds are possible, for the whole city from ten in the morning Thursday through late at night. NBC New York's forecasters expect gusts of forty to fifty miles an hour, and maybe up to sixty later in the day, plus heavy rain.

Here's the rule that decides the balloons' fate. Since nineteen ninety-seven, when a windblown balloon hit a lamppost and badly hurt a spectator, the big balloons can't fly at full height when sustained winds pass about twenty-three miles an hour or gusts pass about thirty-four. The police make the call on the day. The parade starts at eight thirty and ends around twelve thirty, and the strongest winds are expected in the afternoon, so it's going to be close. The balloons could fly low, or be tied to trucks. Only once, in nineteen seventy-one, have all the balloons been grounded by wind. The parade itself is very unlikely to be canceled.

And the mayor will be watching on crutches. The Washington Post reports Mayor Mamdani will have surgery on Friday for the torn Achilles tendon he got playing soccer against the press corps last week.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, starting in Manhattan. Three New York City Council members, led by Eric Dinowitz, who chairs the Council's Jewish Caucus, are asking CUNY, the City University of New York, to open a Title VI investigation at Hunter College. Title VI is the civil rights law that bans discrimination at schools that get federal money. The student government canceled a Cultural Arts Night on November fourteenth, two days before it was supposed to happen. Hillel, the Jewish campus organization, was one of the early co-sponsors. Hunter's president, Nancy Cantor, said the official reason was that too few groups signed on as co-sponsors, but she added the college is "deeply concerned" by reports that some groups pulled out because Hillel was taking part. Hunter says it's investigating.

And in the literary world, a split. Ta-Nehisi Coates, Viet Thanh Nguyen and more than a hundred other writers and artists launched a new group called PEN Writes US. It wants to become an official PEN center, part of the global writers' free-speech network, and unlike PEN America, it supports cultural boycotts of Israel. Its website also criticizes the IHRA definition, the widely used definition of antisemitism. PEN America says it defends writers' right to join boycotts, but doesn't endorse them. The fight goes back to last summer, when PEN America's president resigned after the group published a report on the growing exclusion of Jewish and Israeli writers.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a comeback almost nobody predicted. On Monday, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to bring back Jensen Huang as president and CEO, just eleven days after he resigned over a disclosure about his personal conduct. Last week the board had said he would not return. What changed? Reuters reports that President Trump and Vice President Vance got involved, because they see Huang as essential to America's AI plans. Trump said he "convinced" the board.

Wall Street loved it. Nvidia's stock jumped about thirty-one percent in a single day, its best day since twenty oh three, according to Bloomberg, and it pulled the whole market up: the S&P five hundred rose four point seven percent. Remember, just last Friday, AI stocks had crashed. The board says it's confident his return poses no national security risk. It also plans a new committee on national security and export controls, the rules on which countries can buy advanced chips, and a chief compliance officer, the executive who makes sure a company follows the law. Independent board members are still investigating what led to the resignation. Not everyone is cheering: Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said the "rush to reinstate" Huang shows Congress needs to act. Huang is due to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December third.

For Israel this is a big deal. Nvidia is one of the biggest tech employers here, and Globes estimates its Israel-based revenue is now around twenty-one billion dollars a year.

## Business story

Our business story is about OpenAI, and the question every investor is asking now that Anthropic is public: when will OpenAI sell shares to the public too? The Wall Street Journal reported Tuesday that the longer OpenAI waits, the more exposed it is to an AI downturn. The plan right now is to wait until next year. Jay Ritter, a University of Florida professor who studies IPOs, put it bluntly: "I don't see a compelling reason to wait more than a couple months."

The numbers show why the timing is tricky. According to The Information, OpenAI's quarterly revenue rose about fifty percent to around ten billion dollars. But it still loses a lot of money, and it plans to spend about seven hundred fifty billion dollars on computing power through twenty thirty. Only about eight and a half percent of ChatGPT's weekly users pay.

So OpenAI is also hunting for new customers in unexpected places. On Tuesday it launched a pilot called OpenWorks in twenty American cities, fifteen of them in the Midwest or the South. It pays local people who know AI, up to eight thousand dollars a month, to help small businesses, like independent restaurants, shops and small factories, use AI for one real task. The businesses get the help free, plus three months of ChatGPT Business. As a marketer, notice the playbook: instead of ads, OpenAI is paying local guides to build trust door to door, in places where AI still feels like a coastal thing.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a reason to care about blood sugar that goes beyond weight. A major review of forty studies, reported this week by ScienceDaily, found that people with type two diabetes had a fifty-three percent higher risk of Alzheimer's disease. The researchers estimate that close to five in every thousand people with type two diabetes develop Alzheimer's each year. A review like this shows a link, not proof that one causes the other. But it's one more sign that what's good for your metabolism is also good for your brain. With holiday meals coming, the easy habits still count: vegetables and protein first, and a short walk after a big meal.

## Funding and acquisitions

After Monday's giant deal, today's money stories are smaller, and both are Israeli.

First, a fresh seed round. Dante Security, an Israeli startup less than a year old, raised a ten million dollar seed round led by NFX, the venture fund with deep roots in Israel, with Alpha Drive, Inovia and private investors joining. What does it do? Companies are giving AI agents access to their systems: email, files, customer data. Dante watches what those agents actually do with that access, from start to finish, and ties every action back to the employee responsible for that agent. The founders come from Imperva, the data security company. CEO Moshe Lipsker ran Imperva's Israeli operation and was its global head of R and D. Gabi Beyo worked in Imperva's innovation group, and Shai Feinberg is a serial entrepreneur. Lipsker and Feinberg served together in Sayeret Golani, an elite army unit. Lipsker's pitch is simple: agents "can still be influenced by malicious instructions," and old security tools can't always tell if an agent's action is legitimate. Is ten million big? It's a healthy seed, typical for a well-known founding team in Israel's crowded AI security space.

Second, in case you missed it, a bigger Israeli seed from earlier this month that we haven't covered. Dreeze, a Tel Aviv company, came out of stealth with thirty-three million dollars, led by Norwest and Bessemer Venture Partners, with Claltech joining. Thirty-three million is a very large seed, and the reason is the founders: Ziv Kedem, Oded Kedem and Avi Raichel founded Zerto, which Hewlett Packard Enterprise bought in twenty twenty-one, and before that Kashya, which EMC bought. Dreeze rebuilds entire cloud applications after a cyberattack or outage, not just the data, but the settings, permissions and connections that make an app actually work. It already has about seventy people in Israel and the US, works on Amazon's cloud today, and plans full support for Microsoft's Azure in early twenty twenty-seven.

And an update on CrowdStrike and Zscaler. The Daily Upside adds two useful details. The companies already share about two thousand customers through an existing partnership. And CrowdStrike says the deal could expand its total addressable market, the total amount customers could spend on what it sells, by one hundred eighty-five billion dollars. SecurityWeek notes the combined company would have more than ten billion dollars in annual recurring revenue.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, partnerships are often the trial run for an acquisition. CrowdStrike and Zscaler spent years co-selling, meaning selling together to the same customers, and building integrations. Those two thousand shared customers became the proof that the products fit. William Blair's analyst Jonathan Ho framed the result in one line: one of the few security vendors able to compete with Palo Alto Networks and Microsoft for consolidated spending. Notice the framing again: not what the product does, but who it can now fight. For a marketer, partner programs aren't just a lead source. They're where the next big story starts.

Second, Dante's positioning is about accountability, not fear. Lots of AI agent security startups sell on danger: rogue agents, attacks, leaks. Dante's line, tying every agent action to a responsible person, speaks to the executive who has to answer to auditors and regulators. That's a smart way to stand out in a crowded category: sell to the person who gets blamed.

Third, Dreeze is a lesson in naming. Instead of competing in backup, a crowded and boring category, it calls what it does "Application Revival," with four pillars: mapping how an app's parts depend on each other, protected backups, automated rebuilding, and proof that the rebuild worked. That's category creation, defining a new category so you lead it from day one. It works when buyers feel the pain, and anyone who has lived through a big outage this year knows that recovering the data is not the same as getting the app back.
