## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Friday, October twenty-third, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. Israel's final polls are out before the legal blackout, and the two camps are basically tied, four days before Tuesday's vote. In the Gulf, Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it hit seven ships, oil jumped to its highest price since May, and Trump says Iran is faking it. And Art Basel Paris shuts its doors two days early tonight, as France braces for another weekend of protests. Plus a Roman marble that sold for thirty-four million dollars, OpenAI's new trillion-dollar fundraise, and the rise and fall of an Israeli drone stock.

## Headlines

We start with Israel's election, because today is a milestone. Israeli law bans publishing new polls from Friday until election day, so the numbers that came out Thursday are the last ones you'll see before Tuesday.

And the picture is a dead heat. The final Times of Israel poll gives each camp fifty-one seats, ten short of the sixty-one needed for a majority in the Knesset. Eisenkot's Yashar party gets twenty-three seats, down three from the previous week, and Likud gets twenty-one, up one. Yoaz Hendel's Reservists party squeaks in with four seats, while Ofer Winter's People of Israel falls just below the electoral threshold. Jewish Insider averaged the mainstream TV and newspaper polls from the last two days and found Eisenkot's camp at fifty-two and Netanyahu's at fifty. But two right-leaning outlets, Channel fourteen and i24 News, whose pollsters previously worked for Likud, give Netanyahu's coalition exactly sixty-one. So depending on whose numbers you trust, Netanyahu is either stuck or just barely over the line. One more number worth knowing: about sixty percent in the Times of Israel poll said Netanyahu should retire from politics if he fails to form a government.

Netanyahu himself told the Jerusalem Post he sees "an even tie." Asked if this would be his last term, he joked, "Oh no, I'm gonna have five more terms." He laid out his first principle for any government he forms: "no Palestinian state, period." And he asked voters for more time to finish the job against Iran.

The campaign is getting nastier in its final days. Defense Minister Israel Katz said that if there's an attack in the coming days, it will be the fault of opposition leaders Lapid, Bennett and Golan, because they mocked warnings about attack plots. Security sources have said no specific threat was identified. Lapid called Katz's statement a "gift to Israel's enemies." Eisenkot answered: "Our army is strong, but our leadership is rotten and dangerous." And the Shin Bet said it worked with prosecutors and the Central Elections Committee, the body that runs the vote, to take down a batch of social media accounts tied to a "foreign influence campaign," meaning a foreign government secretly trying to sway Israeli voters. It didn't say which country.

One more moment that tells you about the mood. On Thursday, Israel marked thirty-one years since the assassination of Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin, according to the Hebrew calendar. President Herzog pleaded for calm, saying "Words kill," and he vowed that Rabin's killer, Yigal Amir, will never be released, despite some far-right calls for clemency. Netanyahu skipped the state ceremony, as he has every year since twenty nineteen.

Now Iran and the Gulf, where things heated up again. Iran's Revolutionary Guard says its navy hit four tankers in the northern Persian Gulf that were doing ship-to-ship transfers, meaning moving oil from one tanker to another at sea, a classic trick to hide where oil comes from. It says the ships caught fire, and that three more vessels hit mines west of the Strait of Hormuz. It also warned that if American warships keep escorting ships through the strait, "the conflict will extend to its bases in the region." The market reacted fast. Brent crude touched a hundred and twelve dollars a barrel on Thursday, its highest since May.

Trump's response was unusual. On Truth Social, his social network, he accused Iran of "making up fake hits" and mines to push oil prices up before America's midterms, and called it "truly an evil regime." He also said the US is "not negotiating with" Iran and that "something one way or another will happen, fairly soon," though he added most people expect it after the election.

Some context. The US Navy has now been blockading Iran's ports for a hundred days in this second round, since July fourteenth. Central Command, the US military headquarters for the Middle East, says it has blocked about twenty-five billion dollars of Iranian oil exports and turned back a hundred and thirty-eight ships. The data firm Kpler says Iran's shadow fleet, its old tankers that sail with hidden ownership to dodge sanctions, actually ran out of oil to deliver in mid-October. As one analyst put it, there's "no more oil to discharge to China." That squeeze helps explain why Iran is lashing out at sea. Meanwhile in Israel, the army chief, Eyal Zamir, reportedly sees no sign that a strike on Iran is needed before Tuesday's election, though the army is ready if it is. And today in Istanbul, the foreign ministers of Britain, France and Germany meet their counterparts from Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Egypt to look for a way out.

The Houthi war on Saudi Arabia also got deadlier. The death toll from the attack on Dammam's airport rose to two, a Saudi citizen and an Indian resident, with eight injured. The US State Department has ordered all non-emergency American government staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia. And overnight, the Saudi-led coalition says it shot down Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and at Najran, a city near the Yemen border.

A few more from home. Ultra-Orthodox protesters threw rocks at police near Elad Junction after the overnight arrest of a man who dodged the draft. One officer was hit in the head. In the West Bank, Israel released almost all of the ten suspects arrested over last week's settler attacks to house arrest, including the attack that injured the American photojournalist Heidi Levine. On Thursday night police also detained eleven foreign activists during another olive harvest in the south Hebron hills and sent them to a deportation hearing. And on Gaza, Haaretz reports Israeli officials told Washington that Hamas has no plans to disarm, and that it has thirty to forty thousand fighters and has repaired its tunnels.

Now France, which needs a proper explanation because it's spilling into everything, including the art world. The trigger was small. Last month, students at a high school in Créteil, a suburb southeast of Paris, protested over staff shortages and crumbling buildings. The protests spread to other suburbs, then nationwide, and turned into a wider revolt over the cost of living. Last Saturday more than half a million people marched across France, and in some places it became riots and looting, with more than two hundred shops looted. The justice minister says at least twenty-seven hundred people have been taken into custody. The protesters say schools in poorer areas are neglected and life is too expensive. President Macron says the riots were "seditious acts" by "ultra-left" groups trying to "bring down the Republic," and he's banning what he calls "illegitimate" protests. Paris, Lyon, Lille and Grenoble restricted protests, all protests are banned Saturday in the Marseille region, and the Louvre, the Orsay and the Picasso museum will be closed Saturday. Unions are planning a national day of action on November fifth, so this isn't over.

Quickly around the world. The UAE nominated Lana Nusseibeh, a minister and its former UN ambassador, to be the next UN Secretary-General, after six failed votes in the Security Council. Nigeria reported its first Ebola case in more than a decade, making it the fourth African country in an outbreak that has killed more than forty-six hundred people.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the horse carriages lost.

On Thursday, the City Council voted forty to seven to end Central Park's horse carriage industry by June twenty twenty-eight. That's a veto-proof majority, meaning enough votes to override the mayor if he said no, though Mamdani supports the ban. It's called Romanch's Law, after Romanch Mahajan, an eighteen-year-old tourist who died after being thrown from a carriage in June. For the roughly two hundred drivers and owners, the city must design a transition program by April twenty twenty-seven, with job referrals to places like the MTA and the Taxi and Limousine Commission, plus training. The city only has to consider paying medallion owners. A medallion is the license to run a carriage, and owners paid a lot for them. The drivers' union, TWU Local one hundred, says the bill has "no protections for the workers," and lawyers for the owners say they'll sue, arguing the city is taking their property without fair payment.

Big civic reminder: early voting, meaning voting in person before Election Day, starts tomorrow, Saturday, and runs through November first, and Election Day is November third. This is the midterms, plus the governor's race. Kathy Hochul faces Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive. In polls last month, Hochul led by nine points in the Siena poll and nineteen in Quinnipiac. Don't skip the back of the ballot. New York City has five ballot proposals, which change the City Charter, the city's constitution. They would speed up permits for things like outdoor dining and bike lanes, create one online hub for construction permits, and set a twelve percent target for the city's rainy day fund, its emergency savings account.

On the subway and buses, the MTA has pulled its so-called Eagle teams off the street. These are civilian units, not police, that catch fare evaders, people who ride without paying, on city buses. Video showed team members getting physical with riders, and in one case a Brooklyn school dean was punched and lost consciousness. MTA chief Janno Lieber said he's "concerned about the pattern," and the teams won't return until training is overhauled.

And a follow-up on Brad Lander, who was detained by Israeli soldiers at a West Bank olive harvest. The Jerusalem Post says it saw a letter requesting coordination for a trip, which complicates the army's claim that the group never coordinated, and Defense Ministry maps reportedly showed the area didn't require coordination at all. Lander says that if elected, he'll sign on to a bill from Texas Congressman Joaquin Castro that would sanction people involved in expanding settlements and ban imports of settlement goods. He's flying home to vote early on Saturday.

## Jewish world

Next, the Jewish world, starting with a scary moment in Midtown.

On Wednesday at about ten to six in the evening, a twenty-six-year-old Orthodox Jewish man from Crown Heights was stabbed in the back on Forty-Seventh Street near Fifth Avenue, in the Diamond District. He told police he thinks it may have been a needle or a syringe. He's in stable condition at Bellevue. There's no arrest yet, and the NYPD's Hate Crimes Task Force is investigating it as a possible antisemitic attack. For context, Jewish New Yorkers have been the target of more than half of the city's hate crimes this year, while being about twelve percent of the population.

Up in Riverdale, the heavily Jewish neighborhood in the Bronx, an anti-Zionist group, meaning opposed to Israel as a Jewish state, called Pal-Awda announced a protest at an Israeli real estate event, the kind of event where people buy property in Israel. A group of local officials, including Congressman Ritchie Torres and Bronx Borough President Vanessa Gibson, signed a letter saying they "will not tolerate" it. Earlier protests by the same group at similar events, including outside synagogues, were one reason New York pushed for rules on protests at houses of worship.

In Michigan, the final Senate debate turned partly on Jewish voters. Republican Mike Rogers said some Jewish Democrats are afraid of his opponent, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed, saying "Jewish Democrats who are afraid for their children" are "real." El-Sayed called Rogers "an AIPAC-backed politician" and cited a New York Times report that AIPAC planned up to nine million dollars for a super PAC, a group that can spend unlimited money, backing Rogers. El-Sayed has hired a veteran Jewish outreach director, but a coalition of rabbis and Jewish politicians called Nonnegotiable Michigan says he's "making it painfully difficult" for many in their community to vote for him. It's a tight race, and the vote is November third.

Here's a thoughtful piece from JTA about Israel's election. It points out that peace with the Palestinians has almost disappeared from the campaign. The big fights are about security, the judicial overhaul, the government's push to change the courts' powers, and ultra-Orthodox draft exemptions. A Tel Aviv University survey from July found only twenty-nine percent of Jewish Israelis support any negotiations with the Palestinian Authority, the Palestinian government in parts of the West Bank. That's seven percent on the right and eighty-three percent on the left. The opposition is mostly center-right, and even Yair Golan, whose Democrats party is the most open to the issue, has avoided it on the campaign trail. One political scientist argues the issue "cannot be ignored for a long period of time."

And in Jewish pop culture: season three of Netflix's "Nobody Wants This" dropped Thursday. It's the hit romcom where Adam Brody plays a rabbi who falls for Kristen Bell's non-Jewish podcaster. The new season comes with a cloud. A few weeks ago Brody, who is Jewish, told GQ about the war in Gaza, "Put me down as thinking it's a genocide. Put me down as wanting a free Palestine." Rabbi Steve Leder, who consulted on season one, called the remarks "truly shocking" and called Brody a "relatively ignorant Jew." Some viewers say they're boycotting. But a JTA reviewer who watched anyway says the season is stronger than expected, with Sarah Silverman as a new rabbi, full Hebrew blessings on screen, and scenes filmed at LA's Sinai Temple. Her conclusion: whether it's "kosher to watch" is your call.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, where the numbers keep getting bigger.

OpenAI is reportedly working on a new raise of up to thirty billion dollars at a one point two five trillion dollar pre-money valuation, pre-money meaning the value before the new cash comes in. CNBC reports the investment firm Coatue would lead, with investors from the UAE, possibly tied to a deal for more computing power there. For scale, OpenAI was valued at eight hundred and fifty-two billion in March. It would become the most valuable private company ever. Both OpenAI and Anthropic filed confidential IPO paperwork in June, and Anthropic is reportedly aiming for a value of one and a half to two trillion dollars when it goes public. So the race now is partly about who lists first, and at what price.

On the other side of the world, the Chinese AI lab DeepSeek has reportedly raised about fifteen billion dollars, one of the biggest rounds in China's history. DeepSeek shocked the industry in early twenty twenty-five with a free open-weight model, meaning anyone can download and run it. The timing is pointed. Two days earlier, the US ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek's technology from their systems over security concerns. Its founder, Liang Wenfeng, who started a quant hedge fund, one that trades using math models, before AI, reportedly still owns more than eighty percent of the company.

Now the Israeli angle. Bill Ackman, the billionaire hedge fund manager who runs Pershing Square, spoke Thursday at Sheba Medical Center's ARC summit in Tel Aviv. He predicted Israel will produce a company worth a trillion dollars within a decade, and said he'd be surprised if it took more than five years. He and his wife, the designer Neri Oxman, own five percent of the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange.

Ackman is also putting money where his mouth is. Together with former Google chairman Eric Schmidt, he's backing a secretive Israeli AI startup called Two Delta, founded last year by Dvir Zeltzer, who held a senior role at Google, and the veteran AI researcher Eli David. It raised tens of millions in a seed round. Two Delta works on making AI models run faster and cheaper, using a technique called speculative decoding, where a small, fast model drafts the next words and a big model just checks them. It claims about double the speed of the standard method.

And a big legal one. A judge in New Mexico ordered Meta to pay twenty-five billion dollars, its largest penalty ever, after a jury found Facebook misled users about privacy for years. It goes back to the Cambridge Analytica scandal, when a personality quiz harvested data from about eighty-seven million profiles that was used for political ads. For comparison, the FTC's famous fine against Facebook in twenty nineteen was five billion. Meta says penalties this size are unconstitutional, so expect an appeal. For a marketer, it's a reminder that how a company talks about privacy is now a legal risk, not just a brand message.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with an update on the PHP flaw we told you about yesterday.

It's nicknamed PHP2Shell, and it affects PHP, the language behind WordPress and a huge share of the web. The flaw allows remote code execution, attackers running their own code on a server. The new detail is how it was found. An AI security firm called Calif found the first piece on August first using OpenAI's newest model, and Wiz, the Google-owned Israeli cloud security company, found it independently using AI tools too. The bug had been hiding in PHP for about fourteen years. Wiz said, "It marks an AI inflection point." Now the bad news. Attack attempts started even before the official fix, as early as October seventeenth. Cloudflare saw tens of thousands of attempts within forty-five minutes of the news, and millions by Wednesday evening. Amazon Web Services says it's now "being broadly exploited in the wild." Palo Alto Networks counted twenty-two point six million potentially vulnerable servers, and parts of Wikipedia, the BBC and some government sites went offline during emergency patching. So if you didn't ask your web team yesterday whether your company site is patched, ask today.

Now money and a twist. Upwind is an Israeli cloud security startup founded in twenty twenty-two by Amiram Shachar, with customers like Roku and the Brazilian bank Nubank. The Information reported that Datadog, the American monitoring software giant worth about ninety billion dollars, approached Upwind about buying it for around three point eight billion. Calcalist says it has learned that no offer was actually made. Either way, the numbers are remarkable. Upwind is finalizing a three-hundred-million-dollar round at a three point eight billion dollar valuation, more than double its value in January. Its annual recurring revenue, the yearly subscription income it can count on, reportedly passed fifty million dollars, up from about twenty million at the end of last year. When a startup more than doubles in under a year, you get acquisition rumors, and you get hiring.

Now the big marketing story of the week. OpenAI expanded ChatGPT ads to twenty-six more countries, so they now run in eighty-nine. More than a hundred thousand advertisers have run campaigns, up from about twenty thousand in late August, and the ad business hit a one-billion-dollar annual run rate in August, up from a hundred million in March. A run rate is current revenue projected over a full year. Ads only show to free users and the cheap Go plan, not paying Plus or Pro subscribers. OpenAI says ads don't influence answers and that advertisers don't see your conversations. Why it matters for you: AI chat is becoming a real paid channel, next to Google and Meta. Growth marketers who can say "I've tested ChatGPT ads" will stand out in interviews this winter.

## Business story

Now our business story, about how fast Wall Street can fall in and out of love.

It starts in Israel with two brothers, Aviv and Matteo Shapira. Before this company, they built Replay Technologies, a company behind three-hundred-sixty-degree sports replays, and sold it to Intel in twenty sixteen for a hundred and seventy-five million dollars. In twenty eighteen they started XTEND with Rubi Liani, the founder of the Israeli Drone Racing League, and the defense expert Adir Tubi.

The original idea came from gaming and virtual reality: make flying a drone feel as natural as playing a video game, with a headset and a simple handheld controller. Then the market changed. Militaries needed small drones that soldiers with no flight training could fly inside buildings and tunnels, where GPS often doesn't work. As Aviv Shapira put it, "When we left gaming a long time ago, defense became our biggest market." Today its STRIKER drones run on its own operating system, called XOS.

Here's where the story turns into a Wall Street story. Instead of a traditional IPO, XTEND went public by merging into a small Florida construction company already listed in New York, JFB Construction. It was an all-stock deal, meaning paid in shares, not cash, valuing XTEND at about one and a half billion dollars. The deal came with a hundred-and-fifty-two-million-dollar investment from backers including Eric Trump, the president's son. The shares started trading in September and jumped twenty-five percent on day one.

Then reality arrived. This week XTEND's market value fell below five hundred million dollars for the first time, roughly two-thirds below its debut. And this happened right after good news: a forty-and-a-half-million-dollar US military order for six thousand STRIKER systems, plus a top ranking in a Pentagon drone program. So why the drop? The numbers. Last year XTEND had about twenty million dollars in revenue and lost twenty-seven million. In the first quarter of this year, revenue more than tripled to five point eight million, but losses more than doubled. Meanwhile, the projections in its merger documents promised eighty-five million in revenue this year, a hundred and fifty million next year, and three hundred and eighty-one million in twenty twenty-eight. Investors are asking whether it can really build drones fast enough to hit those targets.

The lesson for a marketer is a good one. A great story can get you a big valuation, especially in a hot sector like defense drones. But once you're public, the market stops grading the story and starts grading the spreadsheet. The gap between what you promised and what you deliver is what moves the stock.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, and a fair cut short.

Art Basel Paris closes at seven tonight, two days early, with no public days this weekend. Organizers cited the protests across France. A spokesperson said, "Safety, protection of artworks and an orderly move-out come first." The fair has two hundred and eleven galleries from forty-one countries at the Grand Palais. The good news for dealers is that most big deals happen in the VIP preview days, when top collectors get in first, and those already happened. The pain falls on smaller galleries, which lose weekend sales and have to pack up fast.

Meanwhile in New York, a jaw-dropping record. At Sotheby's on Wednesday night, a marble torso of a Roman emperor sold for thirty-four point three million dollars, the highest price ever paid at auction for a Roman antiquity. It's about two thousand years old, from the Julio-Claudian period, the dynasty of emperors that started with Augustus. The estimate was eight to twelve million, and five bidders fought for about fourteen minutes. The whole sale made sixty-three million dollars, and every single lot sold. Sotheby's says nearly twenty percent of bidders were under forty, which is a sign younger collectors are buying ancient art, not just contemporary.

And looking ahead to November: Christie's will offer a long-unseen Basquiat, "Untitled, Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads," from nineteen eighty-three. It's seven feet square and riffs on a drawing by Leonardo da Vinci of five caricature-like heads. It carries an estimate of around thirty million dollars at the November nineteenth evening sale. Christie's calls it "a jewel in his output that has been hidden for twenty years."

## Wellness

Finally, a wellness idea for your morning coffee. Researchers at Tufts University looked at national diet data on about thirty thousand American adults, from two thousand three to twenty eighteen. People weren't drinking more coffee, about thirteen ounces a day. But the calories in that coffee rose nearly forty percent, from forty-three to sixty a day. The surprise culprit wasn't sugar. It was non-dairy creamer, which became the biggest source of added coffee calories, because many creamers are high in saturated fat. The lead researcher said, "Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense." So the practical move: check your creamer's label, try a splash of real milk or an unsweetened plant milk, and keep coffee's benefits without the extra fat.
