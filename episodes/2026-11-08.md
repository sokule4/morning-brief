## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Sunday, November eighth, and this is your Morning Brief. This afternoon, President Herzog formally hands Gadi Eisenkot the job of forming Israel's next government, while Netanyahu suddenly calls for national unity. The US is widening its squeeze on Iran with a new task force aimed at its land borders, as two tankers hit mines near the Strait of Hormuz. And at the movies, a Japanese monster film is heading for a record opening in America. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start here at home, with the moment the opposition has been waiting for. On Saturday evening, President Herzog announced that Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief of staff who leads the Yashar party, will get the mandate, the official first chance to form a government. The ceremony is today at five in the afternoon at the President's Residence in Jerusalem. Eisenkot then has twenty-eight days to build a coalition, with a possible fourteen-day extension.

Quick reminder of the math. Sixty-seven of the Knesset's one hundred twenty members recommended Eisenkot, and forty-six recommended Netanyahu. But a recommendation is not a promise to sit in a government. Eisenkot's own four-party bloc has forty-eight seats. The two Arab-led parties, Ra'am and the Joint List, add nineteen more, but several of his partners refuse to depend on them. So the real puzzle is the ultra-Orthodox, or Haredi, parties: United Torah Judaism, with seven seats, and Shas, with six. Add both to Eisenkot's forty-eight and you get exactly sixty-one, the narrowest possible majority.

That's why the next few days are all about the rabbis. Times of Israel reports that a source in Shas's Council of Torah Sages, the rabbis who make the party's big decisions, said Shas would never act against the rabbis of United Torah Judaism. In other words, if United Torah Judaism joins Eisenkot, Shas won't block it. But the official Shas statement called reports that it could join an Eisenkot government "total fake news." And Netanyahu is reportedly pressuring United Torah Judaism not to sit with Avigdor Liberman, the secular right-winger who said on Friday he's ready for "painful compromises" on drafting yeshiva students, young men studying full-time at religious seminaries, just to end the Netanyahu era.

Netanyahu's response came in a video on Saturday night. "This is the time for unity," he said. "This is the will of the people." He means a so-called unity government, where rival camps share power, but one that would still include his far-right partners, Religious Zionism and Otzma Yehudit. He also claimed Israel "would not survive" a government formed with what he called "the Islamists," aiming at the Arab parties. Eisenkot's party fired back, calling him divisive, but said Likud and its allies could join an Eisenkot government if they drop "the extremists" and accept his principles. That offer does not include Netanyahu himself. Eisenkot has said anyone who held major power on October seventh should leave public life.

What happens if he fails? Herzog would have three days to hand the task to someone else, likely Netanyahu, who would get twenty-eight days with no extension. If that fails too, sixty-one lawmakers can ask for a specific candidate. And if every path collapses, the Knesset dissolves and Israel goes back to the polls, while Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister, meaning he keeps running things until a new government is sworn in.

One more thread that keeps growing. On Saturday night, protesters in Tel Aviv's Habima Square rallied against the Shin Bet's investigation into alleged foreign election interference involving Arab parties, and called on the next government to fire the agency's chief, David Zini. Critics, including the Arab parties and opposition leaders, say the probe is political. Haaretz reports this morning that Zini removed critics and tapped an ally to lead it. Expect this to become one of the first fights of whatever government comes next.

Now to Iran, where Washington is tightening the screws. On Saturday, CENTCOM, the US military command for the Middle East, announced Task Force Iron Gate. Its job is to stop Iran moving weapons and banned goods over land, across its borders and the Caspian Sea, the huge inland sea to Iran's north. Until now, America's pressure has mostly been at sea, through the naval blockade of Iran's ports that's been in place for months. CENTCOM says it has redirected one hundred forty-seven ships since July and disabled five that refused, including one called the Huda in the Gulf of Oman on Friday.

At the same time, the sea is getting more dangerous. Iran's Revolutionary Guard says two oil tankers hit mines, explosives hidden in the water, near the Strait of Hormuz, and the UAE says it rescued twenty-two people from a cargo ship that caught fire. Iranian state media also reported new explosions on Qeshm Island and in the ports of Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas. Diplomacy hasn't stopped. Qatar says its mediation is continuing, and Trump says Tehran still wants a deal. But a former Pentagon official told reporters that if this escalates, the next targets would likely be economic, like infrastructure, rather than only military sites. Why it matters to you: every mine in that strait pushes up fuel and shipping costs, here and back home.

Closer to home, a soggy weekend. Heavy rain flooded parts of Binyamina and knocked out power near Gan Yavne, and police closed part of Route 90 by the Dead Sea for fear of flash floods. In Gaza, the Hamas-run media office says rain flooded seventy thousand tents in twelve hours. It blames Israel for limiting shelter supplies, while Israel says it only restricts items that could be used as weapons.

And a quick world note: South Africa announces official results of its local elections today, after early counts showed the ANC, Nelson Mandela's old party, falling to around thirty-one percent, its worst ever.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the midterms changed the makeup of Albany, the state capital. JTA reports that the Shomer Shabbos caucus, the informal group of Sabbath-observant Orthodox Jewish lawmakers in the state legislature, now has a record eleven members. Three are newcomers, all Democrats: Pesach Osina in a swing district in Queens that includes Far Rockaway, Lisa "Malkie" Vider, who narrowly flipped a Long Island State Senate seat, and Joey Cohen-Saban, who beat a Republican incumbent in South Brooklyn in a rematch. Ari Brown is the group's only Republican. It's a striking detail from a blue-wave night: a growing Orthodox presence inside the Democratic Party, at the same time as the party's debate over Israel gets louder.

If you need something to tell your friends back home about, Wednesday is Veterans Day, and the city's parade, one of the biggest in the country, runs up Fifth Avenue from Twenty-Sixth to Forty-Fifth Street, starting at twelve thirty. The grand marshal is Suni Williams, the NASA astronaut and Navy veteran, leading more than two hundred eighty marching units and over twenty-five floats.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, on the eve of a painful date. Tomorrow, November ninth, marks eighty-eight years since Kristallnacht, the Nazi pogrom against German Jews. Ahead of it, Israel's Diaspora Affairs Ministry, which deals with Jewish communities abroad and antisemitism, published a sobering report. Its finding: there may be fewer threats overall, but synagogue attacks are getting deadlier. In the US, false bomb threats against synagogues fell from more than one thousand in twenty twenty-three to fifty-nine last year, but attacks involving lethal weapons rose from twenty-three to thirty-two. Attackers increasingly use guns, firebombs and cars, and target people walking to and from services, especially on Shabbat and holidays. The report also warns that groups and states, including Iran, may be paying local criminals to do the attacks. And the controversial bike ride targeting Jewish-owned restaurants in Philadelphia, which we told you about yesterday, is set for today.

Now a story of memory. Baghdad has its first Jewish museum, the only one in Iraq, inside the restored Shamash School, a Jewish school founded in nineteen twenty-eight. Iraqi Jews trace their history back about two thousand six hundred years, to the Babylonian exile. In the nineteen forties there were more than one hundred thirty thousand of them, one of the largest Jewish communities in the Middle East. Most left after the Farhud, a nineteen forty-one massacre of Iraqi Jews, and after Israel was founded. Today only a handful remain, mostly in the Kurdish north. More than a thousand people, mostly not Jewish, came on opening day. But there's a bittersweet edge. Iraq passed a law in twenty twenty-two that makes contact with Israelis illegal, so the families of most Iraqi Jews, many now living here, can't easily visit. One Jewish advocacy group called it "a display of Jewish absence rather than a restoration of Jewish presence."

And in American politics, pro-Israel conservatives are furious with Vice President JD Vance. After the Republicans' midterm losses, Vance posted that voters who left the party wanted, among other things, to "change our relationship to Israel." Christians United for Israel, a big evangelical pro-Israel group, called him "clearly, demonstrably and unambiguously wrong,". It's an early sign of how Israel could split the Republican race for twenty twenty-eight.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech and AI, starting with your phone. Apple has opened its new Siri AI to all app developers in a developer preview, meaning an early test version just for builders, in the latest iOS beta. In plain words: soon you'll be able to ask Siri to do things inside other apps, like check an order or book a table, and Siri will figure out the steps. Developers can even write little instruction files that teach Siri how to chain several actions together. But don't expect it this week. Apple says customers will see it "in a few months," and apps can only ship it in the spring.

The AI image and video race keeps speeding up. OpenAI released Images two point six, which can now make animated, looping GIFs and generates up to fifty percent faster. Google cut the price of its Gemini Omni video model by thirty percent and now makes clips up to fifteen seconds long.

Now, a reality check on AI money. Data from Ramp, the corporate card company, shows that business spending on AI barely grew, up just one and a half percent across August and September, after a forty-two percent jump in July. The reason seems to be a price war, as OpenAI and Anthropic offer discounts. And on Tuesday's ballots, early counts show voters backed restrictions in thirty-eight of thirty-nine local measures on data centers, the giant server buildings that power AI.

And here in Israel, Globes reports that Bezeq, the old national phone company, is teaming up with the real estate firm Adgar to build a data center in Petah Tikva, moving from phone lines into AI infrastructure.

## Business story

Our business story is about the cottage cheese in your fridge. Tnuva, Israel's biggest food company, is reviving plans for an IPO, its first sale of shares on the stock market. Calcalist reports it's aiming for a listing on the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange in the first half of twenty twenty-seven, at a valuation of about thirteen billion shekels, roughly four point one billion dollars.

Tnuva started almost a century ago as a cooperative of Israeli farmers, including the kibbutzim, Israel's collective farming communities,, and it grew into a giant. It processes about a billion liters of milk a year, and it owns brands you know, like Mama Of poultry and Sunfrost frozen vegetables. In twenty fifteen, Bright Food, a Chinese state-owned company, bought control for a deal valuing it at eight point six billion shekels. Today Bright Food owns about seventy-six percent, and kibbutz organizations own the rest.

Tnuva has tried to go public before. In twenty twenty-one, the kibbutzim fought it, and it collapsed. Last year it started again, then stopped in April this year, citing changes in the dairy market. The sticking point has always been who gets to sell how many shares. Meanwhile the business is growing. Revenue is nearing ten billion shekels this year, up from eight billion last year.

The timing is tricky. A Globes analysis found Tel Aviv's food stocks fell more than eighteen percent after a new food index launched in May. And Tnuva is a sensitive brand. In twenty eleven, outrage over the price of cottage cheese sparked a national protest. The marketing lesson: a company that's part of a country's daily routine has enormous trust, but also enormous scrutiny. Selling shares to the public means selling a story about the future, not just about milk.

## Pop culture, celebs and fashion

Now pop culture, and a monster weekend. "Godzilla Minus Zero," the follow-up to the Oscar-winning "Godzilla Minus One," is heading for about thirty-one million dollars in its US opening, nearly triple its predecessor. That makes it only the fourth non-English film ever to open at number one at the US box office, after "The Passion of the Christ," "Hero," and "Demon Slayer: Infinity Castle." Audiences gave it an A CinemaScore, the grade opening-night moviegoers give a film. Warner Bros.' new "The Cat in the Hat" is a distant second at around eighteen million. And the indie studio A24's documentary "You Can See Everything" has now made almost seventy-nine million dollars and could become only the second documentary of its kind to pass one hundred million, after "Fahrenheit nine eleven."

Music fans, mark your calendar. Grammy nominations are announced a week from tomorrow, Monday, November sixteenth, live from Times Square, and the ceremony is February seventh.

In fashion, a sad farewell. Coperni, the Paris label famous for spraying a dress directly onto Bella Hadid's body on the runway in twenty twenty-two, is closing after its company was placed into liquidation, meaning its assets will be sold to pay its debts. The founders, Arnaud Vaillant and Sébastien Meyer, blame unpaid money owed by their majority shareholder and distributor, not weak sales. "We have tried everything to preserve Coperni," they wrote. It's a reminder that even viral brands can be fragile behind the scenes.

And a comeback that many people are uneasy about. Ye, formerly Kanye West, held his first big runway show in four years, Yeezy Season zero one, at his new Los Angeles flagship store, with Bianca Censori and North West in the audience. The backdrop matters: in twenty twenty-two, his antisemitic remarks ended his Adidas partnership, and in January he took out a full-page newspaper ad apologizing. The industry is still deciding whether that's enough.

For something cozier, the holiday campaigns keep coming. Louis Vuitton's is set in a snowy train station, with miniature trains running through window displays around the world. And Loewe's collection with Disney's "The Nightmare Before Christmas," with Jack Skellington and Sally on bags and knits, launches Thursday, with a behind-the-scenes film featuring Tim Burton.

## Art and design

Next, art, where a Paris court just made a ruling the whole market is talking about. On Thursday, it ordered the return of a Camille Pissarro painting looted by the Nazis, "Darse de pêche et bassin Duquesne," a view of the port of Dieppe from nineteen oh two, to the heirs of Salomon Flavian, a Jewish banker who owned it from nineteen thirty-five. It was seized in Paris in nineteen forty-one. The twist: Sotheby's sold it in London in June twenty twenty-four for one point two million pounds, under a settlement that the heirs' lawyer says only two relatives signed. The court cancelled that sale and ordered Sotheby's to reveal the buyer within two months, or pay one thousand euros for every day of delay. Under French law, cancelling the original theft cancels every sale after it. Sotheby's says it's disappointed, and the restitution firm involved, a company that recovers looted art for families, plans to appeal. Why it matters: provenance, the documented ownership history of an artwork, is now something buyers, auction houses and even marketers of art have to take extremely seriously, because a gap in that history can undo a sale years later.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with a study worth knowing before your next glass of wine. Researchers from the University of Toronto and Columbia reviewed more than seven thousand two hundred studies and found no overall protective effect from drinking at any level. At two drinks a day, a level many people think of as moderate, about one in twenty-five people would die early from alcohol-related causes over their lifetime. Small amounts may slightly lower the risk of heart disease and stroke, but the cancer risks outweigh that. These are population averages, not a prediction for you. The practical idea: if you drink, pick a few alcohol-free days each week, and try a fun mocktail when you're out.
