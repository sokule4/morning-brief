## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Wednesday, November eleventh, and this is your Morning Brief. Israel's new Knesset was sworn in, sixty-seven lawmakers forced a vote to replace the speaker next Tuesday, and Eisenkot's party walked away from talks with Likud after Netanyahu's speech. Trump says the war with Iran is "effectively over," while Tehran calls the talk of negotiations fake news. And Anthropic, the company behind Claude, sets a price for the biggest tech listing of the year. In your Cyber Watch, two Israeli-founded startups raise a combined one hundred thirty million dollars, and the founders of Zerto come back with a new company.

## Israel

We start here at home, where the twenty-sixth Knesset was sworn in on Tuesday afternoon in Jerusalem. All one hundred twenty members took their oath. Forty-six are newcomers, and there's a record thirty-three women. About one in ten members has lost a loved one to terror or war, including Gadi Eisenkot himself, who lost his son in combat. The youngest member, twenty-four-year-old Yonatan Shalev of B'Yachad, broke a record that stood for sixty-six years. The most veteran is Benjamin Netanyahu, who first entered the Knesset in nineteen eighty-eight.

The ceremony was calm, but not without sparks. Members of the Joint List, the alliance of Arab-led parties, walked out during Hatikvah, Israel's national anthem. Mansour Abbas of Ra'am, the other Arab party, stayed in the chamber. When Joint List chairman Yousef Jabareen rose to take his oath, Itamar Ben-Gvir of Otzma Yehudit shouted, "Who is he pledging allegiance to, Hamas?"

President Herzog urged everyone to "do everything possible to avoid another round of elections," saying voters "long for routine, security and tranquility." Netanyahu, in his own speech, again called for a national unity government under his leadership, and, according to reports, said Arab lawmakers want Israel's destruction. That backfired fast. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, had a meeting scheduled with Likud's negotiators, and it canceled it. Its statement said, "Netanyahu does not want unity," and that he "will always choose himself. At any cost." Eisenkot called it a "hate-filled speech." Likud replied that Netanyahu stands by his view that it's "impossible to cooperate with supporters of terrorism."

Now the real power move. Right after the oath, sixty-seven lawmakers signed a demand to replace the Knesset speaker, Likud's Amir Ohana. Why does that matter? The speaker decides which bills reach the floor and when. Ohana set the vote for next Tuesday at four p.m., the latest date the rules allow. He said he "strongly oppose[s]" what the new majority plans, but will "respect the will of the majority." He doesn't have much choice: a twenty twenty High Court ruling says a speaker can't block a vote when a majority demands one. Eisenkot's bloc picked Matan Kahana of Yashar, a former fighter pilot and former minister, to hold the chair while coalition talks continue. Avigdor Liberman is in line to chair the powerful Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, and Liran Avisar Ben-Horin of B'Yachad would be the first woman ever to run the Finance Committee, which controls the budget.

Why rush, before there's even a government? Because the Knesset can pass its own bills. Eisenkot's camp has already filed several: a state commission of inquiry into October seventh, a term limit for prime ministers written into a Basic Law, one of Israel's quasi-constitutional laws, and a package that would reverse the judicial overhaul, including the law that weakened the attorney general. Netanyahu's government stays on as a caretaker until a new one is sworn in.

But a speaker vote isn't a government. Eisenkot's four parties have forty-eight seats. He needs thirteen more for sixty-one. The two ultra-Orthodox parties, United Torah Judaism and Shas, have exactly thirteen together. United Torah Judaism says it won't help replace Ohana before a government is formed. So next Tuesday is the first real test of who's with whom.

A quick economy note: S and P, the credit rating agency, raised its forecast for Israel's growth to five and a half percent this year and seven percent next year, mostly because of Nvidia's big operations here. It expects government debt to fall slowly, but warns that renewed fighting with Iran is the main risk.

And in Gaza, the caretaker prime minister's office says winter supplies are going in, including more than ninety-one thousand tents, and insists Hamas "has not surrendered a single weapon," so there's no rebuilding until it disarms. Aid groups say mobile homes, which they need most, are still banned, and eight countries, including Britain and Saudi Arabia, are calling on Israel to ease limits on so-called dual-use goods, items with both civilian and military uses.

And on the northern border, UNIFIL, the UN peacekeeping force in southern Lebanon, says about a dozen shots were fired at one of its patrols near Tyre, outside the Israeli buffer zone, the strip the army controls. Nobody was hurt. The force is due to leave southern Lebanon at the end of the year.

## World

Now to the world, and Iran. After meeting Qatar's prime minister at the White House on Monday, Trump said Iran, "militarily, is effectively over," and "we're just keeping it the status quo." That status quo means the US naval blockade of Iran's ports and Iron Gate, the American task force blocking smuggling over Iran's land borders, both stay in place. Trump called new strikes "quite unlikely," but added, "if we do have to go back in, we are locked and loaded." Oil fell, with Brent crude down to about ninety-two dollars a barrel.

Tehran tells a different story. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, the body that sets defense policy, told Washington to stop "manufacturing fake news about imaginary talks" and to "fold your hand." Iran says it won't negotiate until the US accepts its seven conditions. Turkey's intelligence chief says Qatar will host a meeting next week between Vice President JD Vance and Rezaei. Iran's foreign minister denies it. Meanwhile the Financial Times reports Netanyahu calls Trump "every day" urging him to resume the war, though another source says there's no real push. And the threats are spreading: the US pulled refueling planes from a British air base after an Iranian threat, the New York Times reports Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was moved to a military base over Iranian threats, Canada reimposed sanctions on eleven Iranian entities, and France blamed Iran's Revolutionary Guard for hacking supermarket fridges and freezers last month and promised sanctions.

In Washington, the House voted two hundred eleven to two hundred four to send Republican Thomas Massie's impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee. Impeachment is Congress's process to remove an official, and sending it to committee quietly kills it. Massie was the only Republican to vote with all the Democrats. Hegseth called it a "farce," saying, "None of these are high crimes or misdemeanors." Massie, who lost his primary, retires in January. That's also when Democrats take control of both chambers, so this fight may come back. Trump, meanwhile, posted, "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," blaming voting machines for his party's midterm losses.

And some cautious hope on Ukraine. Russian and Ukrainian officials met in Abu Dhabi for their first American-brokered talks since February. Jared Kushner, one of Trump's envoys, said they "narrowed the gaps," and the White House calls itself "cautiously optimistic." But a Russian source says Moscow's main goal is a new European security setup, with Ukraine's future "subordinate" to it, which is exactly what Kyiv and Europe fear.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where Mayor Mamdani signed COPA, the Community Opportunity to Purchase Act, at a ceremony in East New York, Brooklyn. It gives certified nonprofit housing groups the first chance to buy certain apartment buildings when owners sell, mainly buildings with serious violations or affordability deals, about twenty-one hundred buildings in all. Nonprofits get twenty days to say they're interested and seventy days to make an offer, and if the owner takes another buyer, they get a right of first refusal, the chance to match that offer. The bill took six years and survived a veto by Eric Adams. Mamdani called it "a tool to keep their homes out of the hands of predatory investors," but admitted it won't solve the housing crisis on its own. Landlord groups say rising costs and the rent freeze are the real problem, and there's no dedicated fund yet to help nonprofits actually buy. It takes effect in November twenty twenty-seven.

And holiday season has officially landed. The Rockefeller Center Christmas tree is up: a seventy-six-foot, eleven-ton Norway spruce from West Edmeston, upstate. It lights up on December second with more than fifty thousand lights and a nine-hundred-pound Swarovski star, and afterward it becomes lumber for Habitat for Humanity.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a new survey on how American Jews voted. The Mellman Group, polling for the Jewish Electorate Institute, found sixty-nine percent of Jewish voters backed Democrats in the midterms, down six points from twenty twenty-four, while most other groups moved toward Democrats. More than a third of Jewish Democrats say their party is "too accepting of antisemitic rhetoric," and one in four Jewish Republicans say theirs is too friendly to white nationalists. Other polls, from J Street and The Jewish Majority, found almost no change, so treat this as one data point. In Manhattan, NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch accepted the World Jewish Congress's Herzl Award at a dinner at the Museum of Modern Art. ## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech. Anthropic set the price range for its IPO, its first sale of shares to the public: seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share. It's selling eight hundred twenty-five million shares on the Nasdaq under the ticker, the stock's short trading name, ANT. At the top of the range, that's about sixty-four billion dollars raised and a value of up to one point four trillion. Partners including Google and Nvidia have agreed to buy twenty-six billion dollars more. Its revenue is now running at about eighty-one billion dollars a year. CNBC reports trading could start November nineteenth. Interesting detail: that valuation is lower than the roughly two trillion people floated weeks ago, a sign of the recent AI stock selloff.

Also on AI, New York's RAISE Act is now in motion. Since Tuesday, AI developers with more than five hundred million dollars in revenue, like OpenAI and Anthropic, must register with the state by January first. They'll have to publish safety plans and report serious safety incidents within seventy-two hours, or face fines of up to one million dollars, and three million for repeat violations.

And an Israeli science story. Researchers at the Weizmann Institute and the Hebrew University, led by Professors Efrat Shema and Guy Ron, published a blood test in Nature that detected early breast cancer with ninety-five percent accuracy, including in women with dense breast tissue, using AI to read tiny chemical tags on DNA in the blood. They first tested it on seventy-seven patients followed for years at the Mayo Clinic and thirty healthy women, then expanded with Sheba and Kaplan medical centers, and it also predicted which cancers would come back with eighty-one percent accuracy.

## Business story

Our business story is about your bank account, maybe. On Tuesday, the Bank of Israel, the central bank, gave Revolut a conditional license to open a bank here. Revolut is the London-born app bank, valued at about one hundred fifteen billion dollars, with more than eighty million customers worldwide, famous for cheap currency exchange and slick card controls.

Here's why that's a big deal. Israeli banking is dominated by five big banking groups, and for about forty years no new bank got a license at all. Revolut is only the third new license since twenty nineteen, after One Zero and Esh, and neither has shaken the giants. One Zero is still losing money, and its controlling shareholder, Amnon Shashua of Mobileye, is reportedly exploring selling his stake.

"Conditional" matters: Revolut Bank Israel can't serve customers yet. It has to hit technical and regulatory milestones first, and there's no launch date. It hired a local insider as CEO, Uri Nathan, who used to run Pepper, Bank Leumi's digital bank. Governor Amir Yaron called it a vote of confidence in Israel's economy.

The marketer's takeaway: Revolut doesn't win on being a bank, it wins on being an app people love to show their friends. If it brings that here, the old banks will have to compete on foreign exchange fees and user experience, which is good news for anyone who gets paid in shekels and spends in dollars.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness: eat your fiber, for a reason you might not expect. Princeton researchers found that fiber does more than feed your gut bacteria. When your microbiome, the bacteria living in your gut, runs short of fiber, some microbes start breaking down the protective mucus that lines your intestine. Fiber stops that, and pushes them toward making helpful compounds instead. Their research also found that indigestible proteins from plants may help in the same way. So today, add one plant to each meal: lentils, oats, beans or an extra vegetable.

## Funding and acquisitions

Today's money story is about AI agents running security, and securing AI agents, and it's mostly Israeli.

First, Daylight. Daylight runs a security operations service, meaning it watches companies' systems around the clock and responds to attacks, but it uses AI agents for most of the work, with human experts stepping in for decisions that need judgment. It raised an eighty million dollar Series B, led by the growth equity arm of Goldman Sachs Alternatives, with Okta Ventures, the investment arm of the identity company Okta, joining existing backers Bain Capital and Craft Ventures. That brings total funding to one hundred twenty million, at a reported valuation of about four hundred million dollars. Daylight was founded less than two years ago by CEO Hagai Shapira and CTO Eldad Rudich, who met in an Israeli army intelligence unit and later held senior roles at Torq. Rudich was actually Torq's very first employee. Ynet describes the company as based in New York, with its research in Israel. It has eighty employees, forty-three in Israel, and customers including Lovable, dLocal and LastPass. Is eighty million big for a Series B? Yes, especially since Shapira says revenue grew more than forty times in one year. The money goes to growing in the US and entering Europe, the Middle East, Africa and Asia-Pacific. Shapira's pitch: "The future of security operations won't be defined by how many people a company can deploy."

Second, Bay, a Tel Aviv company that just came out of stealth, meaning it had been building in secret, with fifty million dollars. That's a thirty-seven million dollar Series A led by Battery Ventures, with M12, Microsoft's venture fund, Micron Ventures, and Wiz CEO Assaf Rappaport personally investing, plus a thirteen million seed round from YL Ventures and Striker that it hadn't announced before. Bay watches what AI agents do across employees' laptops, the cloud and apps, and stops them from breaking company rules. CEO Yuval Zacharia is a Unit 8200 alum who led AI research at Hunters, the Israeli security startup. Customers include Micron, Peloton, ClickUp and Wix. Its selling point is speed: it says it installs in under five minutes without putting software on every device. Micron, which is both a customer and an investor, calls it a "trust layer" that "will become essential infrastructure for the agentic era."

Third, a comeback. Ziv and Oded Kedem, the brothers who founded Zerto and sold it to Hewlett Packard Enterprise in twenty twenty-one for three hundred seventy-four million dollars, emerged from stealth with Dreeze and a thirty-three million dollar seed round led by Norwest and Bessemer Venture Partners. That's huge for a seed. Dreeze restores whole cloud applications after an attack, not just the data, including settings, credentials and connections. It already has about seventy employees across Israel and the US.

And in America, WhiteMatter, based in San Francisco and Austin, raised a twenty million dollar Series A led by Blumberg Capital. It used to be called QuilrAI, and its platform governs AI agents, even assigning each agent its own "guardian agent" to enforce the rules. It says it will use the money to grow its go-to-market and executive teams.

Step back and look at the week: Vanta buying Pillar, Palo Alto still negotiating for Clutch, and now Daylight, Bay and WhiteMatter. Investors are paying for two things: AI that does security work, and security that keeps AI in line.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, Don Jeter's game plan. We told you last week that Abnormal AI hired Torq's bold marketing chief as its CMO. Now he's explained what he wants to do, in Business Insider and MarketScale. He wants Abnormal known beyond security buyers, even by his parents and his fourteen-year-old nephew, and he wants it to be the most talked-about cyber company of twenty twenty-seven. His tools: out-of-home advertising, meaning billboards and ads in public places, live activations, and content people share on their own. His line: "Safe is boring, and boring is invisible." His first job is to fix a perception problem, that Abnormal only does email security. The open question is measurement: Abnormal hasn't said how it will connect buzz to sales pipeline. If you interview for a brand role in cyber, have an answer for exactly that.

Second, listen to how today's startups describe themselves, because each one picked a single sharp idea. Dreeze: "Getting the data back doesn't mean the business is back." That names a fear every executive has after an attack, in plain words. Bay's investor compared it to the firewall: just as every company needed one for the internet, every company will need Bay for AI agents. And Daylight calls itself "agent-native" but sells humans for judgment calls, which answers buyers who are nervous about letting AI run security alone. That's the pattern: one problem, one line, no jargon.

And a packaging lesson from Daylight: it sells three ways, as a fully managed service, alongside a customer's own security team, or with its engineers embedded inside the customer. Letting buyers choose how much control they keep removes a big objection before sales even starts.

Third, the Blue Mantis reporting line. When a CMO reports to the revenue chief, marketing is judged mainly on pipeline. In the press release, the CRO praised her "sharp focus on sales and marketing alignment." For a marketer, that's a clue about what the job will actually measure.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Dreeze. A seed company with seventy people, founders who've already sold a company, and a plan to spend this money on sales, marketing and customer teams. That's a marketing team about to be built, and Israeli founders who know how to scale one.

Second, Daylight. Forty-three of its eighty people are in Israel, and it's expanding into the US, Europe, the Middle East and Asia-Pacific, which usually means marketing roles here supporting those regions. Its founders came from Torq, a company you've been watching.

Third, Bay. A fresh Series A, Tel Aviv headquarters, and brand-name customers. Companies at this stage often make their first senior marketing hire right after announcing the round.
