## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, this is your Cyber Brief. Two Israeli items lead today: Sentra, founded by a former commander of Unit 8200, raised one hundred fifty million dollars to control what AI agents can reach inside a company, and it plans to double its team. And Brava, a twenty-person startup from Unit 8200 and Microsoft veterans, raised a fifteen million dollar seed round.

## Funding and acquisitions

Let's start with Sentra, the biggest round of the week. Sentra makes data security software. In plain words, it finds where a company's sensitive data lives, like customer records or financial files, maps who and what can access it, and cuts back access nobody needs. The new angle is AI agents. CEO Yoav Regev calls them "the busiest consumers of enterprise data," because an agent working for an employee can reach everything that employee can. Sentra flags an agent that suddenly grabs sensitive data it doesn't normally touch, and can remove its permissions automatically.

The round is a one hundred fifty million dollar Series C, led by Capital One Ventures, the investment arm of the American bank, which also gets a board seat, a vote on the company's board of directors. Existing investors Bessemer, Oren Zeev's fund, Standard Investments, Munich Re Ventures and Key1 Capital joined. Total raised is now over two hundred fifty million dollars. Sentra didn't share its valuation, but Regev says it has tripled since its last round, a fifty million dollar Series B in April last year.

Is that big? Yes. It's three times the size of its last round, and Sentra says business grew more than three hundred percent in a year, with about one hundred large customers in the US and Europe and revenue in the tens of millions. Regev's stated goal is one hundred million dollars in sales. The founders are serious Israeli cyber names: Regev once headed Unit 8200's cyber department, and his co-founder and president, Asaf Kochan, is a former commander of the unit. The company has one hundred thirty employees, sixty-five in Tel Aviv, and here's the part for you: it plans to double its workforce in Israel and the US, open a London office, and expand its field and channel programs. Channel means selling through partners, like resellers and consultants.

Next, Brava Security, which raised a fifteen million dollar seed round led by Pitango and F2, with private investors including senior executives from Anthropic, Microsoft and Check Point. Brava tests whether a company's defenses would actually notice an attack, without running a real one. Its AI agents create the digital traces an attack would leave behind, then check whether the security tools catch them. CEO Idan Basre is a graduate of Talpiot, the army's elite science program, spent ten years in Unit 8200, and then led product management for Microsoft Defender. His pitch is sharp: "Everyone is racing to put AI in charge of the SOC," the security operations center, the team that watches for attacks. "You can't automate a decision based on evidence no one ever proved." Fifteen million is a solid seed, and Brava has about twenty people, with research in Israel and a commercial team now being built in the US.

A follow-up on a deal we covered last month: IonQ, the quantum computing company, officially closed its purchase of Elisity, the zero trust network security company, on Wednesday. At closing it paid one point one five billion dollars in IonQ stock, plus up to one hundred fifty million more in an earnout, money paid later only if Elisity hits revenue targets through January twenty twenty-eight. IonQ also set aside a one hundred million dollar retention pool, stock that keeps Elisity's people from leaving. CEO James Winebrenner stays on.

And a small one from Europe: CyberKord, a Milan startup founded this year, raised two point seven million euros in pre-seed money, the very first round before a seed, led by 360 Capital. It uses AI and a digital copy of a company's IT setup to investigate and fix IT and security problems with less manual work.

## People moves

Now people moves, and three go-to-market hires stand out.

The biggest is at Axonius, the asset intelligence company founded by Israelis and based in New York with research in Tel Aviv. Its platform gives security and IT teams one list of every device, account and app they have, and what's exposed. It named Thais Lima as chief revenue officer, leading global growth strategy, go-to-market across industries, and international expansion. She comes from Twilio, the communications software company, where she was senior vice president of global sales and interim chief revenue officer of a business unit, after finance roles at IBM and GE. Axonius also hired Lior Mozel as CTO, chief technology officer, the executive in charge of the technology and engineering teams. He was chief engineering officer at the data company WEKA and, before that, at Cybereason, the Israeli security company. Co-founder Ofri Shur moves from CTO to chief technology strategist. CEO Joe Diamond said demand "is growing faster than any single team can meet by working the old way." A new revenue chief from outside usually means a refreshed go-to-market plan within months, and marketing is part of that.

Acronis, the Swiss company that sells backup and cyber protection mostly through MSPs, managed service providers, the outside IT firms that run tech for small businesses, named HoJin Kim as chief revenue officer. He held the same job at WatchGuard, another security company that sells through MSPs, and worked earlier at SonicWall, Samsung and Hewlett Packard Enterprise. When a channel-heavy company hires a channel veteran as revenue chief, expect more partner programs and partner marketing, the campaigns you run with and for resellers.

And OneTrust, the privacy and AI governance company used by more than half of the Fortune five hundred, reshuffled its leadership. Jim Monroe moves up to COO, chief operating officer, the executive running day-to-day operations, including go-to-market enablement. Tim Mullen goes from CISO to CIO, chief information officer, in charge of the company's own technology. And Kimberly Paine joins as chief legal officer from SecureWorks.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and one data point every cyber marketer should know by heart. 6sense, the sales and marketing software company, surveyed four thousand six hundred eleven business buyers for its new buyer experience report. The vendor a buying group, the team of people who make a purchase decision together, already favored before first contacting any seller won eighty-two percent of the time. Buyers spent sixty percent of a ten-month buying cycle choosing vendors before talking to anyone. Their top source was vendor websites, at thirty-six percent, while only twenty-six percent used AI chatbots to discover vendors. Translation: by the time a lead fills out a form, the decision is mostly made. That's why brand, content and your website matter so much in cyber, where buying cycles are long.

Second, AI in go-to-market keeps consolidating. Salesforce agreed to buy Cresta, whose voice agents can handle customer calls from start to finish and coach human sales reps live. No price was disclosed.

Third, a fun one for you as a creative. Optimizely, the software company, made a B2B marketing comedy called "Murder in Marketing," released as a micro-drama, short vertical episodes made for phones, with a feature-length film premiering November thirteenth. B2B brands are borrowing consumer entertainment formats to get attention.

And a positioning lesson from Sentra. Look at its language: agents are "the busiest consumers of enterprise data," and it secures the data itself, not the perimeter. It also stresses that it analyzes data inside the customer's environment, unlike tools that copy data out. And having a bank lead your round is its own marketing: a buyer becomes a backer. Compare that with Rapid7, an older public security company, which reported Wednesday that its annual recurring revenue fell two percent to about eight hundred twenty million dollars, during a restructuring. CEO Wael Mohamed said, "We are not declaring victory." Investors are paying for the AI agent story, not for the old one.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you. First, Sentra. It just raised one hundred fifty million, it's doubling its team in Israel and the US, and it's expanding field and channel programs and entering London. That means product marketing, which shapes how the product is positioned and pitched, field marketing, the events and campaigns that support local sales teams, and partner marketing roles, and its Tel Aviv office is big. Second, Brava. Twenty people, fresh seed money and a US commercial team being built from scratch. A first marketing hire there would shape the brand and the category story from day one. Third, Axonius. A brand-new chief revenue officer is about to rethink go-to-market, and with research in Tel Aviv, it's worth watching for marketing openings in the next few months.
