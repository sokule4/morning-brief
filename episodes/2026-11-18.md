Good morning Sophie. It's Wednesday, November eighteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. Gadi Eisenkot's camp took control of the Knesset last night, electing Matan Kahana as speaker. Nvidia posted the biggest quarter in its history, and its board said Jensen Huang is not coming back. And a Kandinsky that the Guggenheim Museum sold off in the sixties just set a record for the artist at almost eighty-six million dollars.

## Israel

We start here at home, with the first real show of strength in the new Knesset. Late Tuesday night, lawmakers elected Matan Kahana of Eisenkot's Yashar party as Knesset speaker, by sixty-six votes to fifty-two. He replaces Likud's Amir Ohana. Likud's candidate, Ofir Katz, lost. Who is Kahana? A former air force fighter pilot and a religious Zionist, he served as religious services minister in the Bennett-Lapid government.

It took a while to get there. Netanyahu's bloc held a filibuster, that's talking for hours to delay a vote, for more than six hours before the ballot. Who voted for Kahana? Eisenkot's four parties, plus the two Arab-led parties, Ra'am and the Joint List. The ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism had said it wouldn't back Kahana, and in the end it voted with Netanyahu's side.

Why does a speaker matter so much? Because the speaker controls the Knesset's agenda: which bills come up, and when. With Kahana in the chair, Eisenkot's camp can start moving its own bills even before there's a new government, including rolling back parts of the previous Knesset's judicial overhaul.

Kahana used his first speech to sound like a bridge-builder. "My life's mission is to bring us together," he said, and he turned to Eisenkot: "Gadi, do everything possible to form a government that will unite us." Eisenkot called the speech "very refreshing."

Netanyahu's camp was furious. Netanyahu, still the caretaker prime minister, called it a low point in the Knesset's history and accused Eisenkot, Naftali Bennett and Avigdor Liberman of siding with what he called "terror supporters," meaning the Arab parties. Likud and the Religious Zionist Party called it an "eternal disgrace." On the other side, Yair Golan, who leads the Democrats, put it simply: the speaker has been replaced, and the government is next.

But here's the catch. A speaker vote is not a government. Eisenkot's four parties have forty-eight seats, Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three, and the two Arab parties have nineteen. Eisenkot still needs a path to sixty-one. Ra'am's leader Mansour Abbas, whose votes helped Kahana win, warned on Tuesday that a minority government without the ultra-Orthodox "has no chance" of surviving. Liberman says the bloc will reach a deal with the ultra-Orthodox and still pass a draft law. And Bennett visited a protest "unity tent" outside the Knesset, said he's "very optimistic," and wouldn't say more. Channel 12 reports he backs a rotation, where two leaders take turns as prime minister, with Eisenkot going first and Netanyahu second.

Meanwhile, the election-day arrests story keeps moving. The Central Elections Committee said the Shin Bet's review has found "no evidence" so far of activity that could have affected the results. And a senior police official stood by the arrests of the Ra'am activists, while admitting that the tip coming from the prime minister's office "doesn't sound good."

In the West Bank, a tense night near Jericho. The army says two Israelis were lightly hurt in a suspected ramming and axe attack. Separately, a sixty-year-old Palestinian woman was killed, and Palestinian witnesses say settlers entered Area A, the zone under full Palestinian Authority control, and opened fire. And Britain sanctioned eight Israeli companies involved in building West Bank settlements, plus Tzav 9, an activist group it accuses of blocking aid convoys to Gaza. The move comes ahead of tender deadlines for E1, the planned neighborhood east of Jerusalem that critics say would cut the West Bank in two. Tzav 9 rejected the "extremist" label and said its members protest peacefully.

And one striking economic number. Israel's economy grew at an eight percent annualized rate in the third quarter, meaning the pace it would hit if it kept up for a full year. But Globes reports about half of that growth came from production abroad by Israeli-registered companies, mainly Nvidia. Nvidia designs chips here but makes them overseas, and the value still counts as Israeli. So the headline is real, but the everyday economy is growing much more slowly. One more sign of a strong quarter for the big players: Bank Leumi, one of Israel's two largest banks, reported a record quarterly profit of about three billion shekels, up more than ten percent, and will pay out half of it to shareholders.

## World

Now to the world, and President Trump's Asia trip. He landed in Manila on Tuesday, met leaders of the Quad, the US partnership with Australia, India and Japan, and joined the ASEAN summit dinner with Southeast Asian leaders. He's also expected to sign a trade deal with Thailand. Today he has a state visit with Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos, then flies to Shenzhen, China, for the APEC summit, the yearly meeting of Pacific Rim economies.

The big moment comes Thursday: the first-ever three-way meeting between Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin. The White House says the focus is nuclear weapons, and a senior official says Trump also plans to raise Iran. Ukraine isn't expected to be on the table. And Trump says North Korea's Kim Jong Un "would like to" come too. "We'll see what happens," he said.

Iran is shadowing the whole trip. CNN, citing seven officials, reported that American intelligence believes Iran is developing plans to target senior Trump officials with a dirty bomb, a regular explosive that spreads radioactive material, or with a chemical weapon. The sources say no attack is imminent, and the White House told CNN there are no credible threats to the homeland. Asked about it in Manila, Trump said he's "looking into" it, and warned that if Iran tried, it would "pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before."

On the diplomacy side, nothing moved. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, met Oman's foreign minister in Muscat on Tuesday. Oman called the talks "constructive," but there was no agreement, and neither side's statement even mentioned the Strait of Hormuz. That's notable, because earlier this month Oman's own readout talked about reopening shipping there. The strait is still closed, tankers keep hitting mines, and Iran says it stays shut until Washington accepts its terms, including lifting the American naval blockade and freeing billions in frozen Iranian money. The fighting also spread again to Bahrain on Tuesday, where Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it sent drones at an American radar, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio condemned the attacks.

And a press freedom story from Turkey. Police raided Sözcü TV, a television channel that supports the opposition, made arrests, and the government appointed a trustee to take over its management. Opposition figures have long accused President Erdoğan's government of using takeovers like this to silence critical media.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, with a number that's hard to hear before Thanksgiving. More than half of them had a job, or lived with a partner who did. About two point six million people, almost a third of the city, sometimes or often ran out of food or worried they would. And that's before the latest federal cuts: about twenty-six thousand New Yorkers lost SNAP, the federal food stamp program, on October first. Robin Hood's CEO, Richard Buery, said it plainly: "Work should be enough to put food on the table."

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world. In Philadelphia, the US Education Department opened a new investigation into the city's public school district. The question is whether the district broke a twenty twenty-four agreement it signed to protect Jewish students from antisemitic harassment. Education Secretary Linda McMahon wrote that "antisemitism has no place in our schools," pointing to Title VI, the civil rights law that bars discrimination in schools that get federal money. And in Romania, the European Jewish Congress condemned a banner at a soccer match in Bucharest between Romania and Poland on Saturday. It mocked Silviu Vexler, the head of Romania's Jewish community, over his Jewish identity and his work on laws against hate. The Congress called targeting a Jewish leader for fighting hatred "particularly appalling," and said stadiums must never become platforms for antisemitism.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and Nvidia's first quarter without Jensen Huang came in huge. Revenue more than doubled from a year ago to about one hundred sixteen billion dollars, well above Wall Street's forecasts, and almost all of it came from data centers. Vera Rubin, Nvidia's newest AI system, brought in nearly twenty billion dollars in its first quarter on sale, the fastest launch in the company's history. Nvidia expects even more next quarter, and announced a one hundred fifty billion dollar stock buyback, when a company buys its own shares to return cash to investors. The shares rose more than three percent after hours.

But the leadership question didn't go away. The board's chair, Harvey Jones, said Huang resigned after a disclosure of personal conduct that broke the company's code of conduct, and the board does not intend to reinstate him. Two search firms are looking for a permanent CEO. Colette Kress, the interim CEO, meaning the temporary boss, said it's "business as usual," and promised, "We will take care of our employees during this transition."

Speaking of big days, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, is expected to set its final IPO price tonight and start trading on Nasdaq tomorrow under the ticker A-N-T. The range is seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, which would value it at around a trillion dollars, and reports say orders reached about six times the shares on offer during its roadshow.

And an Israeli startup you'll like, as a marketer. Newsbound, from Tel Aviv, raised a three million dollar pre-seed round, a very first round before a startup has much of a product, led by Joule Ventures and State of Mind Ventures. Its AI watches breaking news, finds the right reporters and drafts pitches, while human PR experts check each step and the company's own executive sends the email. Founder Ran Peled was CMO at five tech companies, and he says seventy percent of his paying customers left a PR agency to use it. His pitch: AI search has made PR more important than ever, but agencies "stick to their old, expensive ways."

## Business story

Our business story is about an Israeli company that keeps breaking its own records. Elbit Systems, Israel's biggest publicly traded defense company, the maker of drones, electronic warfare systems and artillery, reported results on Tuesday. Revenue rose seventeen percent to two point two five billion dollars for the quarter, and profit jumped by about a third.

But the number everyone watches is the backlog, the orders a company has signed but not yet delivered. It hit a record thirty-three billion dollars. A year ago it was twenty-three billion. That's almost four years of sales already locked in. And most of it isn't from Israel: roughly three quarters of the backlog comes from foreign customers, and most of the recent jump came from Europe, where governments are rearming fast.

Here's the twist. Elbit is selling in the same Europe where many governments criticize Israel. Investors loved it anyway: the shares rose almost six percent in Tel Aviv, and Elbit declared a one dollar per share dividend, a cash payout to shareholders. CEO Bezhalel Machlis credits new AI-driven autonomous systems and high-power lasers. The lesson: in a war-shaped decade, battle-tested products are the strongest sales pitch there is.

## Art and design

Next, art, and we told you on Monday this one could make history. It did. At Christie's in New York on Tuesday night, Wassily Kandinsky's "Betonte Ecken," from nineteen twenty-three, sold for eighty-five point eight million dollars with fees. That smashes his previous auction record of about forty-five million.

The backstory is the best part. Hilla Rebay, the Guggenheim's first director, bought it in nineteen twenty-nine and gave it to the museum. Then, in nineteen sixty-four, short on space, the Guggenheim sold it at auction together with forty-nine other Kandinsky oil paintings, for a total of twenty-two thousand pounds. On Tuesday, that one painting sold for more than a thousand times what the museum got for all fifty. A good reminder for anyone building a collection, or an art agency: great work outlives the taste of the moment.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a reason to protect your sleep if you care about your weight. NPR pulled together the latest research. In one trial, overweight adults who were short on sleep were coached to sleep just over an hour more each night, and they ate about two hundred seventy fewer calories a day, without any diet. In sleep restriction studies, people ate about three hundred more calories a day, and brain scans show tired brains crave sweet and salty foods more, especially at night. It works the other way too: diets high in added sugar are linked to worse sleep. The experts' simple advice: a cool, dark bedroom, the same bedtime and wake-up time every day, and fewer sugary drinks.

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a quiet day for brand-new cyber rounds. After Corma's billion-dollar Series A, which we covered yesterday, I couldn't confirm a single new cyber funding round from the last forty-eight hours. So today's money story is about deals closing, the funds behind Israeli startups, and who might get bought next.

First, a completed deal. Quorum Cyber, a security services company focused on Microsoft security, closed its purchase of Ontinue, which runs what it calls an agentic SOC. A SOC, a security operations center, is the team that watches a company's systems around the clock. In Ontinue's version, AI agents investigate and act within limits the customer sets, and people keep control of the big decisions. The deal was announced in September; the price wasn't disclosed. What's interesting for you is how they're handling the brands. CEO Kevin Hanes said, "While this transaction is an acquisition, our mindset is that of a merger." Both companies keep their own names for now, and a single, unified portfolio launches in early twenty twenty-seven. That means a rebrand, which means marketing work.

Second, the people behind the money. New Era Capital Partners, an Israeli-American venture fund that invests in very early-stage startups, added two big names to its advisory board: Jake Sullivan, President Biden's former national security adviser, and Rob Bair, who leads Anthropic's work with the cybersecurity industry and used to run cyber operations at the NSA and US Cyber Command. New Era has about forty portfolio companies, and its cyber exits include Apono, sold in a deal valued at two hundred fifty million dollars, Neosec, bought by Akamai, and Suridata, bought by Fortinet. Its third fund, one hundred twenty million dollars, is nearly fully invested, and a slightly bigger fourth fund is in the works. More money for Israeli seed companies means more young companies that need their first marketer.

Third, who could be bought next. A new PitchBook analysis, reported by Fortune, lists software companies likely to be taken private in twenty twenty-seven, meaning bought by investment firms and pulled off the stock market. The one cyber name in the text is Rapid7, the American security company. Its revenue slipped about two and a half percent from a year earlier, but it still made about twenty-six and a half million dollars in free cash flow last quarter. That's exactly what buyers want: steady cash to pay off the debt used in a leveraged buyout, a purchase paid for mostly with borrowed money. It follows Proofpoint's six point three billion dollar deal for Varonis in October. When a public cyber company gets taken private, marketing is often reorganized, so watch these names.

## People moves

Now people moves. I couldn't confirm any new marketing chiefs at cyber companies in the last forty-eight hours, so here are the leadership moves that did happen.

And a harder story from Petah Tikva. CTech reports that Cellebrite, the Israeli digital forensics company, which makes tools police use to pull evidence from phones, plans to lay off dozens of employees as it reorganizes after management changes. Cellebrite has about twelve hundred employees and is worth about two point eight billion dollars. Its CEO, Shiv Ramji, cut the company's outlook in August, citing longer sales cycles, and in September it moved to reincorporate in the US, its biggest market. CTech went through its latest filing: ServiceNow is cutting jobs in the US, Britain and India, with severance costs up about threefold from last year, while setting aside two hundred eleven million dollars in deferred pay to keep key people from the companies it bought. It also bought four other Israeli startups this year. The message is clear: big companies are trimming their own teams and paying a premium to hold on to acquired Israeli talent.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons about how the giants sell, starting with packaging, how a company bundles and prices what it sells.

First, Microsoft just used its Ignite conference to make life harder for a lot of startups. It launched Microsoft Perception, a layer that coordinates three teams of AI agents: red team agents that attack your own systems to find holes, blue team agents that detect and investigate threats, and green team agents that fix things. Color-coding is a smart move, because every security buyer already knows red and blue teams, so the story explains itself. But the real weapon is packaging. Customers on Microsoft's top business bundles, Microsoft three sixty-five E5 and E7, now get all the features of Sentinel, Microsoft's SIEM, the system that collects and analyzes security alerts, plus longer data storage and cheaper data intake. When the biggest player gives something away inside a bundle, a startup selling the same thing can't win on features. It has to win on focus, speed and a sharper story.

Second, listen to how Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto Networks' CEO, explains why he buys companies. In a recent interview with the podcaster David Senra, reported by CTech, he said, "M&A is not a strategy," meaning mergers and acquisitions only fill a gap when he needs to win a category. He only buys the number one or number two player, because they "end up with disproportionate share of the profits." And on price: "If something's worth two hundred million dollars, it's worth eight hundred million," because the goal is to build a five to ten billion dollar business. He negotiates directly with founders, without bankers, and gives them thirty days to agree on a joint product roadmap before signing. Twice, Palo Alto walked away at that stage. For a marketer at a startup, that's the takeaway: being clearly number one or two in a named category is what makes you worth buying, so the category story is part of the exit plan.

Third, Palo Alto itself reports tonight, after the US market closes. Wall Street expects about three point three billion dollars in revenue, up around a third. The key thing to listen for is platformization, Palo Alto's strategy of getting customers to move all their security onto its platform instead of buying from many vendors. Options traders are betting on a twelve percent move in the stock either way, and the shares fell after each of the last four reports, even good ones. That's a lesson in expectations: when your story is "the market leader," a beat isn't enough; you have to beat by more.

Put Microsoft and Palo Alto together and you see the market you'd be marketing into: giants bundling everything, and startups that survive by owning one clear problem better than anyone.
