## Cold open

## Headlines

We start here at home, because tomorrow Israel votes, and the last forty-eight hours of the campaign have been the most intense yet. The biggest story came Sunday night. Channel 12, one of Israel's main TV news channels, reported what it called an "unprecedented confrontation" between Prime Minister Netanyahu and the IDF chief of staff, Eyal Zamir, the army's top commander. According to the report, which was based on anonymous sources and passed through the military censor, the office that can block reporting on security matters, Netanyahu ordered a military action that defense officials feared could quickly escalate into a confrontation with Iran right before the election. Zamir reportedly resisted, asked for more discussion, and requested signed documents showing the legal authorization for the action. A military source called it "an action that could lead to a flare-up with Iran, at a sensitive and dramatic moment."

Netanyahu's answer, on Channel 14: "nonsense." He said there was no fight with Zamir. In the same interview he said he had recently instructed the army and the Mossad, Israel's foreign spy agency, to topple Iran's regime. The opposition reacted furiously. Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief whose party Yashar has led the final polls, backed Zamir and said the army must not become a tool of Netanyahu's campaign. Yair Lapid said Netanyahu wants an escalation to postpone the election. Avigdor Liberman called it treason. Why does this matter so much? In a democracy, the army takes orders from elected leaders. But the question here is whether a military move could be timed for political reasons, and a chief of staff asking for orders in writing is a very unusual signal.

It came on the same day as a post that shocked a lot of people. On Sunday, Netanyahu shared an AI-generated image on X showing a nuclear mushroom cloud over Tel Aviv, with text warning of a "second Holocaust" if Israelis elect a leader who can't confront Iran. Eisenkot called him "hysterical." Yair Golan, who leads the Democrats party, said Netanyahu should be kept away from social media. Axios reported that White House officials were alarmed, worried that Iran could misread the post.

Now the practical part, because tomorrow is a big day. About seven point three million Israelis are eligible to vote, at around eighteen thousand ballot boxes. Polls open at seven in the morning and close at ten at night, and Election Day is a public holiday. Thirty-eight parties are running for the one hundred twenty seats in the Knesset. A party needs three point two five percent of the vote, about four seats, to get in. The exit polls, the TV channels' surveys of voters leaving polling stations, come out at ten. The final polls showed neither bloc near sixty-one seats. Official results are due by November fourth. Then President Herzog picks the lawmaker with the best chance to build a coalition, and that person gets twenty-eight days, plus a possible fourteen-day extension. So it's very possible we won't know who the next prime minister is for weeks.

A few more election notes. The Shin Bet will now protect three opposition leaders, Eisenkot, Golan and Ofer Winter, the retired general who leads the Amcha Yisrael party, because of rising threats. The army announced a closure, meaning a ban on movement, for West Bank Palestinians on Election Day. And bereaved October seventh families plan to stand outside polling stations tomorrow with photos of their loved ones.

Now to the wider Iran picture. On Sunday, Trump said "No. There will not be a strike today." But in a Fox News interview the same day, he asked: "Do I make a deal with them, or do I finish them off?" Iran's foreign ministry says it "has not closed the window of diplomacy," and Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday. But the New York Times reports two Iranian officials confirmed Tehran is preparing to target American diplomats and personnel if war resumes.

Next door, in Egypt, parliament is set to vote today on President Sisi's request to send troops to help defend Saudi Arabia against the Houthis. Sending troops on a combat mission abroad needs a two-thirds majority, meaning at least two-thirds of all lawmakers, so at least three hundred ninety-eight of five hundred ninety-six. Sisi's allies dominate the chamber, so approval is likely. But some lawmakers say they'll abstain because the government hasn't explained how many troops would go, for how long, or at what cost. The Houthis' parliament speaker warned Egypt that joining could threaten its "national security and domestic stability."

Now, Brazil, where we told you yesterday it was a coin flip. It wasn't, in the end. President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, who is eighty, won the runoff, the second-round vote between the top two candidates, with about fifty-one point eight percent, a lead of nearly four point four million votes over Flávio Bolsonaro, the right-wing senator and son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro. It's a historic fourth term, and almost certainly Lula's last election. In his victory speech he called the result a defeat for "denialism and lies," and said "This country will not accept being anyone's backyard," a jab at outside pressure, especially from Washington.

But here's the twist. Flávio Bolsonaro says he will not concede and will ask the courts to annul, meaning cancel, the vote. The fight is over a last-minute decision. Three minutes before polls were supposed to close, Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin extended voting by two hours. Both campaigns had actually asked for it: Lula's side cited bus problems in São Paulo and Salvador, cities that lean his way, and Bolsonaro's side cited heavy rain in the south, where he's strong. But Zanin used to be Lula's personal lawyer, and Bolsonaro now calls the extension "unacceptable." If you remember, Flávio's father refused to clearly concede in twenty twenty-two, and his supporters stormed government buildings in Brasília a week after Lula took office. Lula's new term begins January fifth, once electoral authorities confirm the result. Watch for whether this stays in the courts or moves to the streets.

And finally, a political earthquake in Europe. In Serbia, Aleksandar Vučić, whose party has run the country since twenty twelve, conceded defeat in Sunday's snap election, meaning an early election called before the normal date. "It is clear that we have lost these elections," he said. His own count gave the opposition Student List about one hundred thirty-five to one hundred forty of the two hundred fifty seats, enough for a majority. Turnout was the highest since Yugoslavia broke up in nineteen ninety-two. The movement began in November twenty twenty-four, when a railway station canopy collapsed in the city of Novi Sad and killed sixteen people. Students blamed corruption in public construction, and the protests grew into a national revolt against Vučić's grip on media and the courts. He resigned as president in September to run for prime minister, betting he'd win; he lost. One of the Student List's candidates said: "Let this night be the beginning of a new chapter." Observers did report intimidation at almost a third of the polling stations they watched. Remember Serbia, because we'll come back to it in the cyber segment, with an Israeli angle.

## Jewish world

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the IPO everyone in AI is waiting for: the one from Anthropic, the company behind Claude. An IPO, an initial public offering, is when a private company first sells shares on the stock market. Before a US company lists, it has to file a document called an S-1, a detailed disclosure of its finances and risks. Anthropic filed a confidential draft in June, and the Wall Street Journal has now seen it. The numbers are enormous. Revenue in the first half of this year was sixteen point three billion dollars, up from one point one billion a year earlier. That's roughly fifteen times bigger in a year. Anthropic told investors that third-quarter revenue alone was sixteen point eight billion. But it also reported a net loss, meaning total losses after all costs, of eighty point seven billion dollars for the half. Most of that, about seventy-two billion, is an accounting charge tied to how it raised money, not cash going out the door.

The real cash story is compute, the computing power needed to train and run AI. Anthropic spent nearly nine billion dollars on it in six months, and it has committed to hundreds of billions more over the next decade, including about one hundred eleven billion with Google Cloud and one hundred ten billion with Amazon Web Services. The draft also lists risks: about twenty percent of revenue comes from just two customers, and a catastrophic AI incident could cost more than its insurance covers. Reuters reported a possible listing in mid-November at a valuation around one and a half trillion dollars; Bloomberg has reported up to two trillion. But nothing is official until the public filing, and that hasn't been filed yet.

Second, a story about what powers all those AI agents. Modal, a New York company, rents out computing power for AI on demand, so customers don't have to buy their own expensive chips. In case you missed it earlier this month, Modal raised seven hundred fifty million dollars in a Series D, a late-stage funding round for a company already growing fast, led by Accel, at a post-money valuation of fifteen point seven five billion dollars. Post-money means the company's value including the new cash. That's more than three times its value in May. Its annual run rate just passed one billion dollars. The interesting part is its product called Sandboxes: safe, isolated spaces where AI-written code can run without touching anything else. Sandboxes alone are now a roughly three hundred million dollar run-rate business. Why? Because every coding agent needs somewhere safe to test its code.

Third, robotaxis are coming to Europe. Wayve, the British self-driving startup, Stellantis, the carmaker behind Fiat, Peugeot and Jeep, and Nvidia, the chip giant, announced, alongside the French government, a partnership to bring self-driving cars to France. Stellantis builds the cars, Wayve supplies the AI driver, and Nvidia provides the computing platform. France's transport minister, Philippe Tabarot, said the rules will adapt while keeping strict safety and data standards. They're targeting a hands-free driving launch in North America in twenty twenty-eight.

And a follow-up on Netanyahu's idea we told you about yesterday, to build an "alternative economy" with crypto, digital currencies like Bitcoin, in case Israel faces international banking sanctions. On Sunday, one hundred nine economists and former senior officials signed a letter criticizing it, calling the idea "puzzling." The Bank of Israel, the central bank, had already said crypto can't be used to get around sanctions. For Israeli tech, the worry is simple: the industry runs on foreign investors and normal banking, and any talk of going it alone can make that money nervous.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cyber and go-to-market. First, a new Israeli startup with a big idea about trust. Modelyo, founded in twenty twenty-four, raised about seventeen million dollars, led by the American firm OlivTree. It's building what it calls a "Sovereign Confidential Cloud." The idea: governments, banks, hospitals and defense agencies want to use cloud services and AI, but they don't want the cloud provider to be able to see their data. Modelyo uses confidential computing, technology that keeps data encrypted even while it's being processed, plus zero trust, the principle that no user or system is trusted by default. Customers keep their own encryption keys. The CEO, chief executive, Benny Meir, a reserve lieutenant colonel with more than twenty years in special operations, has the perfect marketing line: "Instead of asking customers to trust that their data is protected, we give them the tools to prove it cryptographically." Notice the positioning: not "trust us," but "you don't have to." For a security brand, that's a powerful promise.

Second, a breach that hits close to your job search. Bullhorn, an American company whose software runs more than ten thousand staffing and recruiting firms worldwide, took its platform offline after a cyberattack. Bullhorn's main product is an ATS and CRM. ATS stands for applicant tracking system, the software recruiters use to track candidates, and CRM, customer relationship management, is software for tracking contacts and deals. So it holds a lot of résumés and contact details. Bullhorn says it isolated the threat and is restoring service with help from CrowdStrike, but it still hasn't determined whether any data was stolen. Your takeaway: when recruiters reach out over the next few weeks, be extra careful with links and unusual requests. Breached recruiting data is perfect material for fake job offers.

Third, the Israeli angle on Serbia we promised. Citizen Lab, a research group at the University of Toronto that investigates spyware, confirmed with "high confidence" that the phone of Vladan Djokic, rector, meaning head, of the University of Belgrade, was infected this year with Pegasus. Pegasus is spyware made by Israel's NSO Group that can secretly take over a phone, reading messages and even turning on the microphone. His university was at the heart of the student protests. NSO says it sells only to government agencies to fight serious crime and terrorism; Serbian authorities have denied being behind past attacks like this. This matters for Israel's cyber industry, because every Pegasus case adds to pressure on Israeli spyware exports, and it's a reputation issue the whole sector has to market around.

Fourth, a developer platform under attack. Railway, a popular cloud platform where developers deploy apps, says an unauthorized party may have accessed customers' encrypted environment variables, the hidden settings where apps store passwords and keys, and decrypted some of them. Railway believes it's part of a wider campaign targeting AI inference providers, the companies that run AI models for customers. It paused deployments and told customers to change their credentials. The pattern is clear: attackers are going after the plumbing of the AI boom, because that's where the valuable keys are.

## Business story

Now our business story, about a New York startup that began as a tiny app idea and just sold for almost six billion dollars. In twenty eleven, three founders, Bill Magnuson, Jon Hyman and Mark Ghermezian, started a company in New York called Appboy. The iPhone app boom was in full swing, and their idea was simple: help apps talk to their users. Remind someone they left something in their cart, welcome a new user, send the right message at the right moment. They started with about three million dollars from investors.

The company grew with mobile. In twenty seventeen, after raising a fifty million dollar round, it renamed itself Braze, as it moved beyond apps into email, web and text messages. In twenty eighteen it was valued at eight hundred fifty million dollars. By twenty nineteen it passed one hundred million in annual recurring revenue. And in November twenty twenty-one, right at the peak of the tech stock boom, Braze went public on the Nasdaq at sixty-five dollars a share. On day one the stock jumped nearly forty percent, and the company was worth more than eight billion dollars.

Today Braze calls itself a customer engagement platform. In plain words, it's the software big consumer brands use to send you personalized messages across your phone, email, text and even WhatsApp, based on what you just did. It works for B2C companies, business-to-consumer brands that sell to the public, and its customers include Canva, Instacart, Venmo, Equinox, Urban Outfitters and Dairy Queen. It processes trillions of messages a year.

Then, last Tuesday, Salesforce, the giant of business software, announced it will buy Braze for about five point six six billion dollars in enterprise value, meaning the price for the whole business, including its debt and cash. Shareholders get forty-seven fifty a share in cash. That's a fifty-eight percent premium, meaning that much above the stock price the day before. Patrick Stokes, who runs applications and marketing at Salesforce, said: "Braze was built to turn live customer behavior into personalized engagement at scale." CEO Bill Magnuson promised: "we'll continue building as Braze."

Here's the twist worth noticing. Forty-seven fifty is a big premium, but it's still below Braze's sixty-five dollar IPO price five years ago. The day before the deal, the stock closed at about thirty dollars, less than half its IPO price. For Salesforce, it's a bet that AI-powered, real-time marketing is the future. It's more than double the roughly two and a half billion dollars Salesforce paid in twenty thirteen for ExactTarget, which became its Marketing Cloud. The deal needs shareholder and regulator approval and should close in twenty twenty-seven.

Why it's for you: this is the world of lifecycle and growth marketing you know. The tools you use to run journeys and campaigns are consolidating into a few giants. Knowing Braze, Salesforce Marketing Cloud and how they fit together is a real skill to put on the table in interviews.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, and a painter whose market could be about to explode. Hilma af Klint was a Swedish artist, born in eighteen sixty-two, who painted bold abstract works, meaning art that doesn't show recognizable objects, years before the famous male pioneers of abstraction, but she was barely recognized in her lifetime. Her big moment came in twenty eighteen, when a Guggenheim show in New York drew more than six hundred thousand visitors. Now, on November nineteenth, the Swedish auction house Bukowskis in Stockholm will sell one of her geometric oil paintings from nineteen twenty, called "Ser Three Number Five," estimated at six hundred to eight hundred thousand dollars. Her current auction record is only about one hundred sixty-six thousand dollars, for a watercolor. Why so low for such a famous name? Supply. A foundation set up by her nephew tightly controls her abstract paintings, so almost none ever reach the market. This one was deaccessioned, meaning officially sold off from the collection, back in nineteen ninety-five. It's a perfect lesson in art market value: fame plus scarcity.

Next, a building made for just one painting. In Künzelsau, a small town in southern Germany, the Würth Collection has opened a pavilion designed by the British architect David Chipperfield for a single work by Anselm Kiefer, the German artist famous for huge, heavy paintings using materials like soil and twigs. The painting, "Schwarze Flocken," "Black Flakes," from two thousand seven, is eleven meters high, so big it had never been shown.

## Wellness

For wellness, something practical for this week. Israel turned its clocks back an hour over the weekend, but the US doesn't do it until this Sunday, November first. So for this one week, New York is only six hours behind you, not the usual seven. Good to know when you call home. And to adjust to the darker evenings, sleep experts give one main tip: keep your regular wake-up time, even if you feel like sleeping in. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine also says an extra hour won't fix a chronic sleep debt, so treat the change as a reset, not a bonus. Then get outside into daylight early in the morning. Morning light is the strongest signal your body clock uses to set itself, so a short walk before work, especially with the earlier sunsets now, can help you feel more alert during the day and sleepier at night.
