Good morning Sophie. It's Sunday, November fifteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. The White House says Trump will meet Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin together in China on Thursday, the first meeting of its kind. And SpaceX caught a spaceship coming back from orbit with giant mechanical arms, a first in history. Let's go.

## Israel

We start here at home, where the coalition chess game moves into a room today. Negotiating teams from Likud and from Yashar, Eisenkot's party, are scheduled to meet this Sunday. Not the two leaders themselves, just their teams. And almost nobody expects a deal.

Here's how we got here. On Saturday evening, Prime Minister Netanyahu publicly invited Eisenkot to talks on a unity government made up of all the Jewish-majority parties. "Let us go into the room, without preconditions," he said. His argument: a government that relies on Arab parties can't stand up to Iran, which he claims is working to rebuild its nuclear weapons program. Notably, unlike his earlier calls, he didn't say out loud that he must be the one to lead it. But a senior Likud minister told Ynet that Netanyahu would insist on going first in any rotation agreement, meaning a deal where two leaders take turns as prime minister.

Eisenkot's answer came about an hour later, and it was blunt. He called the offer "another spin," pointing out that the two teams had already scheduled today's meeting. "Nice try," he said, "but these tricks don't work on me." Eisenkot holds the mandate, so his position is simple: he's open to Likud joining a government he leads, without Netanyahu, if Likud breaks with what he calls the "extremists." He won't negotiate with Itamar Ben-Gvir's Otzma Yehudit at all.

Why is this so hard? The math. Eisenkot's four-party bloc has forty-eight seats. Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three. The two Arab parties, the Joint List and Ra'am, have nineteen. Nobody has sixty-one. Ynet describes Eisenkot working three tracks at once. With the ultra-Orthodox parties, talks are mostly behind closed doors, with no breakthrough; Eisenkot says "there is more hidden than revealed." With the Arab parties, the talks are only about Knesset votes, not about joining a government. And with Likud, today's meeting is seen as more symbolic than practical.

The ultra-Orthodox track matters most, because Avigdor Liberman, whose seats Eisenkot needs, refuses to join a minority government that depends on Arab votes. But the gap on the draft is huge. One opposition source told Ynet that the most the bloc can offer "does not even come close to the minimum demands of the ultra-Orthodox parties." If that track fails, the likely last resort is a minority government that survives because Arab lawmakers abstain, meaning they don't vote against it.

Meanwhile, the pressure campaign from the right is getting creative. Bezalel Smotrich, leader of Religious Zionism, the pro-settler party, called on Eisenkot to accept Netanyahu's offer, and Likud lawmaker Elisha Medan pitched a protest tent outside the Knesset, writing that he'll stay "day and night, in the cold and the rain."

What's next: Tuesday is the first real test of power. Eisenkot's bloc plans a vote to replace Likud's Amir Ohana as Knesset speaker with Yashar lawmaker Matan Kahana. Liberman is also expected to take over the Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, the Knesset's most powerful security panel. Eisenkot's deadline to form a government is December sixth, with a possible two-week extension.

Now a painful story from Arab Israeli society. On Saturday, someone allegedly threw a grenade into a pastry shop in Kafr Manda, a town in the Galilee. Lina Qassem Zidan, fifty-one, was killed and three others were hurt. Police arrested three suspects. Hours later, a fifty-nine-year-old man, Mohammad Yazan, was shot dead in Taibeh. President Herzog said Zidan was the two hundredth murder victim in the Arab community this year, a number he called "chilling and incomprehensible." He urged the next government to act immediately against criminal organizations and illegal weapons. "We must intensify the fight against this crime," he said. Arab community leaders have long said the state isn't doing enough against the crime gangs, so watch whether this becomes part of the talks with the Arab parties.

And one bright economic note. According to new International Monetary Fund projections, the IMF being the global body that tracks and lends to economies, Israel's GDP, the total value of everything the economy produces, will hit about seven hundred forty-seven billion dollars this year. That makes Israel the world's twenty-fifth largest economy, up from twenty-seventh, passing Sweden, despite the war.

## World

Now to the world, starting with a big diplomatic first. The White House says President Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin will hold their first-ever three-way meeting on Thursday in Shenzhen, in southern China, on the sidelines of APEC, the yearly summit of Pacific Rim economies. Trump leaves Washington on Monday, stops in the Philippines on Tuesday for the ASEAN summit, the Southeast Asian nations' meeting, and then flies to China. He'll also have a working lunch with Xi, their fourth meeting since he returned to office.

What's on the table? Nuclear weapons, first. White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump "will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world." A senior official said Trump also intends to raise Iran. What's not on the table: Ukraine, which is handled on a separate track, and American chip export rules. "We do not negotiate American export controls," an official said. Elon Musk is traveling with Trump but won't be in the room.

Speaking of Iran, the signals from Washington are softening, at least for now. Reuters reports, citing three sources, that Trump told advisers he may hold off on new attacks on Iran if there are no new provocations from Tehran or from the Houthis in Yemen. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and Turkish President Erdogan both urged him not to restart the bombing. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said more strikes aren't needed because Iran's economic pain is already severe. Reuters adds two important details: Israeli officials are still lobbying for another military campaign, and a Reuters-Ipsos poll found fifty-five percent of Americans say the war wasn't worth the cost.

But Trump also raised the bar for what counts as winning. In a radio interview with Hugh Hewitt on Thursday, he was asked if it would be a defeat if Iran's regime is still in power when he leaves office. "Yes, that would be a defeat," he said. "Absolutely."

On the water, it's still dangerous. On Saturday, Iran's navy said an oil tanker caught fire after hitting a naval mine in the eastern Strait of Hormuz. And the UN's nuclear watchdog, the IAEA, says it's "increasingly concerned" that Iran won't let inspectors into the Isfahan site, where about nine hundred pounds of highly enriched uranium, nuclear fuel refined close to bomb grade, is believed to be buried. Oil is feeling it: Brent crude was near ninety-six dollars a barrel on Friday, versus about seventy-two before the war.

A quick follow-up on Friday's American air travel shutdown. The FAA lifted its ground stop, the order that kept every plane on the ground, after more than four hours, the longest in twenty-five years. Thousands of flights were canceled. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed "old hardware," overloaded switches and routers, the equipment that moves data between computers. Homeland Security officials said there was no sign of a cyberattack and no link to the Microsoft outage. Duffy says it's "safe to fly," but expect delays for a few days.

And one quick one. In Alaska, Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the governor's race, the first Democrat to win it since nineteen ninety-eight, beating three Republicans, including one Trump endorsed.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where we're learning more about the Flatbush building collapse. Eight people were killed, including two children, and eighteen were hurt, after a three-story apartment building on Flatbush Avenue fell into a construction pit next door on Wednesday. City Limits found the excavation site had been cited twice this year, once in March for demolition without required safety protection and once in April for working during a stop-work order. The collapsed building itself had ninety-eight open housing violations. Mayor Mamdani says the city has identified six companies involved at the site, and inspectors are now checking about one hundred sixty other sites where those companies work. The Brooklyn district attorney has opened an investigation, according to the New York Times. At a vigil on Friday, Reverend Al Sharpton said, "This could have been any one of us."

And something lovely for the holidays: the New York Transit Museum's Holiday Train Show opened Friday at Grand Central for its twenty-second season. It's free, runs through February, and features a thirty-four-foot, two-level model train layout with tiny subway cars, a Polar Express, and miniature New York landmarks, including CBGB, the famous East Village punk club, with a little statue of the Ramones.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world. Israel lost the last Righteous Among the Nations living in the country earlier this week. That's the title Yad Vashem gives non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews in the Holocaust. Jaroslawa Lewicka died at ninety-one in Haifa. As a little girl in Złoczów, she slipped into the Jewish ghetto carrying a school bag filled with food, getting past German and Ukrainian police, while her mother and grandfather helped Jews trapped inside. She later moved to Israel and became a citizen in nineteen ninety-five.

A creative way to teach that history: Steven Van Zandt, the guitarist from Bruce Springsteen's E Street Band, has a new gig in Holocaust education. His music-based school program, TeachRock, launched a four-day lesson called "Jewish Heritage and the Holocaust: Exploring an Enduring Culture Through Music." Day one opens with KISS's "Rock and Roll All Nite," and students learn that KISS frontman Gene Simmons is the son of a Holocaust survivor. Van Zandt, who isn't Jewish, says he's alarmed by antisemitic misinformation online. "Show business doesn't exist without the Jewish contributions," he told JTA. It was piloted with fifty high school students at the Florida Holocaust Museum.

And on Broadway, "Here There Are Blueberries" just opened at the Ethel Barrymore Theatre for ten weeks. It tells the true story of a photo album delivered to the US Holocaust Memorial Museum in two thousand seven, showing Auschwitz officers relaxing and laughing, and the archivists who uncovered who they were. Critics are mostly glowing; one called it "a masterpiece."

## Tech

Next, tech, and your AI may soon do your shopping. On Friday, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet. ChatGPT's agents, which it calls "dots," can now help you pick products and actually check out for you. For now it's limited to a small group of American Pro subscribers with US-issued cards. Here's how it works: you add your card once, and the dot pays using a payment token, a stand-in code, so neither the agent nor the store sees your real card number. You still review and approve every purchase with Face ID or a fingerprint. OpenAI plans to add spending limits so you can pre-approve some purchases. The catch: many retailers block AI agents, and Amazon, the biggest of all, bans them and pushes its own assistant, Alexa. From a marketer's angle, this is huge: if agents do the buying, brands will have to persuade software, not just people.

## Business story

Our business story is a rocket that came home. On Saturday at Starbase, SpaceX's site in south Texas, a Starship spaceship returned from orbit and was caught in midair by the launch tower's giant mechanical "chopstick" arms. SpaceX had caught its booster, the lower stage of the rocket, three times before, but never the spaceship itself, which falls faster and from much higher up.

The mission was the fifteenth Starship test flight. The ship orbited Earth fourteen times over twenty-two hours and released twenty-six Starlink satellites, the ones that power Musk's satellite internet. Not everything went perfectly: the booster had a technical problem on its way down and was diverted into the Gulf of Mexico instead of being caught. Still, NASA chief Jared Isaacman called the recovery "almost magic," and a SpaceX spokesman said, "We caught a Starship and now, everything changes."

Why is this a business story? Because reuse is the whole business model. A rocket you catch and fly again is like an airplane, not a fireworks show, and that's what can make launches cheaper and more frequent. SpaceX says it can now build a new spaceship every two weeks and a booster every four. Wall Street is paying close attention: SpaceX raised about eighty-five point seven billion dollars when it went public in June. Big hurdles remain, especially refueling in orbit, which Starship needs to reach the Moon as NASA's lunar lander for Artemis, the US program to return astronauts there.

## Art

Next, art, and a fight over a masterpiece that touches the Jewish world too. On Wednesday night, Sotheby's in New York plans to sell Van Gogh's "Blossoming Chestnut Trees," painted in eighteen ninety and not seen in public for more than sixty years. It's expected to fetch around one hundred eighty million dollars, the highest estimate of the season, which could set a new auction record for Van Gogh. Sotheby's has guaranteed it, meaning the seller is promised a minimum price.

Now the descendants of its Jewish former owners want the sale stopped. Samuel Fischer, a famous German publisher, bought the painting in nineteen oh nine. After his death, as the Nazis banned many of the family firm's authors, the family left it with a dealer, who sold it to a Paris collector in nineteen thirty-six. The heirs' lawyer, Gunnar Schnabel, says the family was forced to pay the "Reich flight tax," a Nazi levy on people fleeing Germany, and that no auction should happen unless Sotheby's proves it was a fair sale. He says they're open to a solution under the Washington Principles, non-binding guidelines for returning Nazi-looted art. Sotheby's says its research "has not established that the painting was sold under duress," but that it wants a resolution both sides can accept. Watch Wednesday night.

## Travel

Now travel, and an update for anyone who used to fly east through Dubai. Today, a Transportation Ministry delegation visits the UAE to be briefed on the investigation into the September thirtieth incident on flydubai flight one-zero-seven-three. That's the Dubai to Tel Aviv flight where, according to Israeli security officials, the Omani co-pilot is suspected of trying to crash the plane. The captain, crew and Israeli passengers fought him off, and the plane, with one hundred seventy-four people on board, made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia. After that, flydubai suspended its Tel Aviv flights, and as of last month Israeli airlines weren't allowed to fly to the UAE either.

Why it matters: Dubai was Israelis' favorite gateway to Asia and Africa. Between January and August this year, eight hundred thirty-seven thousand passengers flew between Israel and the UAE, about half on flydubai. Since the incident, the main direct option has been Etihad to Abu Dhabi, or connecting through Europe at a higher cost. After today's briefing, the ministry will decide whether flydubai can come back, so if you're planning a winter trip to Asia, hold off on booking a Dubai connection for a few more days.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with a twist for your workouts. A new review from Semmelweis University in Budapest, published in Sports Medicine Open, pooled twenty-four studies with more than twelve hundred healthy adults. It was a meta-analysis, a study that combines many earlier studies. The researchers looked at blood markers that show new bone being built. Sports with bursts, quick turns and changes of direction, like recreational soccer and handball, raised those markers by up to about forty-six percent. Walking, jogging and standard gym weight training showed smaller or no significant effects. Two caveats: these markers aren't the same as proven stronger bones, and a big analysis in the British Medical Journal this fall did find brisk walking and running improve bone density. So keep walking, and try adding something bouncy, like a dance class, tennis or a friendly soccer game, building up slowly.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

It's been a quiet weekend for brand-new cyber deals, so today we'll catch up on two Israeli money stories you may have missed, then spend more time on how companies are selling.

First, the biggest number in Israeli cyber right now. Calcalist reported this week that Cyera, the Israeli data security company, is raising about eight hundred million dollars at a valuation of roughly twenty billion dollars. That's nearly double its twelve billion valuation from June. The round is in advanced closing, so these are reported terms, not announced ones. Cyera was founded in twenty twenty-one by Yotam Segev, Tamar Bar-Ilan and Yonatan Itai, all graduates of Talpiot, the army's elite science program, and Unit 8200 veterans. It started by helping companies find and protect their sensitive data, and has since expanded into securing AI agents. Its estimated annual recurring revenue is around three hundred million dollars, which means investors are paying roughly sixty-five times revenue. It has also made six acquisitions since twenty twenty-four, worth about one point three five billion dollars combined, including Oasis Security for about a billion.

The surprising part is who's writing the biggest check. Cyberstarts, a fund best known for backing Israeli founders at the very earliest stage, is putting in about three hundred million dollars. Calcalist says that reflects how close the fund is to Cyera's founders. For you, the signal is simple: Cyera is now a company of more than fifteen hundred people in eighteen countries, and money like this pays for expansion into new regions and new product lines, each of which needs marketing.

Second, a seed round in case you missed it. Brava, a Tel Aviv company, came out of stealth with fifteen million dollars, led by F2 Venture Capital and Pitango. It was founded in twenty twenty-four by Idan Basre and Itay Gabbay, veterans of Unit 8200 and Microsoft. Here's what it does in plain words: instead of running a real attack to test your defenses, Brava's AI generates the telemetry, the digital traces an attack would leave behind, and checks whether your security tools would notice. It then rewrites detection rules in the SIEM, the system where security teams collect and search their alerts. It says customers see first results ten to fifteen minutes after connecting. Fifteen million dollars is a big seed round, a sign investors expect fast growth.

And one more Israeli deal to keep on your radar. Alongside its talks to buy Clutch Security, Palo Alto Networks is reportedly negotiating to buy Beacon Security, a two-year-old Israeli startup whose platform lets security teams build AI agents that investigate and respond to threats. Beacon raised a thirteen million dollar seed round in July and says its annual recurring revenue grew more than three hundred percent in the first half of the year. Neither deal has been announced.

Now people moves. I couldn't confirm any new marketing chiefs at cyber companies over the weekend. The one notable name from SecurityWeek's weekly list is Jaya Baloo, who became the first CISO of Cohere, the Canadian AI company that builds models for businesses. She came from AISLE, the cyber startup Cohere recently bought, where she was a co-founder and chief operating officer. Why it matters to a marketer: when AI companies sell to banks and governments, a well-known security chief is part of the sales pitch. She'll be on stages and in customer meetings, helping prove the product can be trusted.

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, partnerships as distribution. On Friday, SiliconANGLE published an exclusive: Onyx Security, the Israeli startup that governs AI agents inside big companies, is integrating with Glean, an enterprise AI platform valued at seven point two billion dollars last year. Glean helps companies build agents; Onyx finds them, labels their risk and enforces the rules. Onyx calls its product a "secure AI control plane," meaning a central dashboard that controls every agent from one place. Notice three smart moves. One: it's ridden a bigger platform's customer base instead of hunting every customer alone, which is classic co-selling, two companies selling together. Two: it gave the news to one outlet as an exclusive, which usually buys a deeper story. Three: both sides delivered quotes that sell the same idea. Glean's VP of partnerships said context and governance are "foundational to making enterprise AI both useful and trustworthy." For a young company, that quote from a partner is worth more than any ad.

Second, a lesson from a strange week of outages. Microsoft's cloud went down for more than a day, and then a hardware failure grounded American flights. In both cases, officials quickly said there was no sign of a cyberattack. That's a test for cyber marketers. The temptation is newsjacking, jumping on a big headline to promote your product. But scaring buyers about an attack that didn't happen can badly damage trust. The smarter angle is resilience: how fast can you recover when systems fail, for any reason? That's exactly the pitch of Israeli startups like Dreeze, which we covered last week.

Third, category expansion. Cyera started as a data security company. Now it tells investors it secures AI agents too, with products like Agent Guardian, and it bought companies to back that up. That's how a company justifies a bigger valuation: by telling a bigger story, and then proving it with products. If you interview there, ask how marketing explains one brand that now covers several categories.

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Brava. A fresh seed round, research and development in Tel Aviv and a commercial team in the US. Companies at this stage often hire their first marketing lead to build the brand and generate demand from scratch, a role your growth background fits well.

Second, Onyx Security. Over one hundred fifty million dollars raised, a six hundred forty million dollar valuation as of its July round, and now a strategy built on partners like Glean. That points to partner marketing and product marketing roles.

Third, Cyera. At twenty billion dollars, it's no longer a startup, but it's still growing fast, and a company covering several categories at once needs product marketers for each one.
