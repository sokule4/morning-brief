## Cold open

## Israel

We start here in Israel, where the coalition puzzle got a new shape on Wednesday.

Quick reminder of the math. In the October twenty-seventh election, Netanyahu's bloc, Likud with the far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties, won fifty-three of the Knesset's one hundred twenty seats. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, and its partners hold forty-eight. The Arab parties hold nineteen, and they oppose Netanyahu. Nobody has sixty-one, the number you need for a majority. Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in. President Herzog gave Eisenkot the first chance to form a government, and his time runs out on December sixth, with a possible fourteen-day extension.

On Tuesday night, as you heard yesterday, Eisenkot and Liberman agreed to sign a coalition deal. On Wednesday, Liberman went on Kan Reshet Bet, the public radio station, and said his talks with Yashar had "effectively concluded." Then he added the line everyone is quoting: "There will be no minority government; there will be a government with a Jewish majority." That rules out Eisenkot's fallback plan, a government that survives because the Arab parties don't vote against it. Liberman also said there is progress with United Torah Judaism, one of the two ultra-Orthodox parties.

That fits a report from JNS, the Jewish News Syndicate. It says United Torah Judaism, Shas, the other Haredi party, and Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu are discussing an alternative government together. Yashar has even asked for an official from the Finance Ministry to join the talks about funding for the Haredi community. The two hardest issues are money and military service for yeshiva students, and JNS says Liberman, who has fought for decades to draft Haredi men, has softened his stance. JNS added that it couldn't independently confirm every detail, and that Netanyahu and senior Likud figures are working to block a deal like this.

Meanwhile, the talks with Likud keep going. Yashar and Likud held their fourth round of unity talks in two weeks. According to the Jerusalem Post, Monday's four-hour meeting didn't even get to rotation, the question of who serves as prime minister first. Tuesday's two hours focused on the courts and the justice system. Netanyahu sounded warm: "There is a desire to reach agreements that will put an end to the division among us." But a Likud source told the Jerusalem Post, "It's all a show. Yashar does not really want a unity government."

And the left is pulling the other way. Yair Golan, whose Democrats halted talks on Tuesday, is reportedly demanding one of the three top ministries: Defense, Foreign Affairs or Finance. Yashar reportedly offered him national security instead, and the Democrats said no. So Eisenkot now has a partner on his right, Liberman, saying no to any reliance on the Arab parties, and a partner on his left, Golan, who prefers exactly that. What's next: ten days on the clock, and the Haredi track is suddenly the one to watch.

On Gaza, Netanyahu spoke on Tuesday with Jared Kushner, the US envoy, about the humanitarian situation there. An Israeli official said they "agreed to continue working together." That call came after Kushner warned in an interview that Israel is heading toward becoming a "global pariah," a country the world shuns, if its approach to Gaza doesn't change. Kushner designed the Board of Peace, which oversees the fragile Gaza truce, and the board has accused Netanyahu's government of blocking its plan to disarm Hamas.

And on Iran, Defense Minister Israel Katz said, "Iran is in the process of developing chemical weapons," and he raised the possibility of a preemptive strike, an attack to stop a threat before it happens. Iran denies it.

## World

Now to the world, and the war in the Red Sea is spreading along Saudi Arabia's coast.

Yesterday we told you the Saudi-led coalition ordered civilians out of Hodeidah, the Houthis' main port in Yemen. On Wednesday it struck. The coalition says it hit military targets at Hodeidah and in the Saada and Amran provinces in the north. Why does Hodeidah matter so much? About ninety percent of Yemen's imported goods come through it, so strikes there touch food and fuel for millions of people.

The Houthis hit back along the coast, just as they had threatened. They struck the Saudi port of Jizan, on the Red Sea, which the coalition says caused minor damage and no civilian casualties. A drone was shot down over the port of Jeddah, Saudi Arabia's biggest Red Sea port. Saudi air defenses also intercepted ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, and four drones over Ras Tanura, the giant oil export hub on the Gulf coast. And Tuesday's drone strike on a skyscraper in Riyadh's financial district injured at least eleven people, including two Indian citizens.

How did we get here? The Houthis are an Iran-backed movement that controls northern Yemen. In July they declared a blockade of Saudi ports. In September they seized territory near Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow strait at the bottom of the Red Sea. And last month, Yemen's government forces, backed by Saudi Arabia, launched a counteroffensive. With the Strait of Hormuz already crippled by the Iran war, the Red Sea is the backup route for world trade, and now both are under fire.

In Washington, President Trump told the New York Post that the Iran war is funded "up until really the end of January, pretty much," and warned that ending it early could turn a "total win" into "a loss." At the UN Security Council, Iran's ambassador claimed the US used AI tools, naming Palantir's Maven system and Anthropic's Claude, to hit two thousand targets in ninety-six hours. Those are Iran's claims, as reported by CBS News.

And a big verdict in Brooklyn. A federal jury convicted Huawei, the Chinese tech giant, on ten of twelve counts after a trial of more than two months. It was acquitted of the most serious one, racketeering conspiracy, the law used against organized crime. The Justice Department says Huawei helped an Iranian intelligence apparatus. Huawei says the charges "lack legal merit," and sentencing is set for next September.

In US politics, a surprising meeting. Trump says he'll meet Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic senator-elect from Michigan, a doctor and the first Muslim elected to the Senate. El-Sayed has proposed a single, low-cost government health plan, and offered to call it "Trumpcare." Trump called him "a smart guy." Remember the new math: Democrats will hold fifty-six Senate seats and two hundred fifty-eight House seats when the new Congress meets in January. That's a majority, but not enough to override a veto, a president's power to block a bill, which needs sixty-seven senators. Trump's plan, in his words: "I'm just going to veto everything there is, and then at some point, I think they're going to want to negotiate."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where history was made before a single balloon took off. On Wednesday afternoon, while the giant balloons were being inflated next to the Museum of Natural History, Macy's, NBC and the city announced that the one hundredth Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade is moving to Saturday, November twenty-eighth. In a joint statement they cited "extreme weather conditions" and the safety of "participants and spectators."

It's the first time in the parade's history that weather has pushed it off Thanksgiving Day. The parade was canceled during World War Two, from nineteen forty-two to nineteen forty-five, and the balloons were grounded once, in nineteen seventy-one, but it has never moved days before. The reason is a high wind warning, a rare alert the city last saw in April twenty twenty-four. The National Weather Service expects gusts up to fifty-eight miles an hour and sustained winds up to forty. The balloon rule bans flying above twenty-three miles an hour sustained, or thirty-four in gusts, so today was never going to work.

The new plan: Saturday, starting at eight thirty in the morning and running until noon, on the usual route from Seventy-Seventh Street down to Herald Square. Organizers say they're still working out the logistics. For anyone in the city today, NYC Emergency Management warns of possible widespread power outages, so charge your phone before the turkey goes in.

## Business story

Our business story is Black Friday, which is tomorrow, and this year there's a new kind of shopper: AI. Salesforce, which tracks data from thousands of online stores, forecasts that global online sales during Black Friday will hit three hundred fifty-seven billion dollars, about six percent more than last year, which would make it the biggest ever. And it estimates AI agents, assistants that search, compare and recommend for you, will influence about one fifth of those sales, nearly seventy-six billion dollars.

The details are fascinating for a marketer. Traffic to retail sites that comes from AI tools has more than doubled in a year. Shoppers sent by an AI tool are about twice as likely to buy. People who use AI while shopping spend about eight percent more and buy twenty-three percent more items. One in three shoppers discovered a new brand through an AI assistant. But here's the catch: seventy-four percent say they'd trust an AI recommendation, yet only eight percent let an AI actually make the purchase for them. People let AI do the research, but they still want to click "buy" themselves.

Caila Schwartz, who studies agentic commerce, shopping done with the help of AI agents, at Salesforce, put it simply: "Consumers are turning to personal agents to discover gifts and find the best deals." Meta is already moving. This week it put its AI assistant into Instagram direct messages and partnered with Walmart on Black Friday deals.

Why does this matter to you? The old marketing funnel started with a Google search or an Instagram ad. Now, more and more, it starts with a chatbot's answer. Brands that show up in those answers win, and that is quickly becoming its own marketing job.

## Pop culture, celebs and fashion

Now pop culture, and the Taylor Swift story has flipped. On Monday, Vanity Fair published "The Mastermind," the long piece by May Jeong built on more than a hundred sources that never names Swift. By Wednesday, the story was the backlash against the magazine.

Chris Willman, Variety's chief music critic, wrote a column asking whether the piece is "really journalism," and called Monday "a bad day for journalism." Piers Morgan called it the nastiest hatchet job, a deliberately damaging attack piece, he'd ever read in an elite magazine. And Dan Wakeford, the former editor of People, made the sharpest media point. He said the piece is written like a blind item, the gossip format where you hint at a star without naming her. But a blind item only works when readers don't know who it's about, and here, he said, "everybody knows," so the device "just looks timid."

The detail that went viral wasn't a feud. It was a former classmate's memory of Swift wearing Ugg boots in Pennsylvania, which turned into a meme on TikTok and X. One PR expert said that's exactly the problem: if the most shareable moment is the boots, the material was thin. And the Swifties, Swift's famously organized fans, mounted a defense within hours. Swift still hasn't commented, and Vanity Fair didn't respond to Fox News. For a marketer, it's a lesson in brand loyalty: when your fans fight for you, a hundred anonymous sources can look smaller than one community.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and good news if you can't do long, hard workouts. A study published in PLOS One followed more than ten thousand university students in Norway for a year. The researchers found that how often people exercised was the most consistent link to better mental health, more than how hard or how long they worked out. Students who exercised less were more likely to meet the criteria for anxiety or depression a year later, and the benefit kept growing up to about ten hours of activity a week. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. But the takeaway is easy: a short walk or a twenty-minute class most days may do more for your mood than one heroic workout a week. A second small study, from the University of Missouri, found older adults with a regular daily routine reported less pain and fewer depressive symptoms. The researcher said, "When we regulate our circadian rhythm well, our bodies function well." Circadian rhythm is your body's internal twenty-four-hour clock.

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a quiet Thanksgiving week for new cyber rounds, so today's money story is a big one in the making, and it's Israeli.

Globes reported on Tuesday that Eon, the Israeli cloud backup company, is in talks to raise money at a valuation of six billion dollars. The round is expected to total several hundred million dollars, and it's being led by Georgian, a Canadian venture fund that already invested in Eon and has also backed Cyera and Island. Eon hasn't commented.

What does Eon do? Big companies keep huge amounts of data in the cloud, on Amazon, Microsoft and Google. Eon backs that data up, so it can be recovered after a cyberattack or an outage, and it also lets companies search and use their backups, for example to feed AI tools. It was founded by Ofir Ehrlich, Ron Kimchi and Gonen Stein, who sold their earlier company, CloudEndure, to Amazon Web Services and then spent four years inside AWS.

Now, is six billion big? Look at the climb. In twenty twenty-four Eon raised two hundred million dollars over three rounds, and its valuation went from two hundred million to one point four billion by the end of that year. Late last year it raised three hundred million dollars in a round led by the investor Elad Gil, at about four billion according to PitchBook. Globes estimates Eon's annual recurring revenue, the yearly subscription income, at about sixty million dollars this year, up from ten to twenty million at the end of last year, mostly from one large US bank. Six billion would be roughly a hundred times that revenue, which tells you investors are paying for the founders and the future, not today's sales. Eon competes with Rubrik, Veeam, Druva and Cohesity, and the big clouds keep improving their own recovery tools.

And an update on CrowdStrike and Zscaler, now that the legal filings are public. The merger agreement includes a one point four billion dollar termination fee, the penalty paid if the deal is called off under certain conditions. Shareholders holding about thirty-four percent of Zscaler's stock have already agreed to vote yes, and Zscaler's founder and CEO, Jay Chaudhry, is set to join CrowdStrike's board. Zscaler also released its quarterly results: revenue up twenty percent to about nine hundred forty-nine million dollars, and annual recurring revenue up twenty-one percent to almost three point nine billion. But it canceled its earnings call, the quarterly call with investors, and withdrew its forecast because of the deal. The companies expect to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal twenty twenty-eight, which means late next year at the earliest.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, how to sell an analyst win. IRONSCALES, the email security company founded by the Israeli entrepreneur Eyal Benishti and now based in Atlanta, was named a Leader in the twenty twenty-six Gartner Magic Quadrant for Email Security. The Magic Quadrant is Gartner's famous chart that ranks vendors in a category, and buyers use it to build shortlists. Look at how IRONSCALES framed it: not "we won," but that it's "a recognition of what our eighteen thousand global customers see every day in their inboxes." That turns an analyst ranking into customer proof. And the press release ends with a promise of a free copy of the full report soon. That's a classic move: companies license the report and offer it behind a sign-up form, so the award becomes a source of leads. Notice the hashtags too, aimed at channel partners, the resellers and MSPs who sell IRONSCALES to small businesses.

Second, merger messaging is a discipline. CrowdStrike's note to analysts repeats one line: "Both companies will operate independently until closing." That's for nervous customers and for the sales teams of both companies, who will spend a year competing with each other while planning to merge. At the same time, CrowdStrike is setting up the future pitch: Zscaler joins Falcon Flex, CrowdStrike's flexible licensing program, so customers can buy from both companies under one contract. Calm today, upsell tomorrow.

Third, timing. Eon's last big round, three hundred million dollars, was announced during AWS re:Invent last December. Re:Invent, Amazon's huge cloud conference in Las Vegas, starts on Monday. If Eon's new round lands next week, that's no accident. A funding announcement in front of every cloud buyer in the world is worth more than any ad.
