## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Friday, November thirteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. Jensen Huang, the man who built Nvidia into the world's most valuable chip company, has resigned, and its finance chief is running the company for now. Trump says "we don't need a deal with Iran" and pulled JD Vance off the talks, and overnight Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed it hit an American radar in Bahrain. And here at home, Gadi Eisenkot finally got his security briefing from Netanyahu, while the ultra-Orthodox parties draw up their price for joining him. In your Cyber Watch: a quiet day for deals, so we look at what the CyberArk takeover really cost, and why "digital sovereignty" is becoming a sales pitch in Europe.

## Israel

We start here at home, where the fight over who controls security decisions in this in-between period had a busy Thursday. Quick reminder: Netanyahu is still the caretaker prime minister, which means he runs a caretaker government, a temporary government with limited authority, until a new one is sworn in. Gadi Eisenkot holds the mandate to form that new government.

On Wednesday, Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of refusing to give him the security briefings an incoming prime minister normally gets, and asked the attorney general to step in. It worked, at least partly. The two men met for about two hours, with Netanyahu's military secretary in the room. Channel 13 reports the army told Eisenkot there's no immediate urgency to strike Iran, but that Israel should join in if the United States renews its attacks. A Channel 12 source gave the opposite spin, saying the briefing was meant to stress how urgent renewed fighting is. So, same meeting, two stories.

Netanyahu's side is pushing back hard. Defense Minister Israel Katz said a caretaker government needs no approval from the opposition or the lawyers for military action. "Israel's security is not on hold," he said, adding that he, Netanyahu and the army chief, Eyal Zamir, "see eye to eye on the Iranian issue." Avigdor Liberman answered that "a caretaker government is not authorized to make decisions that could drag Israel into war." And Likud managed to block, until Monday, the vote making Liberman chair of the temporary Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, the Knesset panel that oversees the army and gets classified intelligence. Until then, he can't see that intelligence.

Now the coalition math, and the price tag. Eisenkot's four-party bloc has forty-eight seats, short of the sixty-one needed for a majority, so it needs outside help. Channel 12 reports that the Hasidic leaders inside United Torah Judaism are drafting conditions. Hasidim are one of the main streams of ultra-Orthodox Judaism. One idea: every one of the bloc's forty-eight lawmakers, not just the party leaders, would sign a pledge to pass a law exempting Haredi men from military service. They also want an override clause, a provision letting the Knesset re-pass a law the High Court, Israel's Supreme Court, has struck down. That would be a very hard sell for parties that campaigned on sharing the burden of service.

There's another twist. Kan reports that Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas has been urging United Torah Judaism to talk to Eisenkot's bloc, and that UTJ chairman Yaakov Asher has spoken several times with Abbas and with Ahmad Tibi of the Joint List. Asher's office calls those chats casual. The idea floating around is that the Arab parties might abstain, meaning not vote at all, on key votes. The Joint List wants to freeze the Kaminitz Law, a law that makes it easier to demolish illegally built homes, which hits Arab towns hard, and UTJ hasn't ruled that out. But inside Eisenkot's own party there's resistance: Miriam Peretz, the well-known bereaved mother, said her son, Yashar lawmaker Elyasaf Peretz, would resign from the Knesset if his party sits with an Arab party.

Yair Lapid, number two on Bennett's list, tried to steady the camp in a video: "A government will be formed," he said, admitting that "it is impossible to form a government without painful compromises."

Two more things. In Gaza, an Israeli strike on Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis targeted Muhammad Abu Ismail, the commander of Hamas's Khan Younis Brigade. The army says the hospital was being used as a Hamas headquarters, and that he had held hostages there. The Hamas-run health ministry says six people were killed, including a surgeon. The Board of Peace, the international body overseeing Trump's Gaza plan, said the strike "underscores precisely why" the plan needs to move forward. That plan has stalled over Hamas's refusal to hand over its weapons on Israel's terms.

And in Europe, the European Commission, the EU's executive arm, must now formally examine a citizens' petition calling to suspend the EU-Israel Association Agreement, the treaty behind most of Israel's trade with Europe. It gathered over one point one million verified signatures. The Commission has until May twelfth to respond, and it isn't obliged to act.

## World

Now to the world, and a sharp turn on Iran. On Thursday, Trump wrote on Truth Social, "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed," and claimed the US has "their money" and "their Strait." In the same breath, he took Vice President JD Vance off the diplomacy, telling him to put his "complete and total attention" on what Trump calls crime and corruption in Democratic-run cities. That came one day after the Wall Street Journal reported Vance was weighing face-to-face talks with Iran's security chief. Remember, after the midterms Vance wrote, "The reason we lost is the war." Trump denies he was annoyed by that.

Trump also told radio host Hugh Hewitt he "wouldn't hesitate" to strike again if things go the wrong way, and said it would be "a defeat" if Iran's regime is still in power when he leaves office.

Then, overnight, the Revolutionary Guard claimed it destroyed an American early-warning radar, a system that spots incoming missiles and planes, in Bahrain. It called that revenge for an air attack on a telecom tower in Sirik, an Iranian town on the Strait of Hormuz coast. Nobody has claimed the Sirik attack, the radar claim can't be verified, and there was no immediate comment from Washington or Bahrain. But Reuters notes it's Iran's first claim of hitting American military assets in a Gulf country since early September. The Guards also warned companies working with the US of "more severe penalties."

Why does Iran keep pushing? It has set seven conditions for a ceasefire, including lifting the naval blockade, sanctions relief, releasing frozen money, compensation for war damage, and ending fighting on every front, including Lebanon and Gaza. Secretary of State Marco Rubio calls them "unacceptable." Iran's chief negotiator, parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, told Al Jazeera that American officials even asked Vladimir Putin to get Iran to talk directly with Washington. The White House didn't directly respond. Analyst Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group summed it up: "Both sides are banking on the other's pain threshold being lower than their own." And the pain is real: oil prices are still more than forty percent higher than before the war, even after falling about seven percent this week.

One more Washington note: Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to become speaker in January, called for "dramatic change" on Israel and backed a two-state solution. With Democrats about to run both houses of Congress, that's a signal Jerusalem will be watching.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where Mayor Mamdani is about to keep one of his most controversial promises. A spokesperson, Dora Pekec, told Politico that "our administration will disband the Strategic Response Group." The Strategic Response Group, or SRG, is the NYPD's unit of about six hundred officers for crowd control, counterterrorism and mass-casualty attacks. It was created in twenty fifteen under Commissioner Bill Bratton. Mamdani has long said it "brutalized countless New Yorkers exercising their first amendment rights," meaning their constitutional right to protest. The plan is to split protest policing from counterterrorism, possibly by executive order, a direct order from the mayor that doesn't need the City Council.

The other side: Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch, whom Mamdani kept on, has been at odds with him over the unit. She says it isn't used for peaceful protests. In twenty twenty-five the NYPD handled four thousand two hundred fifty-five protests, and the SRG was on hand at only two hundred forty-five. Critics warn that scrapping it could leave the city without a dedicated team for a terror attack.

And a lighter Upper West Side story: Warner Brothers is rebooting "You've Got Mail," a reboot meaning a new version of an old film. Jason Orley, who made "I Want You Back," will write and direct. The nineteen ninety-eight original, by Nora Ephron, starred Tom Hanks and Meg Ryan as bookstore rivals who fall in love by email, and gave the neighborhood its most famous movie moment, in the cash-only line at Zabar's. No cast yet.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world. Richard Posner, one of the most influential American judges of the last half century, died on Tuesday at eighty-seven. Born in New York to a Jewish family, he was a pioneer of the field called law and economics, using economic thinking to analyze legal rules. Ronald Reagan put him on the Seventh Circuit, a federal appeals court in Chicago, in nineteen eighty-one. Over thirty-six years he wrote nearly thirty-three hundred opinions and more than sixty books, including rulings that struck down same-sex marriage bans in Indiana and Wisconsin. Former University of Chicago law dean Saul Levmore called him "the greatest and most skilled judge in American history."

In Philadelphia, the anti-Zionist group behind last weekend's bike ride past Jewish and Israeli restaurants canceled its follow-up rides. Counter-demonstrators supporting the restaurants had outnumbered them. The group says its event was "hijacked by pro-Israel extremists."

And in the Netherlands, the mayor of Amersfoort stepped in after the city museum canceled an exhibition marking three hundred years of the local synagogue, citing "tensions" since October seventh. The Dutch chief rabbi, Binyomin Jacobs, who lives there, warned that moving it elsewhere would be "tantamount to saying Jews are allowed to be in some places and not in others." The community expects good news in a few weeks.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the end of an era. Jensen Huang has resigned as Nvidia's CEO and left its board, effective immediately. He co-founded the company in nineteen ninety-three and ran it for thirty-three years, turning a graphics-card maker into the company whose chips power most of the AI boom. The board accepted his resignation on Wednesday. It follows his admission of an affair with a woman US officials consider a Chinese spy, and a reported second relationship with an Nvidia executive who reported to him. In a memo to staff he wrote, "I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did."

Colette Kress, the CFO, chief financial officer, the executive in charge of the money, becomes interim CEO, the temporary boss, while staying CFO. She joined in twenty thirteen after about twenty-five years at Cisco and Microsoft. And for the first time, Nvidia has a board chair, the person who leads the board of directors, separate from the CEO: Harvey Jones, a former Synopsys CEO who has sat on Nvidia's board since nineteen ninety-three. The stock fell as much as thirteen percent at Thursday's open and closed down more than four percent. TechCrunch says Nvidia had lost more than three hundred billion dollars in market value since last Friday. Trump has already floated former Apple CEO Tim Cook as a replacement. The next test is Tuesday, when Nvidia reports quarterly earnings. Here, it matters a lot: Nvidia is one of Israel's biggest tech employers, and Calcalist credits it with helping push Israeli tax revenue up more than thirteen percent.

## Business story

Our business story is about a family business with no next generation. You know the name Nakash even if you don't know it: brothers Joe, Ralph and Avi Nakash built Jordache, the jeans brand, in New York. About twenty years ago they bought control of Arkia, Israel's third-largest airline. On Thursday they signed a deal to hand control to a group of Israeli investors: Einav Adiv-Berar, who owns medical centers including TLV Medical Center; real estate entrepreneurs Kidan Dahari and Yaron Adiv; and Yariv Lerner of Negev Group. The buyers promise to inject "tens of millions" into the airline, and the Nakash brothers keep a minority stake. Their explanation was very human: "We are selling Arkia. We have no next generation."

Why sell now? Arkia has lost about thirty million dollars this year, and expects to lose thirty to forty million for the full year. Over the past year it expanded into long flights to New York, Phuket and Hanoi, but in July about seventy percent of its flights ran on wet leases, renting planes with foreign crews, which is expensive. It's been for sale for a year and a half. One earlier buyer, Brooklyn investor Ezra Unger, fell through partly because workers rejected his demand to stop flying on Shabbat.

And the workers matter here, because they own about a fifth of the company. Hours before the signing, the Histadrut, Israel's main labor federation, declared a labor dispute, a formal step that allows a strike later, over job cuts. Arkia says flights are running normally, though its Tel Aviv to London route is suspended until December twelfth. The lesson: a family business can be a great brand, but someone still has to want to run it.

## Art and design

Next, art, where New York is having a big week. City Council Speaker Julie Menin used it to point out a record three hundred twenty-three million dollars for the city's Department of Cultural Affairs.

And a controversial new way to "play" the auctions. Kalshi, the betting-style platform where people trade yes-or-no contracts on future events, named Artnet, the art price database, its official art partner. Artnet suggests topics and supplies auction records, and Kalshi runs the markets. You could bet, say, on whether a painting sells above its high estimate, the top of the auction house's expected price range. The first markets are live ahead of next week's big New York sales. For your agency, it's a sign of where the market is heading: art as entertainment, and as data.

## Travel

Now travel, with a useful read on where Europeans are heading this winter, which tells you where the crowds and prices will be. The European Travel Commission surveyed six thousand people in ten countries. Seventy-six percent plan a trip between October and March, up three points. More are booking early, thirty-eight percent already have, and people are taking more, shorter trips. More than half want quieter, off-the-beaten-track places, with the Netherlands, Switzerland and Poland on top. The busiest stretch is Christmas, and city breaks, short trips to a city, peak in October and November. The practical move: if you want a European city at a good price, go in January, when demand dips, and book soon.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a reason to rethink that afternoon energy drink. Preliminary research presented at the American Heart Association's big annual meeting, not yet peer-reviewed, meaning not yet checked by independent scientists, looked at more than twenty-eight thousand reports to an FDA safety database from twenty seventeen to twenty twenty-five. Reports involving energy drinks or stimulant supplements mentioned cardiac arrest, when the heart suddenly stops, about eleven times as often as reports about other foods and drinks, and heart attacks about four and a half times as often. Adults eighteen to thirty-four made up about forty-five percent of the stimulant reports. It's a link, not proof. The practical idea: healthy adults are usually told to stay under about four hundred milligrams of caffeine a day, and one energy drink can have up to three hundred, so if you also drink coffee, add it up.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

It's been a quiet forty-eight hours for new cyber rounds after the big Israeli wave we covered this week, Daylight, Bay and Dreeze. So today is less about fresh money, and more about what deals are worth after the champagne.

First, the one new round worth knowing. Finite State, a company from Columbus, Ohio, raised a twenty-seven million dollar Series C, led by funds managed by The O.H.I.O. Fund, with National Grid Partners, the venture arm of the British power company, and Energy Impact Partners joining. Finite State does product security: it scans the software and firmware, the low-level code built into physical devices, inside finished products like car electronics, medical devices and power grid equipment, and tells manufacturers which security holes to fix first. It also produces SBOMs, software bills of materials, basically an ingredients list of every piece of code inside a product. The money is going to international expansion. For a Series C, twenty-seven million is modest; Daylight raised eighty million at Series B this week. But Finite State sells into a market being forced open by regulation, like Europe's Cyber Resilience Act, an EU law that makes companies prove their connected products are secure or risk losing access to the market.

Second, an Israeli-founded company in limbo. In case you missed it, back in October Proofpoint, the email security giant owned by the private equity firm Thoma Bravo, agreed to buy Varonis for about six point three billion dollars in cash, forty-nine dollars seventy-five a share. Varonis protects sensitive company data, and about a thousand of its roughly twenty-eight hundred employees work in Israel, mostly at its development center in Herzliya. It just reported quarterly revenue of about one hundred eighty-nine million dollars, up from one hundred sixty-two million a year ago, but gave no forecast and held no earnings call because of the pending deal, which is expected to close in the first half of next year. That's a long time to be neither independent nor merged, and those months are usually when marketing teams wonder whose brand survives.

Third, the real cost of an Israeli mega-exit. Calcalist went through Palo Alto Networks' annual report and found the job cuts after it bought CyberArk will cost about sixty million dollars in total, including severance, the pay given to laid-off workers. About seven hundred people were let go, roughly a hundred of them in Israel. Notice periods ran from three to twelve months, and the plan should be "substantially complete" by the end of Palo Alto's fiscal year, its twelve-month accounting year, in July twenty twenty-seven. Meanwhile, Palo Alto's headcount jumped from about sixteen thousand to almost twenty-two thousand, with forty-two hundred people coming in from CyberArk, and it says it will keep growing in Israel. Palo Alto reports earnings next Wednesday, so expect questions there about the integration and the stalled talks to buy Clutch.

## People moves

On people moves, I couldn't confirm any new marketing chief at a cyber company in the last two days, so instead, a hiring signal worth hearing. A Canadian Cybersecurity Network report found cyber job postings in Canada jumped almost sixty-five percent in a year, but entry-level roles fell from about four percent of postings to one and a half percent in a single quarter. Its founder, François Guay, put it bluntly: Canada "cannot solve its cybersecurity talent shortage by asking every candidate to arrive experienced." The pattern isn't just Canadian. Companies are hiring, but they want people who can deliver from day one. For you, that's good news: five years of marketing, including growth at a cyber startup, is exactly the "already experienced" profile they're asking for.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with two lessons.

First, digital sovereignty is becoming a sales pitch. Digital sovereignty means a country or region keeping control of its own data and technology, instead of depending on foreign companies. Context, a research firm that tracks what distributors sell, the wholesalers between vendors and resellers, found that in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, sales of cybersecurity products from EU-based vendors grew thirty-five percent in two years, versus eight percent for American vendors. EU vendors now hold about fifteen percent of the market, up nearly three points, while American companies still hold sixty-two. Europeans are strongest in security awareness training, vulnerability management, and SIEM, the systems that collect and analyze security logs. The drivers: less trust in the Trump administration, plus EU rules like NIS2, which sets cyber duties for critical industries, and DORA, a similar law for banks. Context's research chief, Joe Turner, said: "Digital sovereignty is becoming a buying decision." But an Omdia analyst warned, "Local hosting is not sovereignty." The marketer's takeaway: buyers now ask who holds the encryption keys and who can see their logs. Answer that in plain words on the website and in the sales deck, with proof like audits, and you win trust that a "hosted in Frankfurt" badge alone won't.

Second, the power of naming the problem. Finite State didn't just announce a round; it tied its whole story to a word the industry is already using: the "Vulnpocalypse," the flood of security holes being found by AI-powered research, where the gap between finding a flaw and attackers using it has shrunk from weeks to days. Its CEO, Matt Wyckhouse, said, "The Vulnpocalypse isn't a one-month event. It's the new baseline." That's smart positioning. When you attach your product to a fear buyers already feel, you don't have to explain why you exist; you only have to explain why you're the answer.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Finite State. A fresh Series C earmarked for international expansion, in a market pushed by European regulation. Expanding abroad usually means hiring regional and product marketers who can translate a compliance story for new buyers, and an Israeli marketer who knows how to sell security across borders fits that.

Second, think about the sovereignty trend from the Israeli side. Many Israeli cyber companies sell heavily in Europe, and they'll need sharp messaging on data control and EU compliance to compete with local vendors. If you're talking to an Israeli company with big European customers, ask how they're answering the sovereignty question. It's a great interview topic, and a gap many of them haven't filled yet.
