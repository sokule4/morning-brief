## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Monday, October nineteenth, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. President Trump has officially endorsed Benjamin Netanyahu, eight days before Israel votes. Egypt is moving toward sending forces to defend Saudi Arabia against the Houthis, as the Gulf war turns regional. And the four founders of Wiz, who sold their company to Google for thirty-two billion dollars, may be about to buy McDonald's Israel. Plus Kylie Jenner in a diamond bra, and a new Olivia Rodrigo and Chappell Roan remix.

## Headlines

We start with the election, and a very loud voice from Washington. On Sunday, President Trump posted on Truth Social, his own social media platform, giving Netanyahu what he called his "Complete and Total Endorsement." In all capital letters, he wrote, "Bibi Netanyahu will never let you down." And he went further: "If Israel has the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi, America will have to do this work ourselves." He said Iran had been "systematically destroyed," and that "we will soon finish the job."

Trump told Axios, "I was always going to do that. I just didn't want to do it too soon." Asked whether he could work with a different Israeli prime minister, he said, "Maybe I will, maybe I won't. Who wants to take the chance?" That's a shift. Earlier this month, in an interview with Time, he avoided endorsing anyone.

Netanyahu jumped on it. On TV Sunday night, he told voters that Trump will finish the Iran job only "if the two of us do it together," and said, "listen to him, weigh his words, and vote for me." His main rival, Gadi Eisenkot, who leads the Yashar party, answered that only Israeli citizens choose their prime minister. "Israel is a democracy," he said, adding that he had asked for "no intervention in Israel's internal election." He also said he sees no Iranian threat that needs urgent action before the vote.

So where's the race? Four polls came out over the weekend. Channel Twelve has Yashar at twenty-three seats and Likud at twenty. Channel Thirteen has them at twenty and eighteen. Averaged across all four, the opposition and Netanyahu's bloc are basically tied at about fifty-one seats each. Nobody is anywhere near the sixty-one needed for a Knesset majority.

Inside Netanyahu's camp, it's getting messy. Itamar Ben Gvir claims Netanyahu promised European leaders he won't be in the next government. That's not confirmed. Ben Gvir's pitch to voters: "Bibi needs a strong Ben Gvir, not a strong Eisenkot." And there's a report that Yitzhak Yosef, the former Sephardi chief rabbi, meaning the chief rabbi for Jews with Middle Eastern and North African roots, told his followers not to vote for Ben Gvir's party, Otzma Yehudit, his far-right party. One more campaign moment: a Channel Fourteen pundit, Yinon Magal, called Eisenkot a "monkey," which many heard as a slur on his Moroccan heritage. Magal says he meant "puppet." Netanyahu condemned the remark. On the other side, Naftali Bennett and Avigdor Liberman say that after the election they'll push laws for term limits, a cap on how long a prime minister can serve, and to bar anyone under indictment from the job. And on Sunday morning, Haredi protesters, ultra-Orthodox Jews opposing the army draft, blocked Route Four near Yavne for two hours at rush hour.

Now Iran, which answered Trump directly. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi wrote on X, "Want to try your luck again? Be our guest," adding, "we have unfinished jobs too." He accused Netanyahu of dragging the US into another war to win the election. A spokesman for the Revolutionary Guard, Iran's elite military force, warned that any attack on Iran's energy sites would turn Israel into "scorched earth." And late Sunday, the Guard's navy claimed another oil tanker hit a mine south of the Strait of Hormuz and caught fire. It's not clear if that's a new ship or one of the ones already claimed, and nothing has been independently confirmed. Separately, a British maritime agency says a tugboat off the UAE was hit by two projectiles. One person had minor injuries.

There is a diplomatic door still open. Araghchi says Iran's seven-day plan is still on the table: if the US meets Iran's conditions, the strait reopens on day six, and talks start on day seven. Russia's foreign minister, Sergei Lavrov, is expected in Tehran today. Oil dipped a little in early Asian trading this morning. Brent crude fell about one percent, to around a hundred and nine dollars a barrel. And overnight, the US told Americans across the Middle East to "exercise heightened vigilance."

Next, a war that's pulling in more countries. Egypt's President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi asked his parliament on Saturday to authorize defending Saudi Arabia against the Houthis. He said, "It is inconceivable that we would allow Saudi Arabia, its security or stability to be harmed," and, "We will only defend Saudi Arabia." Egypt would only act if Riyadh asks. The vote has reportedly been pushed to tomorrow. The Houthis warned Egypt that joining would be "a grave danger" to its own security. Meanwhile, foreign ministers of the GCC, the Gulf Cooperation Council of six Gulf monarchies, met in Bahrain on Sunday. They declared that an attack on one is an attack on all, and called for the Houthis to be designated a terrorist group. In Riyadh, two drones were shot down early Sunday, and the city's famous ninety-nine-story Kingdom Centre told office tenants to work from home "until further notice." The UN's migration agency counts more than two hundred and thirty-three thousand people displaced inside Yemen.

To the West Bank, where the attack on European diplomats near Taybeh has become a European issue. On Sunday, all twenty-seven EU countries condemned it and called for action to stop what they called "pogroms," violent mob attacks on a community. The French mayor who was kicked to the ground, seventy-eight-year-old Bernard Baudoux, said he was wearing his official sash. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar condemned the "rioters," but said the delegation hadn't coordinated with the army. Netanyahu said there's no "compelling evidence" that settlers did it. An army log, though, shows activists calling for help at one thirty in the afternoon, with the message, "At least seven masked settlers armed with clubs. Come quickly." Troops only learned about it around three. And Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine, beaten by settlers on Friday, has broken ribs and four broken vertebrae. US Ambassador Mike Huckabee called it a "despicable act of terror." No arrests have been announced. OCHA, the UN humanitarian office, counts nearly two hundred settler attacks this olive harvest, already more than all of last season. Also overnight, an Israeli man in his sixties was shot dead in Eizariya, next to Ma'ale Adumim. Police are investigating and haven't said if it was terror.

A few more quick ones. In Gaza, a mass funeral was held in Gaza City for the remains of seventy-five people recovered from rubble. Civil Defence estimates eight thousand people are still buried. On Lebanon, a Lebanese military delegation will join US-backed talks with Israel tomorrow in Tampa, Florida. President Joseph Aoun also wants UNIFIL, the UN peacekeeping force in south Lebanon, extended through twenty twenty-seven.

In Ukraine, Kyiv launched one of its biggest drone attacks of the war. Russia says it downed more than a thousand drones overnight, and an oil terminal in Saint Petersburg was hit. President Zelensky told NBC that a US tilt toward Russia would be a "historic mistake." Pope Leo led the Angelus, the Catholic Sunday noon prayer, in his first big public appearance since lung cancer surgery. He said, "I will gradually resume my activities." And in America, a Washington Post-ABC poll has Trump's approval at thirty-three percent, a record low, two weeks before the midterms. Democrats lead by eight points on who should control the House.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where a shooting in Midtown has riders on edge.

Around noon on Saturday, a man was shot in the chest during an argument on the Queens-bound E platform at Seventh Avenue and Fifty-Third Street. He died at Bellevue. Police haven't named him. The suspect, wearing a black mask, a black vest and a red top, ran north up Seventh Avenue. As of Sunday afternoon, no arrest. Subway crime overall is roughly flat this year, but felony assaults underground are up eleven percent. Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch says two hundred and fourteen of the seven hundred and thirty-four people arrested for major transit crimes this year already have three or more arrests in twenty twenty-six. One rider, seventy-two-year-old Eileen, told amNY, "as a woman, I feel unsafe." Subway safety is one of the big issues in the governor's race, with early voting, meaning in-person voting before Election Day, starting this Saturday.

A brighter one: Mayor Mamdani turned thirty-five on Sunday, and East Village Pizza on First Avenue celebrated by selling thirty-five-cent slices for two hours. The mayor also announced last week a quantum computing lab with NYU at seven-seventy Broadway. His line: "economic justice doesn't come at the expense of innovation." It opens in twenty thirty.

This Thursday, the City Council votes on ending Central Park's horse carriages. The bill would phase them out by June twenty twenty-eight. Mamdani says, "The horse carriage industry is coming to an end in New York City." The drivers' union says the bill offers "no meaningful economic relief for workers."

Sports. A rough Sunday for both football teams. The Giants were up ten to nothing against the Saints, then gave up thirty-four straight points and lost thirty-four to twenty-seven. But here's the fun one: tomorrow night, the Knicks raise their first championship banner in fifty-three years at Madison Square Garden, then open their title defense against the Sixers, who added LeBron James and Jaylen Brown this summer. ## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, starting with a record gift to Israel from a New Yorker.

John Paulson, the Queens-born hedge fund billionaire, is giving two hundred and fifty million dollars to the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. It's the biggest donation in the university's history. The Mount Scopus campus will be renamed the Alina and John Paulson Campus. The money funds a new engineering faculty, medical research, research into trauma and recovery, and a think tank. Paulson said, "If there was ever a time to step up and support the country and its institutions, this would be it."

In Paris, during this weekend's huge cost-of-living riots, a temporary synagogue space in the nineteenth district was ransacked. Prayer books and tefillin, the leather prayer boxes observant Jews wear, were thrown around and the rabbi's office was searched. The Torah scrolls weren't touched and nothing was taken. In Marseille, several Jewish-owned shops were damaged. Here's the careful part: France's Jewish community security service says there's "no evidence" Jewish sites were deliberately targeted. One member said, "It really looks like an attempted robbery." So for now, this looks like riot crime, not a hate attack.

In Ireland, a story that is about hate, at least according to the community. A Dublin hotel, the Herbert Park, cancelled more than seventy rooms booked for a conference of special envoys on antisemitism, organized with the European Commission. Maurice Cohen, who heads Irish Jewry's representative council, says the hotel's manager first asked, "Will the IDF be involved?" The rooms were cancelled the next day, with no reason. Cohen called it "contemporary antisemitism." Ireland's chief rabbi was more cautious, saying the hotel "may simply have wanted to avoid the possibility of protests." The hotel didn't respond. Ireland has only about twenty-two hundred Jews.

Back in New York, in case you missed it last week, City Comptroller Mark Levine, the official who manages the city's pension money, broke his campaign promise to resume buying Israel Bonds, bonds Israel sells abroad to raise money. Levine said they're "not an investment that we can make right now," pointing to the war and political instability. Mamdani backed him: "I don't believe that our city should purchase Israel bonds." Former comptroller Scott Stringer called it "a major victory for BDS," the boycott, divestment and sanctions campaign against Israel. The city's pension funds still hold about two hundred and eighty-five million dollars in Israel-related investments.

## Tech, startups and AI

Time for tech, and the biggest AI listing story of the year, which, full disclosure, is about Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, the AI that helps put this show together.

Anthropic told potential investors last week that its revenue for the July to September quarter was sixteen point eight billion dollars, up about forty-five percent from the quarter before. That's huge, but slower than the hundred-and-forty-seven percent jump the quarter before that. According to reports in the Financial Times, CNBC and Fortune, investors are now talking about a valuation of about one and a half trillion dollars for its IPO, its initial public offering, down from earlier talk of two trillion. The company says it's been profitable on an adjusted basis for two quarters, though those numbers aren't audited. The listing is planned for after the US midterms. Why it matters to you: whatever price this IPO sets becomes the yardstick for AI companies everywhere, including the Israeli startups you'd be selling for.

Next, an Israeli startup that's hiring salespeople. Zenzap, a Tel Aviv company that puts AI agents into workplace chat, announced a thirty-three million dollar Series A, co-led by Bessemer and Lightspeed. Including its eleven million dollar seed round, its very first funding, it has raised about forty-four million. Its pitch is basically Slack for companies Slack never reached: clinics, warehouses, real estate offices. CEO Guy Weiss said, "We are targeting non-tech organizations that Slack hasn't reached." Customers have built more than forty thousand automations, like updating sales records or chasing missing invoices. It has forty-five people across Tel Aviv, the US and Europe, and the money goes to development, sales and international expansion. One caveat: Calcalist reports the round may have actually closed a while ago and was only announced now.

Salesforce is buying another Israeli company. It's Loops, a Tel Aviv startup whose AI watches a company's business numbers, explains why they moved, and suggests what to do, instead of you staring at dashboards. It'll be folded into Tableau. The price wasn't disclosed. Loops had raised fourteen million dollars. It's Salesforce's tenth Israeli acquisition, and it's expected to close in early November.

A founder story to love. Elon Gliksberg is twenty-five, a veteran of Unit 8200, the army's elite intelligence tech unit. Last year he started HexAI, and he never raised a shekel from investors. It was bootstrapped, built with no outside money. Now he's selling it for a reported thirty to thirty-five million dollars to Hex-Rays, the Belgian company behind a famous reverse engineering tool, meaning software that takes finished programs apart to see how they work. HexAI built AI agents that hunt for zero-days, malware and hidden backdoors, and it can run air-gapped, fully cut off from the internet, for government customers. All six employees are joining the buyer, which plans to grow in Israel.

One more. Microsoft is cutting jobs in its Israeli Copilot Studio group, the team building its AI agent platform. Globes says it's dozens of people, with most moving to other roles. It's the same theme we keep seeing, from monday.com to One Zero: AI teams themselves being restructured.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Now cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with a number every salesperson should hear.

At the Israel IT Conference in Tel Aviv, Itai Margalit, who runs sales for Salesforce in Israel, said the company had about a hundred million leads, and roughly seventy-five percent of them were never answered by a human. So Salesforce put AI agents on first contact. They reach out, and hand off to a person when it needs a human touch. He says that brought in more than eighty-eight million dollars in revenue, and saved hiring about four hundred people. Salesforce didn't say over what period, and nobody has checked the math independently. But this is the AI SDR story, meaning AI doing the sales development rep's job of qualifying new leads. The takeaway for you: the very top of the funnel is being automated, and the human jobs are moving toward the conversations that actually close.

Related, a debate that changes how reps get paid. In a Calcalist column, Erez Agmon, CEO of the pricing startup Vayu, argues that per-seat pricing, charging per user, is dying. His example: if an AI support agent handles eighty percent of tickets, a customer cuts fifty reps down to ten, and the software vendor loses eighty percent of its seat revenue. His line: "Per-seat pricing simply loses all logic when labor moves from human hands to autonomous code." He thinks the industry moves to usage-based pricing, meaning paying for how much you use, or paying for results. If you're interviewing, it's a smart question to ask any company: how are you pricing AI, and how does that change quotas?

Now a breach. ConnectWise, which sells software to MSPs, the IT firms that run technology for other companies, found a problem on October fourteenth. By Saturday, it had suspended its Cloud Backup service for all users. Researchers at Huntress say the attacker used stolen app credentials to try to sign into almost a thousand Microsoft cloud accounts, and got into about three hundred. A targeted wave hit organizations in policy, national security and critical minerals. ConnectWise says there's "no evidence" its backup archives were accessed. This is a supply-chain attack, hacking victims through a vendor they trust. And because app credentials don't need MFA, the second login code on your phone, they slip right past it. That's exactly the pitch identity and cloud app security companies make.

Two deals. Cohere, the Canadian AI company, plans to buy AISLE, a young startup whose AI finds and patches unknown software flaws. In its first year, it found more than five hundred new vulnerabilities, including all twelve in one major OpenSSL release. And the quantum computing company IonQ is buying Elisity for up to one point three billion dollars in an all-stock deal, meaning the buyer pays in its own shares. That includes an earnout, part of the price paid later if results are hit. Elisity does zero trust, giving nobody more network access than they need, through microsegmentation, which walls off parts of a network so a breach can't spread. IonQ's angle is post-quantum cryptography, encryption quantum computers can't break.

## Business story

Our business story today: how a thirty-two billion dollar cybersecurity exit might turn into Big Macs.

Rewind to nineteen ninety-two. An Israeli businessman named Omri Padan buys the franchise, the right to run the McDonald's brand locally, for Israel. A year later, the first branch opens at the Ayalon Mall. For three decades, Padan builds it into a national chain.

Then came the Gaza war. Israeli branches gave free meals and discounts to soldiers, and that set off boycotts of McDonald's across the Arab world, and a global sales slump the company openly admitted to. In twenty twenty-four, McDonald's corporate bought the Israeli business back from Padan, two hundred and twenty-five branches and about five thousand workers. Reports put the price between one and a half and two billion shekels.

But running it from headquarters didn't go well. According to Calcalist, branches are emptier and customers complain about service. So about eighteen months ago, McDonald's hired Goldman Sachs to find a new local owner. A long list of Israeli money lined up: the founder of Max Stock, private equity firms, which buy stakes in private companies, insurance giants, the owners of Café Café. And here's the twist. McDonald's wasn't chasing the highest bid. It reportedly ruled out public companies, anyone likely to face regulatory trouble, and anyone who had made public political statements. It wanted, quote, "a private franchisee capable of holding the business over the long term." Some bidders found out they were out when Goldman simply stopped answering their calls.

Who's left standing? The four founders of Wiz: Assaf Rappaport, Yinon Costica, Ami Luttwak and Roy Reznik. After Google bought Wiz for thirty-two billion dollars, each of them reportedly walked away with around three billion before tax. Their family office, a private firm that manages their fortunes, is now reportedly the only group still negotiating. The estimated price: about five hundred million shekels, around a hundred and sixty million dollars. That's roughly a third or less of what McDonald's reportedly paid Padan. On top of that, the new owner would pay royalties, a cut of sales, to McDonald's, and the deal is expected to pay for itself in five to seven years.

It fits a pattern. Rappaport and Costica have already committed a hundred and twenty million dollars to the TV channel Reshet Thirteen, and bought most of the entertainment company Israel Bidur. A source close to Rappaport put it this way: "Assaf wants the deal, but he wouldn't be upset if the negotiations didn't come to fruition." Nothing's signed, and talks could take weeks. But it's a fascinating question: can tech founders fix a damaged consumer brand? And notice what the seller valued most. Not the biggest check, but the right buyer.

## Wellness

And your wellness idea for the day: watch the ultra-processed food.

A new study in JAMA Network Open, from McMaster University in Canada, scanned the brains of more than eighty-seven hundred middle-aged adults. Those whose diets were nearly half ultra-processed food, things like chips, sweetened cereals and sweet flavored yogurts, were more than forty percent more likely to show signs of brain blood vessel damage, including silent strokes, strokes with no symptoms that only show up on a scan. They also had more visceral fat, deep belly fat around the organs. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof of cause. But the fix is easy. Swap sweet yogurt for plain with fruit, and boxed cereal for oats. The co-author's advice: "eat at home as much as possible."
