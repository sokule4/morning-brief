## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Wednesday, October twenty-first, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. Six days before Israel's election, the first polls since Benny Gantz quit show Gadi Eisenkot's party widening its lead, but nobody close to a majority. Iran tells mediators that "not even a tiny bit" of its enriched uranium will leave the country, while oil touches a five-month high. And back home, the Knicks raised their championship banner, then lost to LeBron James and the Sixers. Plus a hundred-million-dollar Modigliani in Paris, and a surprise baby reveal on Dancing with the Stars.

## Headlines

We start with the election, now six days away. The first full polls since Gantz pulled out are in, and they point in one direction. A Walla poll published Tuesday has Eisenkot's Yashar party at twenty-three seats, up one. Netanyahu's Likud is at nineteen, down one. Naftali Bennett's party, Together, has fourteen. Channel Twelve has a similar picture: Yashar twenty-four, Likud twenty-one. Channel Thirteen has it tighter, twenty to eighteen.

Here's the part that matters most. Israel's parliament has a hundred and twenty seats, so you need sixty-one to form a government. In the Walla poll, Netanyahu's bloc gets forty-eight seats, and the opposition gets fifty-four. Neither side gets there. That leaves the Arab parties, polling around fourteen seats combined, as the big unanswered question. Whether the opposition could form a government that depends on them is one of the biggest questions of this campaign. Two small parties are hovering just under the electoral threshold: former general Ofer Winter's party, and the Reservists party. If they fall short, every vote they get is wasted, which is why both sides keep pressuring small parties to quit, just like Gantz did.

Two more election moments. A day after Trump endorsed Netanyahu, Vice President JD Vance pointedly did not. "I'm not anti-endorsing. I'm not pro-endorsing," he said. "I'm not getting involved in the Israeli elections." And a surprise defection: Tzachi Hanegbi, a longtime Likud minister and Netanyahu's former national security adviser, said he'll vote for Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu. His reason: "giving power to Avigdor Liberman strengthens Israel's security." That's a veteran of Netanyahu's own party walking away a week before the vote.

There's also a fight over the draft, meaning mandatory military service, which Haredi men have long been exempt from. Military police detained more than forty Chabad men at Ben Gurion Airport, suspected of dodging service. Netanyahu, who relies on the Haredi parties, criticized the arrests, calling them "a deliberate attempt to harm the process of drafting Haredim without coercion." His opponents say the law should apply to everyone. Expect this to be a big talking point in the final days.

To Iran, where the message hasn't changed. Pakistan's army chief, Asim Munir, flew to Tehran on Tuesday to try to mediate between Iran and the US. Iran's answer was firm. Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran's top security council, said on state TV: "Not even a tiny bit of enriched nuclear material will leave Iran." Enrichment means processing uranium so it can fuel reactors or, at higher levels, a bomb. Getting that material out of Iran has been one of Washington's main demands. The parliament speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Ghalibaf, said Iran has "nothing new to say" until America meets its seven conditions, which include a ceasefire across the region, unfreezing Iranian money and lifting oil sanctions. Qatar, which is also mediating, says "the negotiation is ongoing." One Arab outlet reported a possible compromise: Iran gets interest on six billion dollars of frozen funds, and in return lets in the IAEA, the UN's nuclear watchdog. That's unconfirmed.

What about Russia? Sergei Lavrov's visit to Tehran ended with a show of unity. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said, "Never in our history have Iran and Russia worked as closely as now." That's a direct swipe at Trump, who had posted that Russia was doing very little to help Iran. Trump's reply: "We don't need any help. But I was testing them."

Meanwhile, the Strait of Hormuz keeps rattling markets. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims three more tankers hit mines near the strait on Tuesday, one of them carrying over two million barrels of crude. US Central Command called that claim false. Either way, traffic is a trickle. One tracker logged just four ships passing through on Saturday. Before the crisis, it was about eighty-five a day. Brent crude jumped to about a hundred and eleven dollars and sixty-seven cents early Tuesday, its highest since May, then slipped back to around a hundred and eight and a half on mixed reports about the talks. For you, that means fuel, flights and anything shipped will stay expensive.

The Saudi-Houthi war just cost Saudi Arabia its biggest business event. The Future Investment Initiative, known as "Davos in the Desert," a glitzy conference where world CEOs come to Riyadh to make deals, was due to start next Monday. It's been postponed to twenty twenty-seven over security concerns, after the Houthi strike on Riyadh's main airport earlier this month that killed at least thirteen people. That's a real blow to the Saudi crown prince's plan to attract foreign money and move the economy beyond oil. Egypt's parliament still votes next Monday on whether to send forces to help defend Saudi Arabia.

Lebanon. The US-hosted military talks between Israel and Lebanon ran for about six hours in Tampa on Tuesday, at the headquarters of US Central Command. No results were announced, and talks resume today. Lebanon wants Israel to stop demolishing homes in the south, pull back its troops, and release prisoners. Israel wants a direct way to check, together with the Lebanese army, that Hezbollah's weapons are really gone from test areas. Lebanon rejects anything that gives Israel a direct role in monitoring its army.

In Gaza, medics say Israeli fire killed at least four people on Tuesday, including a husband and wife in an airstrike on a Gaza City apartment, and a seventeen-year-old in Khan Younis. The victim's brother told Al Jazeera, "We're supposed to be in a ceasefire period, but honestly, we are not seeing a ceasefire." The Israeli army says it's continuing to act against Hamas weapons stockpiles. Prices are soaring too. Israel has blocked commercial goods since last week in a dispute over a ten-thousand-shekel fee per truck, and flour now costs more than double what it did.

And in the West Bank, more detail on the attack on Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine last week. She's sixty-three. She was beaten by around eighty masked settlers for more than ten minutes, and needed hand surgery. US Ambassador Mike Huckabee called it "a despicable act of terror carried out by masked cowards." The UN's humanitarian office, OCHA, counts more than two hundred attacks on Palestinian olive farmers since September first, already more than all of last year's harvest season.

Quickly around the world. In Oslo, Zelensky met Trump's envoy Steve Witkoff, who goes on to St. Petersburg today to meet Russian officials. He's bringing proposed security guarantees for Ukraine modeled on NATO's Article Five, the promise that an attack on one member is an attack on all. Zelensky said, "We have some clear ideas on how to get to peace." In France, targeted overnight curfews have brought a bit of calm after the riots. But the cost is showing up in markets. France's ten-year bond yield, basically the interest rate the government pays to borrow, hit a twenty-four-year high. More protests are planned for Saturday. And in Canada, voters in Alberta, the oil-rich western province, rejected a path to leaving Canada in a referendum, a direct public vote. About seventy-six percent chose to stay. Prime Minister Mark Carney said Albertans "chose unity."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where ring night didn't go to plan.

The Knicks raised their championship banner at Madison Square Garden on Tuesday night, their first title since nineteen seventy-three. It now hangs next to the nineteen seventy and seventy-three banners. The players got their rings, and Jalen Brunson told the crowd, "The joy that this city had this summer, we would love to replicate that again." Resale tickets were going for about fifteen hundred dollars and up.

Then the Sixers spoiled the party, winning one hundred and six to one hundred. It was the Philadelphia debut of LeBron James and Jaylen Brown, both new to the team. Tyrese Maxey led everyone with twenty-eight points. Brown had nineteen, and hit a buzzer-beater three to end the third quarter, off a LeBron pass. LeBron had fourteen points, nine rebounds and six assists. For New York, Brunson scored twenty-one, but on six of nineteen shooting, and Karl-Anthony Towns had nineteen points and twelve rebounds. The Knicks shot just thirty-seven percent. Knicks fans chanted "We don't want you" at LeBron, and he went back and forth with fans and Spike Lee courtside. The Knicks play in Boston on Friday.

Now City Hall. Mayor Mamdani and Governor Hochul appeared together on Monday to expand 2-K, free child care for two-year-olds. Ten thousand new seats are coming in fall twenty twenty-seven, covering every school district in the Bronx and Staten Island. Add the two thousand seats that opened this fall, and it's more than twelve thousand kids. Demand is real: over fifty-seven hundred families applied for the first two thousand spots. The state is putting in one point two billion dollars. Mamdani said, "Today, for ten thousand more families, we have an answer." It's also a politically useful photo for Hochul, two weeks before Election Day.

Speaking of which, the governor's race. A new Republican internal poll, meaning one paid for by a campaign, claims Hochul leads Bruce Blakeman by only about four points. Take that with caution. The independent average still has her up about thirteen, with polls ranging from nine to nineteen points. A Republican strategist called the race "very competitive." Hochul's campaign says she "continues to build her lead and momentum."

Here's one you'll notice walking around. The city says it has taken down about twelve hundred sidewalk sheds since January. Those are the scaffolding tunnels that cover sidewalks during building repairs, sometimes for years. That's more than fifteen percent of them in ten months. The city also showed off two new shed designs outside the Buildings Department downtown: one with a see-through roof that lets daylight in, and one on wheels that can be moved quickly. Mamdani's line: "New Yorkers shouldn't have to accept dark, obstructed sidewalks as a fact of life."

On safety, the city is expanding its anti-violence program, which pays community groups to step in before disputes turn into shootings, to Bushwick, Harlem, and Washington Heights and Inwood. Each area gets one point six million dollars.

And tomorrow, the City Council is expected to pass the bill ending Central Park's horse carriages by spring twenty twenty-eight. It's called Romanch's Law, after an eighteen-year-old tourist who died in June after being thrown from a carriage. The drivers' union is unhappy. It says the bill only "considers" compensation and guarantees nothing. One driver, Christina Hansen, called it "a concept of a plan." We'll tell you how the vote goes.

## Jewish world

Next, the Jewish world, starting in London.

On Monday afternoon in Stamford Hill, a heavily Haredi neighborhood in north London, a man punched at least three Jewish women in the street. One was pushing a baby stroller and was hit twice in the head. Shomrim, the Jewish volunteer neighborhood patrol, says its members arrived within thirty seconds and held the suspect until police came. A thirty-three-year-old man was arrested on suspicion of a racially and religiously aggravated attack. Victims say only visibly Jewish women were targeted. London police are treating it as a suspected antisemitic hate crime and have added patrols. The European Jewish Congress said "Jewish women should not have to fear violence simply for being visibly Jewish."

In Croatia, prosecutors have charged twenty-three people over chants before a soccer match last Thursday. Fans of the local club Hajduk Split marched before a game against Ajax Amsterdam, a Dutch club long associated with Amsterdam's Jewish community. They carried a mock coffin labeled "Ajax," and chanted things like "Crush the Jews," and slogans praising the Ustasha, Croatia's pro-Nazi regime during World War Two. Croatia's president called it "unacceptable and shameful," and the prime minister condemned the "messages of anti-Semitism." Hajduk apologized, saying fans "witnessed scenes which we do not want Europe to remember."

In American politics, Michigan's Senate race has become a test for Jewish voters. Democrat Abdul El-Sayed told ABC this week that Israel is "not actually our friend," adding, "An ally that takes your money to push you into wars you shouldn't be fighting is not an ally." He also says he is "committed to the safety and security" of Jewish voters. His Republican opponent, Mike Rogers, said, "It's pretty hard to say that this guy is anything but anti-Jew." Michigan's Jewish community is split. A pro-El-Sayed letter from local Jews has more than nine hundred and fifty signatures, while hundreds of others, including lifelong Democrats, pledged to vote for Rogers. A super PAC, a political group that can spend unlimited money, linked to AIPAC, the main pro-Israel lobby in Washington, has sent mailers against El-Sayed to Jewish homes. The final debate is tonight, and the vote is November third.

A diplomatic moment in Budapest. President Isaac Herzog visited Hungary, the first high-level Israeli visit since Viktor Orbán lost power in April after sixteen years. Hungary's new prime minister, Péter Magyar, reversed Orbán's decision to leave the ICC, the International Criminal Court, which had issued an arrest warrant for Netanyahu. Even so, Herzog invited Magyar to Israel, and Hungary's president reaffirmed a "zero tolerance" policy on antisemitism. There's a nice family echo: Herzog's father, Chaim Herzog, made the first visit to Hungary by a sitting Israeli president, back in nineteen ninety-one.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a neat Israeli exit.

Boomi, an American company that connects business software and data, based outside Philadelphia, is buying the Israeli startup Elementary Data for about forty-three million dollars. Elementary does data observability, basically a smoke alarm for company data. It catches broken or strange numbers before they reach reports, models and AI agents. Over fifteen hundred data teams use it, including Fiverr and Patagonia. It has only seventeen employees, and its founders, CEO Maayan Salom and Or Avidov, are both Unit 8200 veterans. Here's why it's a good exit: Elementary raised only about eight and a half million dollars, so the sale is roughly five times what investors put in. Salom's pitch: "When agents act on your data at machine speed, trust in that data stops being a nice-to-have." Boomi's CEO, Steve Lucas, said, "AI is only as trustworthy as the data behind it." It's Boomi's third Israeli acquisition.

Next, a big one for Israeli startups: Stripe is getting ready to launch in Israel. Stripe is the American-Irish company behind the "pay" button on a huge share of websites. According to Globes, it posted a job for a general manager for Israel, to build a local sales team and work with banks and regulators. The posted pay: up to about one point eight million shekels a year. Right now, Stripe's payment processing isn't available in Israel, so many Israeli startups set up companies abroad just to use it. A local launch would make life easier for Israeli online businesses, and put pressure on local payment companies.

Now defense tech, which keeps growing. High Lander, an Israeli company that makes software to manage fleets of drones and the low airspace they fly in, raised sixteen million dollars. The interesting part is the investor: EDGE, a state-linked defense group from the United Arab Emirates, increased its stake. That's a sign the business ties from the Abraham Accords, the twenty twenty normalization deals between Israel and Gulf states, are still producing deals. And Elbit Systems, Israel's biggest defense company, completed its purchase of a Canadian underwater robotics firm, International Submarine Engineering, which has built more than two hundred and fifty underwater vehicles. Israeli defense giants are buying into autonomous sea systems.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with bots.

Blackwall, a web security company from Estonia, raised a one-hundred-million-dollar Series C, a later-stage round for a growing company, at a two point two billion dollar valuation. That's about four times its value a year ago. Blackwall protects websites from bots and DDoS attacks, where attackers flood a site with fake traffic to knock it offline. Its origin story is fun: it started as BotGuard, after a co-founder's recipe website got swamped by automated traffic. And the go-to-market is smart. Instead of selling to millions of websites one by one, Blackwall sells through hosting and cloud companies, which offer it to their own customers. One investor put the trend this way: "A tsunami of agents will overwhelm infrastructure optimised for human traffic." As AI agents browse and shop on the web, telling good bots from bad ones is becoming its own market.

Now a marketing-finance story you'll like. Otter dot AI, the AI meeting note-taker, raised three hundred and twenty million dollars from General Catalyst's Customer Value Fund. Here's the twist: that fund doesn't buy shares. It pays for a startup's marketing and customer acquisition, and gets paid back from the revenue those new customers bring in. In other words, it treats marketing spend as an investment you can finance, like a loan for a factory. Otter passed a hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue, the yearly subscription income, last year, and forty million people have used it. It also just hired a new CMO, chief marketing officer, Alex Gay, from Superhuman. His pitch: "when you come into a meeting, you spend the first twenty minutes recapping." For a growth marketer, this is the dream scenario: a new CMO with a fund built to pay for acquisition, as long as the numbers work.

A breach worth knowing about. Ahold Delhaize, the Dutch-Belgian grocery giant behind Stop and Shop, Food Lion, Hannaford and Giant Food in the US, confirmed that "malicious actions" caused a week-long outage across its US and European brands. Stores stayed open, but online ordering and deliveries went down. The company says it's found no evidence that sensitive customer data was taken. One estimate puts the cost at ten million euros or more, and it's the company's second cyberattack in two years.

And a new kind of target: AI keys. OpenCode, a popular open-source AI coding tool, says attackers got into an old system and may have taken users' API keys for OpenAI, Anthropic and Google. An API key is a password that lets software use a paid service, so attackers ran up AI bills on other people's accounts. It hit about one point four percent of its online users. Co-founder Dax Raad summed it up: "Whole new world with AI-powered hacking."

That leads to the policy story. US agencies, including NIST, the government's standards body, and CISA, the cyber defense agency, published guidance on how to respond when AI agents "act without instruction," meaning rogue AI agents. And Microsoft is now telling customers to treat AI agents like insider threats, the way you'd treat an employee who might go rogue. When governments and Microsoft use the same language, budgets follow. For your job hunt, "AI agent security" is the phrase to know.

Last, a commerce trend. The Wall Street Journal reports that companies are pushing back on AI shopping agents, the bots that buy things for you. Yelp makes them pay for its data. Delta restricts outside agents from booking flights. Amazon is suing the AI company Perplexity over it. Meanwhile Meta is signing deals with Walmart, Gap, Sephora and Best Buy. And consumers aren't sold either: only ten percent of Americans would give AI full control of a purchase. One analyst said, "Consumers still like making their own decisions and being in control, especially when money is involved." For marketers, that means a new decision: block the bots, or partner with them.

## Business story

Now our business story, about a Jerusalem company that started with welding and ended up inside Israel's laser air defense.

In two thousand eight, a physicist named Dr. Eyal Shekel founded Civan Lasers in Jerusalem's Har Hotzvim tech park. His idea was to make very powerful lasers by combining many smaller beams into one, and steering them precisely. At first, the customers were factories. High-power lasers are used for industrial welding and cutting metal. Not glamorous, but useful.

Then the turning point. The same technology that focuses a beam to weld steel can also focus a beam to hit a drone or a rocket in the air. Civan's tech became a core part of Iron Beam, the laser air-defense system built by Rafael and Elbit. Iron Beam is the newest layer of Israel's defenses, and it's cheap per shot, because a laser costs a few dollars of electricity instead of an expensive interceptor missile.

Of course, success brought a fight. Civan and Elbit, a shareholder and partner, ended up in a dispute over who could sell what. The Defense Ministry had to mediate. The deal: in Israel, Civan sells through Elbit's optics unit, and abroad it can sell smaller systems, under one kilowatt, on its own.

And now, the next chapter. Globes reports that Civan is preparing an IPO on the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange, at a pre-money valuation, its worth before the new money comes in, of one point two to one point eight billion shekels. Some insiders hope for five hundred and fifty to six hundred million dollars. It plans to raise up to about three hundred and fifty million shekels from Israeli investors. It has about two hundred employees, revenue estimated in the tens of millions of dollars, and a backlog, signed orders not yet delivered, in the hundreds of millions. The founder still owns more than a quarter.

Why it matters: defense tech has become the hottest part of the Israeli economy since the war, and now that wave is reaching the local stock market. The lesson is a good one for founders, too. Civan didn't start out as a defense company. It started by doing one hard thing very well, and found its biggest market later.

## Celebs, culture and fashion

Next, celebs, culture and fashion, starting with drama in Hollywood.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, where Paris is selling despite the chaos.

Art Basel Paris opened its first VIP preview on Tuesday at the Grand Palais, and the headline is a painting priced above one hundred million dollars. It's Modigliani's "Nu étendu," a reclining nude from nineteen seventeen, offered by the gallery Pace Di Donna Schrader. The same family has owned it since nineteen twenty, and it has barely been seen in public for a century. It hasn't sold yet. The dealer said it would take time to place, and called the fair's activity "like drinking from a fire hose."

Real sales came fast. A Joan Mitchell painting sold for fourteen and a half million dollars, and a nineteen eighty-two Basquiat for eleven million. Hauser and Wirth sold a Gerhard Richter for around ten million. White Cube sold two Julie Mehretu paintings for seven point eight five million each. Dealer Thaddaeus Ropac said, "We're doing very well, but it was expected, to be honest," adding that collectors "make decisions fast in Paris." Dealers seemed happier than at Basel in June, even though the riots kept some collectors home.

The protests reached the art world directly too. On Monday, students blocked the entrance to the Louvre, and the museum stayed closed through the morning. Their signs read "French public schools are falling apart." It happened exactly one year after the Louvre's crown jewels heist.

## Wellness

Finally, a wellness idea about recovery. A sports scientist at the University of Bath, Max Davis, reviewed the evidence on what actually helps your body recover after exercise. The surprise: regular ice baths may actually blunt your long-term gains, because some inflammation after a workout is how muscles adapt. Massage helps soreness, but the performance benefit is "modest and inconsistent." What works is boring. Sleep seven to nine hours. Eat twenty to forty grams of protein after a workout, with some carbs, like Greek yogurt with fruit. Whey protein, a milk protein powder, has a small to moderate benefit. And plan real rest days. His line: "recovery cannot be bottled and sold."
