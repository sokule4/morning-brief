## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, this is your Cyber Brief. Two items lead today. Abnormal AI, the email security company, just hired Don Jeter, the marketer behind Torq's loud, provocative brand, as its chief marketing officer. And a New York cyber investor with a big Tel Aviv portfolio is raising a second two hundred fifty million dollar fund, with some blunt advice for founders.

## Funding and acquisitions

It's been a quiet forty-eight hours for new rounds after Sentra's one hundred fifty million dollar raise, which we covered yesterday. So let's look at the money behind the money.

Stephen Ward is a former Secret Service agent with a great backstory. Years ago in New York, he arrested the hacker behind the biggest credit card data theft of that era, then turned him into an informant. Audible made it into a podcast called "Operation Firewall." Ward went on to run security at TIAA and Home Depot as CISO, then spent three years investing at Insight Partners, working on deals including Wiz and Abnormal. Two years ago he co-founded Brightmind Partners, a New York venture fund that invests only in cybersecurity. Its first fund closed at two hundred fifty million dollars late last year.

TechCrunch reports he's now raising a second fund of about the same size, this time as the sole general partner, the person who decides where the money goes, after his co-founder left in April over what Ward calls differences about the firm's future. The track record so far: fourteen investments and one big exit, meaning a startup sold. That was SGNL, an identity security company CrowdStrike agreed to buy for seven hundred forty million dollars, just eleven months after Brightmind led its Series A. The detail for you: roughly forty percent of the first fund's startups are based in Tel Aviv. And Brightmind co-led the twelve million dollar seed round of Rig Security, the Tel Aviv startup that tells apart what an employee does from what their AI agent does.

Ward's advice to founders is blunt. He says the best time to sell a cyber startup is between its third and seventh year: "sell on the way up." And he named Qualys and Tenable, two older public security companies, as incumbents young startups could beat.

In case you missed it, two late-October deals we haven't covered. Cognition, the company behind Devin, an AI coding agent, acquired Corridor, a startup that checks the security of code written by AI. Its co-founders, Jack Cable and Ashwin Ramaswami, will work with Cognition's security chief, Alex Stamos, the former head of security at Facebook. No price was disclosed. The founders say AI agents "can now execute end-to-end attack chains," whole attacks from start to finish. And &Truth, with offices in Palo Alto and Singapore, came out of stealth with a seventy million dollar seed round. It uses formal verification, mathematically proving that software does exactly what it should, instead of just testing it. Seventy million is enormous for a seed. Its co-CEO is Clark Barrett, a Stanford computer science professor, and the company is betting that as AI writes more code, proof will matter more than tests.

## People moves

Now people moves, and the one that matters most for you is a marketing hire.

Abnormal AI, the American company that started in email security and now uses what it calls behavioral AI to protect identities and AI tools, named Don Jeter as CMO. Abnormal protects more than five thousand organizations, including thirty percent of the Fortune five hundred. Jeter comes from Torq, the Israeli-founded security automation unicorn, where he built a brand that Forbes called "unapologetically bold." Before that he held senior marketing roles at Symantec and Veritas, and led global marketing at a company whose annual recurring revenue grew from about five million to one billion dollars. He reports directly to the founder and CEO, Evan Reiser, and runs the global marketing team. Reiser's quote is a great one-liner about why marketing matters: "Everyone talks about AI. We've been building for it for years. Don will make sure the market knows it."

Next, Glow, the endpoint security company, endpoint meaning employees' laptops and devices. Glow was founded last year by former Meta, Snowflake and Claroty executives, led by CEO Roi Tiger, a former Meta vice president of engineering. It came out of stealth in July with one hundred eighty million dollars at a one point two billion dollar valuation, backed by Sequoia and Cyberstarts. It's headquartered in Palo Alto, but about seventy percent of its roughly one hundred employees are in Israel. Glow named Alex Patman as chief revenue officer. He was CRO at Drata, the compliance automation company. When a young unicorn hires its first big-name revenue chief, a real go-to-market machine, how a company brings its product to customers, usually follows: sales teams, partners and marketing.

Gong, the Israeli-founded revenue intelligence company, whose software records and analyzes sales calls, named Liron Shtraichman as chief R and D officer, the executive in charge of research and development. He led global engineering at monday.com. Gong also promoted Guy Havusha to CISO from inside the company.

Three more quickly. Armor, a cloud security services company, named Nicholas Lee as CEO, previously CEO of Fujitsu Intelligence, while founder Chris Drake becomes chief innovation officer. DNSFilter, which blocks dangerous websites at the network level, hired Justin Foster as CTO from Forescout. And Dragos, the leading company protecting industrial systems like power plants and factories, named Rob T. Lee as chief AI officer, the executive who sets how a company uses AI. Lee led AI work at the SANS Institute, the big cybersecurity training organization. And one from late October we missed: Ent, an AI endpoint security startup that came out of stealth with a one hundred million dollar seed round, named Kevin Arsenault as CRO. He was on CrowdStrike's early sales team and became its vice president of sales for North America through its twenty nineteen IPO.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and today has a clear theme: standing out.

First, the Jeter hire itself. Abnormal could have hired a safe enterprise marketer. Instead it hired someone known for provocative, unconventional campaigns. In a crowded market where every vendor claims AI, a distinctive brand is now treated as a competitive weapon, not decoration. If you're interviewing, a portfolio of bold, specific creative work is worth more than a list of channels you managed.

Second, a warning about AI-washing, calling any product "AI" to ride the hype. Brightmind says PitchBook, the startup data company, counted two hundred seventy-eight AI security deals in the first half of this year. When Brightmind reviewed them by hand, it found only thirty were true AI security companies. Investors and buyers are getting skeptical, so positioning has to be specific: what exactly does the product protect, from what, and how is that different?

Third, the money is concentrating. PitchBook data says early-stage cybersecurity funding hit four point seven billion dollars through September, already more than all of last year, even though the number of deals fell from three hundred fifty-eight to two hundred forty-eight. The average seed round grew from about five and a half million dollars to eight point four million. Fewer, bigger bets mean young companies have money to hire marketers earlier, but they'll expect results fast.

And two reality checks on AI in go-to-market. Perplexity published a customer case study saying its agent built Meta ad campaigns for a lobster seller and raised return on ad spend, how much revenue each ad dollar brings back, by sixty-three percent. That's a vendor's own claim, but expect AI-run campaigns to become a standard pitch. On the other hand, Uber said that in its busiest week of October, just four rides were booked by AI agents, against about three hundred twenty million trips and orders. Agentic shopping, AI agents buying on their own, is a great story, but customers aren't there yet.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you. First, Torq. Its CMO just left for Abnormal, which means one of Israel's best-known security brands may soon be looking for new marketing leadership, or rebuilding the team under a new head. Keep an eye on its careers page and on who gets the job. Second, Glow. About seventy Israeli employees, one hundred eighty million in the bank, and a brand-new revenue chief. That's the moment a company usually starts hiring product marketing, the people who shape how a product is pitched, and demand generation, the marketers who fill the sales pipeline. Third, Brightmind's portfolio. With about forty percent of its startups in Tel Aviv and a new fund coming, it's a useful list of young Israeli companies to research and follow.
