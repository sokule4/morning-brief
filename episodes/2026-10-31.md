## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, October thirty-first, Halloween, and this is your Morning Brief. In Israel, the coalition puzzle got stranger: one of Netanyahu's oldest ultra-Orthodox allies is reportedly weighing talks with the other side, while the left flatly rules out a unity government. Hackers linked to North Korea stole about six point three billion dollars from Binance, the world's biggest crypto exchange, in roughly twenty minutes. And DoorDash is buying Instacart for seventeen billion dollars, a deal that will change how a lot of people get their groceries. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in Israel, where the counting is done and the bargaining has begun. Quick reminder of the numbers: Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three seats, Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc has forty-eight, and the two Arab parties have nineteen. Sixty-one is a majority. Official results go to President Herzog on Wednesday, and the new Knesset is sworn in on November tenth.

The most interesting new twist comes from inside Netanyahu's own camp. United Torah Judaism, the Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox party with seven seats, is really two factions glued together, and one of them, Degel HaTorah, answers to a council of senior rabbis. According to the Haredi news site Kikar HaShabbat, Rabbi Dov Lando, Degel's top spiritual leader, told the faction's lawmakers to open talks with the opposition. Channel Twelve reported that party figures are even considering letting an Eisenkot-led minority government, a government without a majority that survives because others don't topple it, stay in power with their outside support. One party figure told Channel Twelve, "Our votes are not in Netanyahu's pocket." The party's chairman, Yaakov Asher, denied any instruction was given.

Why would they even consider it? It's all about the draft. For decades, full-time yeshiva students, men who study religious texts in a yeshiva, a Torah academy, have been effectively exempt from military service. Courts have ruled that unfair, the army needs soldiers after three years of war, and arrests of draft evaders have become a weekly flashpoint. On Thursday night, ultra-Orthodox protesters blocked roads after the arrest of a draft evader, police used batons and stun grenades, and dozens of protesters and officers were reported hurt. The rabbis' priority is protecting the yeshiva students' status, and they'll talk to whoever might protect it. Netanyahu, for his part, called his far-right partners Itamar Ben Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich this week and told them, "We are together and we are staying together."

On the other side, Yair Golan, who leads the Democrats, the main left-wing party, said "absolutely not" to a unity government with Likud, and called Netanyahu "a national security danger." His math: the opposition's forty-eight seats plus Ra'am's eight makes fifty-six, with the Joint List supporting from outside. That's the kind of arrangement Netanyahu says would be close to treason, and Ra'am's own leader, Mansour Abbas, is skeptical it would hold. There's also a plan for day one. Eisenkot, Naftali Bennett and Avigdor Liberman have discussed using the Knesset's first session to replace the speaker, the lawmaker who controls what comes to a vote, currently Likud's Amir Ohana. A two thousand twenty court ruling says a demand by sixty-one members must be put to a vote, even before a government exists. But reaching sixty-one would require the Arab parties' votes, which is politically sensitive. Golan even talked about repealing laws from the judicial overhaul, the government's push to weaken the courts, before a government is formed. Netanyahu's allies are preparing a public campaign to fight it. Herzog says he'll do everything to avoid a new election. If all of this fails, Israelis vote again early next year.

Now Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, where October ended with oil climbing again. Brent crude was trading around one hundred seventeen dollars a barrel on Friday, up about fifteen percent this month, its fourth straight monthly rise. The trigger is a new kind of attack. Iran says tankers hit naval mines on an "unauthorized route." But Britain's maritime security agency says one tanker was damaged by explosives a small boat attached to its hull. Those are called limpet mines, bombs stuck to the side of a ship. Analysts at the Institute for the Study of War say it's Iran's first successful limpet mine attack of this war. One ship had twenty-seven Indian crew, and India says they're all safe. The head of US forces in the region, Admiral Brad Cooper, briefed Trump in person on military options, and the Times of Israel reports ten US military transport planes landed in Israel. But Trump's tone was softer on Friday: Iran "wants to talk," he said, the US "has time," and the war "will be wrapped up soon." Qatari mediators are back in Tehran. Trump has said there will be no strike before Tuesday's midterms.

Closer to your wallet, two Israeli cost-of-living stories pulling in opposite directions. Home prices keep sliding: the Times of Israel's monthly housing snapshot shows the downward trend continuing, with a record number of unsold new apartments and developers offering bigger and bigger discounts, even as Israel builds at a record pace. But the supermarket is getting pricier. Egg prices went up two point one percent last Sunday, and flour may rise by as much as fourteen percent, which could push up the price of bread and pasta too.

The war is also spilling into Saudi Arabia. The Houthis, the Iran-backed rebels who control much of Yemen, have been firing missiles and drones at Saudi cities and oil sites since fighting with the Saudis flared again in July. This week the Saudi-led coalition said it shot down drones over the Mecca and Medina regions, home to Islam's two holiest sites, and missiles were intercepted over Riyadh. The US has already pulled non-essential diplomats and their families out of Saudi Arabia.

In Europe, a constitutional showdown in Poland. The centrist government suspended the chief justice of the Constitutional Tribunal, Poland's top court for constitutional questions, Bogdan Święczkowski. He was appointed under the previous government run by Law and Justice, the nationalist right-wing party. The government argues the court's judges were improperly appointed and the court isn't legitimate. Law and Justice and its ally, President Karol Nawrocki, say the government is destroying judicial independence. On Friday riot police surrounded the court, the court's own account called it a "coup d'état," and Nawrocki cut short a trip to Africa and walked into the building past the police. Both sides accuse the other of breaking the constitution, and there's no referee left that both accept.

In Ukraine, a strange mix of escalation and quiet deals. A Russian strike on a crowded train platform in Dnipro killed at least fourteen people, and Ukraine's prime minister said Russia is "deliberately hunting civilians." At the same time, the US approved the sale of five hundred Patriot missiles, the air defense interceptors that shoot down incoming missiles, to Ukraine for about two point one billion dollars. The Wall Street Journal reports Ukraine has quietly paused strikes on Russian oil refineries under American pressure, at least until after the midterms, since those strikes push up gas prices. And in Oleshky, a Russian-occupied town in the south, both sides agreed to let in food and medicine and let residents leave, in a local ceasefire reportedly brokered by First Lady Melania Trump.

Two more quick ones. In Russia, Novaya Gazeta, the independent newspaper whose editor Dmitry Muratov won the Nobel Peace Prize, will close on November ninth after three decades, blaming pressure from the authorities. Six of its journalists have been murdered over the years. And here's the most-shared story in America yesterday: Pope Leo the Fourteenth, the first American pope, requested a mail ballot from Illinois for Tuesday's midterms. Even the Pope votes absentee.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, where Tuesday's midterms are forcing hard choices. Start with Nancy Pelosi. The former House Speaker said on a podcast that she disagrees with Mayor Mamdani and with Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate candidate in Michigan, on Israel. Anyone in the party who denies Israel's right to exist as a Jewish state, she said, is wrong: "It's more than concerning: It's wrong." Coming from one of the most powerful Democrats of the last twenty years, that's a notable break, four days before an election.

The Michigan race itself got uglier. Hasan Piker, a hugely popular left-wing streamer and an anti-Zionist, meaning someone who opposes Israel existing as a Jewish state, has campaigned with El-Sayed. On a livestream, he talked about El-Sayed volunteers knocking on doors in West Bloomfield, a heavily Jewish suburb of Detroit. He called it "the equivalent of going to that, like, Klan, KKK neighborhood to stump for a Black candidate," and said refusing to vote for El-Sayed over Israel is "Jewish supremacy." The local state representative, Noah Arbit, said the rhetoric "threatens every single one of us in West Bloomfield." The background: many Jewish voters there were already upset with El-Sayed for saying "hurt people hurt people" after an attempted terror attack on a local synagogue and preschool. He faces Republican Mike Rogers on Tuesday.

In Georgia, Jewish voters face a different dilemma. Senator Jon Ossoff, who is Jewish, has angered some Jewish groups by voting to block certain arms sales to Israel. His Republican challenger, Congressman Mike Collins, has strong backing from the Republican Jewish Coalition, but faces questions about people around him: his son-in-law made antisemitic remarks, and a former chief of staff left after reports of a group chat with white nationalist figures. Collins says, "I don't believe or support those views." J Street, the liberal pro-Israel group, is spending three million dollars against Republicans backed by AIPAC.

And back home, a sweet one. ## Tech, startups & AI

Next, tech, and a stock drop that tells you a lot about where software is going. ServiceNow, the Silicon Valley company whose software big companies use to run IT, HR and customer service, reported strong results on Wednesday, with revenue over four billion dollars, up more than twenty percent. But it forecast slightly slower growth in its backlog, the pile of signed contracts not yet billed, and the stock fell about twenty-five percent. What everyone's talking about, though, is what the CEO, Bill McDermott, said about jobs. Thanks to its own AI products, ServiceNow says it saved more than a billion dollars a year and hired fewer people. McDermott called a smaller workforce "a gorgeous situation." One detail worth noticing for your career: the number of quota-carrying salespeople, sellers with a revenue target, went up by double digits, while sales operations, the support staff behind them, shrank significantly. And ServiceNow keeps shopping in Israel: it closed the seven point seven five billion dollar purchase of the cyber company Armis in April, and in September bought Sweep, an Israeli startup building AI agents for go-to-market teams.

Anthropic, the AI company that makes Claude, filed to go public this week, and now a reality check on its finances. The Financial Times reports that Blackstone, the giant investment firm and an anchor investor in a sixty-three billion dollar deal to fund Anthropic's chips, told investors to expect a "junk" credit rating, a grade meaning riskier debt, after the IPO. Specifically double-B, the top tier of junk, just below investment grade, the safe category. In plain words: lenders see a company spending enormous sums on computing power, so its borrowing will cost more. Anthropic and Blackstone declined to comment.

Two deals that show where the money is flowing. Salesforce is buying Rune Technologies, a two-year-old startup whose AI helps the US military predict supply needs in the field, for a reported three hundred seventy-five million dollars. It's defense tech, technology built for militaries, and it's Salesforce's latest acquisition after buying Braze last week. And Waymo, Google's self-driving car company, will use Toyota's electric bZ4X as a robotaxi, a driverless car you hail like an Uber, built at a Toyota factory in Japan, for Japan and the UK.

Now Israeli startups. A tiny Israeli AI company called Two Delta, with fewer than ten employees, raised tens of millions of dollars in a seed round from the hedge fund billionaire Bill Ackman and former Google chairman Eric Schmidt. Its founders are Dvir Zeltzer, a former senior Google manager, and Eli David, a veteran AI researcher. It makes AI models run faster and cheaper, using a trick called speculative decoding, where a small model drafts the answer and a big model checks it. Also this week: WhiteSage came out of stealth with twenty million dollars to buy traditional businesses and run them with AI. BloomX raised thirteen million to pollinate crops with robots and AI, which matters because bee populations are struggling. And Sightec raised six million for technology that lets drones navigate when GPS is jammed or faked, a big deal for defense. The bigger picture from IVC, the Israeli research firm: Israeli tech had fifty-five exits, meaning companies sold or listed on a stock market, in the third quarter, and one hundred thirty-eight so far this year, almost matching all of twenty twenty-four.

## Cybersecurity & GTM

Now cybersecurity and go-to-market, and the biggest theft in crypto history. On Thursday evening, hackers drained about six point three billion dollars from Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, a platform where people buy, sell and store digital currencies. Blockchain analysts at TRM Labs and Chainalysis say most of it moved in about twenty minutes, across ten different blockchains. The money came out of Binance's hot wallets, the accounts kept online so customers can withdraw quickly, which is exactly why they're a target. Both firms say with high confidence the attackers were TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit linked to the Lazarus Group, the same team the FBI blamed for stealing one and a half billion dollars from the exchange Bybit last year. That was the old record. Experts say stolen crypto is a major source of money for North Korea's weapons programs.

How did they get in? Binance first pointed to an engineer's hacked laptop, then said it found "additional points of breach." About three billion dollars has been frozen, more than two point three billion of it by Tether and Circle, the companies behind the two biggest stablecoins, digital coins pegged to the dollar, which can block stolen coins. Binance says it will cover every dollar from its own funds and emergency reserve, telling users, "Binance will absorb the financial impact of these stolen funds, not our users." It says it's on track to start restoring withdrawals this weekend, and promises a proof of reserves, a public snapshot showing it holds customers' assets, by Monday. Bitcoin fell to a six-week low around seventy-six thousand dollars. One expert put the lesson simply: "Any assets held at a centralised exchange are only as safe as the exchange itself."

Now the fundraising side. Cyera, the Israeli data security company founded in twenty twenty-one by Yotam Segev and Tamar Bar-Ilan, is in early talks to raise money at a twenty billion dollar valuation, according to The Information. Its trajectory is wild: three billion in twenty twenty-four, six billion in twenty twenty-five, twelve billion in January, and now possibly twenty, just four months later. Notice how its story has evolved: it started as "find and protect your sensitive data," and now it's pitching protection for AI agents and non-human identities, the logins used by software and bots instead of people. Same product core, new narrative, riding the biggest budget line in security right now.

On the AI side, Google launched Gemini Four Carbon, its newest frontier model, and is pitching it heavily on cybersecurity: finding software vulnerabilities and writing patches. Interestingly, it's releasing it first only to vetted defenders through a program called Fairwind, while it tests safeguards. "Defenders first" is becoming a launch strategy, and a marketing message, for every big AI lab.

And a GTM lesson from the law. Crosby, a two-year-old AI-first law firm backed by Sequoia, which has raised about eighty-five million dollars, put in a bid to buy the brand, trademark and some internal data of the collapsing firm Weil Gotshal. Think about that from a marketing angle: a startup bidding for a ninety-five-year-old name, because trust and brand recognition are the hardest things for a new company to build.

## Business story

Now our business story, and it's about the app on your phone that brings your groceries. On Thursday, DoorDash agreed to buy Instacart for about seventeen billion dollars. Let's go back to the beginning.

Instacart was founded in twenty twelve in San Francisco by Apoorva Mehta, a former Amazon engineer. The idea was simple: you order groceries in an app, and a gig worker called a shopper goes to your local store, picks your items off the shelf and drives them to you. For years it struggled to make money. Then the pandemic hit, and suddenly everyone wanted groceries delivered. Instacart went public in twenty twenty-three and today works with nearly one hundred thousand stores. It also built a big advertising business, where brands pay to show up when you search for cereal, and software that lets supermarkets run their own online shops.

DoorDash was started in twenty thirteen by Stanford students, including Tony Xu, who is still the CEO. It began delivering takeout around Palo Alto and became the dominant food delivery app in the US. For years, the two stayed in their lanes: DoorDash for restaurants, Instacart for groceries. But they've been invading each other's turf, with DoorDash adding groceries and Instacart adding restaurants.

The deal: DoorDash pays sixty-five dollars a share, mostly in cash, a twenty-six percent premium, the extra paid over the market price, versus Instacart's price before rumors leaked. Instacart's CEO, Chris Rogers, says over fourteen years Instacart "transformed online grocery." Tony Xu's line: "Grocery is one of the hardest things to get right online." Customers would get access to fifty thousand more stores, and members of DashPass and Instacart Plus would pay one subscription instead of two.

But don't expect it tomorrow. The deal isn't expected to close until late twenty twenty-seven or the first half of twenty twenty-eight, because it needs antitrust approval, the regulators who stop mergers that hurt competition, in the US, Canada and Israel. Critics will argue that one company controlling most delivery means higher fees for restaurants and stores, and Mayor Mamdani has already criticized the merger publicly. Bloomberg reports that Instacart held talks with Uber first and walked away. The takeaway for you: in a mature market, growth often means buying your rival's customers, and the real prize here may be the advertising business, the same retail media trend, stores and shopping apps selling ads, that's reshaping marketing jobs.

And quickly, the Weil Gotshal story, since it's such a New York institution. Founded in nineteen thirty-one, Weil handled the bankruptcies of Enron and Lehman Brothers and has about twelve hundred lawyers and more than two billion dollars in yearly revenue. Then a planned merger with the rival firm Willkie Farr fell apart, star partners left for competitors, and the partner chosen to take over leadership turned the job down. Its leaders have recommended an "orderly wind-down." It would be the biggest law firm collapse since Dewey and LeBoeuf in twenty twelve. The lesson: even a two-billion-dollar business is really just its people, and when the top people walk, the brand can't hold them.

## Art & design

Now art and design. London's National Gallery announced the largest exhibition ever devoted to Piero della Francesca, the fifteenth-century Italian painter famous for calm, geometric scenes and almost pastel light. It opens next October and runs until January twenty twenty-eight. Ahead of it, scientists studied two of his masterpieces in Urbino, "The Flagellation of Christ" and "The Madonna of Senigallia," to learn more about how he painted them. If you've never seen a Piero, his "Baptism of Christ" is already at the National Gallery and it's the perfect introduction.

In New York, Christie's will sell works from the collection of Agnes Gund, the legendary New York collector and longtime Museum of Modern Art patron, led by a Vija Celmins painting estimated at about eight million dollars. Celmins, born in Latvia, is famous for painstaking, almost photographic images of night skies, ocean waves and desert floors, and she works so slowly that her paintings rarely come up for sale. That scarcity is exactly what drives her prices.

And under your feet back home: MTA Arts and Design, the program that puts permanent art in subway stations, commissioned new works from three artists, including Trevor Paglen, known for art about surveillance and secret military sites, and Howardena Pindell, the abstract painter famous for her dotted, textured canvases. Next time you're waiting for the train, look up.

## Wellness

Your wellness idea today fits the clock change in the US tonight. A University of Texas at Austin study of young adults who wore Fitbits for months found that people who exercised on more days got more non-REM sleep, the deep, restorative stage, and woke up with better mood and less stress. About ten minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, the kind that feels like a six out of ten, was linked to better sleep. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. But the practical move is easy: instead of saving workouts for two big sessions, aim for at least ten brisk minutes every day.
