## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Tuesday, November tenth, and this is your Morning Brief. At four this afternoon, Israel's new Knesset is sworn in, and the first big fight is already on: who gets the speaker's chair. In Washington, a Republican congressman has filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over the Iran war, with a vote possible this week. And in Cyber Watch, Vanta, the American compliance company, is buying Israeli AI security startup Pillar for about one hundred fifty million dollars.

## Israel

We start here at home, where today is a big ceremonial day with a very political edge. At four p.m. in Jerusalem, all one hundred twenty members of the twenty-sixth Knesset take their oath. Forty-six of them are first-timers, including fifteen of the twenty-one lawmakers from Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party. There will be thirty-three women, up from twenty-nine in the last Knesset. The youngest is Yonatan Shalev of B'Yachad, who is twenty-four, the youngest Israeli lawmaker ever, breaking a record from nineteen fifty-nine. The new class also carries October seventh with it. Noam Tibon, a retired general who set out alone that morning to rescue his son's family from Kibbutz Nahal Oz, joins with B'Yachad. And Sharon Sharabi joins with Yisrael Beytenu. One of his brothers, Yossi, was killed in Hamas captivity, and another, Eli, was held in Gaza for almost five hundred days.

Now the fight. The Knesset speaker controls the parliament's agenda: which bills come up, when votes happen, how committees are run. Right now that's Amir Ohana from Netanyahu's Likud. Eisenkot's bloc wants to replace him fast, even before a new government exists, because a friendly speaker could advance bills Netanyahu has blocked, like a state commission of inquiry into October seventh.

On Monday, Eisenkot met Avigdor Liberman, the Yisrael Beytenu leader whose seats he needs. According to Haaretz, Ynet and Channel 12, they agreed the next speaker should come from Yashar, probably Eisenkot himself, temporarily, stepping down once he forms a government. They also reportedly agreed to reach out to the two ultra-Orthodox parties, Shas and United Torah Judaism, the Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox alliance, including on a softer version of a military draft law. Liberman is quoted telling Eisenkot, "Netanyahu wants us to establish a government with the Arab parties. It's good for him. A government with the Haredim stresses him out; he knows it can last for four years." And, "You'll be the prime minister; I'm not seeking the job for myself."

But the ultra-Orthodox aren't playing along yet. Yashar sent letters asking Shas and United Torah Judaism to sign on to replacing Ohana. United Torah Judaism's chairman, Yaakov Asher, said no. A new speaker, he said, "should take place simultaneously with the formation of a new government," and he warned against "an absurd situation" where the speaker opposes the government. Replacing Ohana takes a majority of sixty-one, and Eisenkot's bloc has forty-eight.

Here's why the ultra-Orthodox are suddenly so courted. They broke with Netanyahu because he never passed a law protecting yeshiva students from the draft. Channel 12 reports they'd want a one-year, maybe eighteen-month, arrangement with no real enlistment and no arrests of draft dodgers. Netanyahu is fighting back. Channel 12 says he tried to meet Rabbi Dov Lando and Rabbi Moshe Hillel Hirsch, leaders of the party's "Lithuanian" wing, the non-Hasidic yeshiva world, and was turned away. United Torah Judaism officials called the pressure "frenzied." Channel 12 also published what it says are transcripts of a Netanyahu confidant, Natan Eshel, urging Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas not to back Eisenkot. Abbas's reported answer: "We'll do what's good for us." Eshel says "no messages were conveyed."

Eisenkot's deadline is December sixth, with a possible fourteen-day extension.

One more big story: American military aid. On Election Day, Trump said the roughly four billion dollars a year "gets cut off at the end of the year." Ynet now reports that's not what's on the table. The current memorandum of understanding gives Israel three point eight billion a year through twenty twenty-eight: three point three billion in foreign military financing, money Israel must spend on American weapons, plus five hundred million for missile defense like Iron Dome. The new deal under negotiation would continue that for five more years and then end it, while possibly raising missile defense funding. A senior Israeli official put it this way: "We are moving here from a model of aid to a model of partnership." The goal is to sign before the new Democratic Congress arrives in January.

## World

Now to the world. In Washington, Trump met Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, at the White House on Monday. Qatar is the main go-between with Iran, almost nine months into the war. On the table is a two-day proposal: the US would lift its naval blockade and release some frozen Iranian money, and Iran would take steps toward letting nuclear inspectors back and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, before bigger talks. Washington hasn't accepted it. The US wants Iran to give ground first on its stockpile of enriched uranium, nuclear fuel refined close to bomb grade. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, replied: "Meet the requirement, including end to war on all fronts, and you will see results." Secretary of State Marco Rubio, heading to a G7 meeting in Paris, said, "I wouldn't say we're on the verge of a breakthrough."

Back in Congress, Republican Thomas Massie of Kentucky has filed articles of impeachment, the formal charges in an impeachment, a process to remove an official, against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. His thirty-four-page resolution has eight articles. Three accuse Hegseth of waging war on Iran without Congress's approval. Others cite a strike on an Iranian school and the Pentagon's boat strikes in the Caribbean. Massie's move starts a two-legislative-day clock, meaning Republican leaders must schedule a vote quickly, likely this week. Here's why it's close: the new Democratic majority doesn't take over until January, so Republicans still narrowly control the House, and Democrats need some Republican votes. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries is a "hard yes." Speaker Mike Johnson says impeaching Hegseth during hostilities would be "a terrible, terrible look." Some Republicans, like Don Bacon of Nebraska, say they're "wrestling" with it. Hegseth calls them "sham articles" from a "disgruntled, defeated congressman." If impeached, he'd be only the third cabinet secretary in American history, after William Belknap in eighteen seventy-six and Alejandro Mayorkas in twenty twenty-four.

And in Turkey, the UN climate summit, COP31, short for the thirty-first Conference of the Parties, opened Monday in Antalya and runs until November twentieth. Turkey hosts, Australia runs the negotiations, and the United States isn't there at all, after Trump pulled out of the Paris Agreement, the twenty fifteen global climate deal, again. The summit's president, Turkey's environment minister Murat Kurum, admitted the world will "overshoot the one point five degree target," the warming limit countries agreed on in Paris, and said, "We will not look away, and we will not lower our ambition."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the mayor is getting ready for winter. Mayor Mamdani announced a twenty-five million dollar expansion of the city's cold-weather response. The background is grim: by mid-February last winter, nineteen people had been found dead outdoors during a long cold snap. The plan expands Code Blue, the emergency protections for homeless New Yorkers when it hits freezing. More than three hundred extra outreach workers will canvass the five boroughs, up to fifty-five mobile medical units will be on the streets, and the city is adding eight hundred shelter beds for the coldest nights, plus up to twenty warming buses. "We have spent the past year preparing for any challenge winter throws our way," Mamdani said.

And Mamdani is looking beyond the city. He confirmed he's meeting national labor leaders, including the heads of the painters' union and the flight attendants' union, about the Democratic Party's future, and Axios reports he wants progressives to have their own candidate in twenty twenty-eight. Mamdani's line: "I don't think that there's a tension in helping to elect Democrats."

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a scare in Washington that ended the right way. The metal detector went off. A security staffer checked him with a handheld wand, and Finn admitted he had a gun clipped to his waistband, a fully loaded revolver. In Illinois, voters in eight towns, including Oak Park outside Chicago, approved nonbinding measures, meaning symbolic with no legal force, urging the state to repeal its anti-BDS law. That twenty fifteen law bars state pension funds from investing in companies that boycott Israel, and it was the first of many like it around the country. In Oak Park, about sixty-five percent voted yes. A local rabbi, Adir Glick, said the campaign hurt, but "it also galvanized our Jewish community."

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, released two new models on Monday, Mythos five point two and Fable five point two, ahead of its planned IPO. It says they're better at coding, reasoning and vision, and Fable is about thirty percent faster. It also cut Fable's price for input, the text you send in, from ten dollars to eight dollars per million tokens, which TechCrunch notes makes it cheaper than OpenAI's rival, GPT-6 Astra. The bigger conversation is about safety. A report from METR, an independent group that tests AI models, suggests Anthropic may be approaching recursive self-improvement, meaning AI that helps train better AI, finding that its internal models were roughly doubling the pace of its own research.

And here in Israel, the government wants its own AI muscle. Erez Askal, head of the National AI Directorate in the Prime Minister's Office, told AI and Cyber Week at Tel Aviv University on Monday that the national target is about three hundred thousand GPUs, the chips that train and run AI. About a hundred thousand would go to universities, government, defense and startups, and the rest would come from private data center builders, all needing about two gigawatts of electricity. The goal is an Israeli foundation model, a big general-purpose AI built at home, so the country doesn't depend on foreign cloud companies. One caveat: only one hundred thousand chips are formally approved so far.

## Business story

Our business story is Israeli, and it's a lesson in what happens when you cut too deep. Monday.com, the Tel Aviv company whose colorful work-management boards are used by teams around the world, reported results on Monday. On paper, a good quarter: revenue of three hundred seventy-seven and a half million dollars, up nineteen percent, and profits well above Wall Street's expectations. But its guidance, its forecast, for the next quarter is only about fourteen percent growth, and the stock fell around seven percent in early trading.

Here's the backstory. In July, Monday.com laid off about twenty percent of its workforce. Its chief revenue officer, Casey George, said back in August that the cuts focused on "non-quota carriers," people who don't personally carry a sales target, which means a lot of marketing, enablement and customer success. Now he's admitted the disruption "lingered, candidly, a little longer than we thought." The company is also moving upmarket, toward big enterprise customers, where deals take longer, while more small businesses are leaving.

The bright spot is AI. Monday.com's AI revenue went from roughly zero at the start of the year to about thirty-six million dollars a year, mostly from AI credits, prepaid units customers spend on AI features. Co-CEO Roy Mann says the product is moving "from managing work to executing it." The takeaway for you as a marketer: cutting the people who build pipeline saves money fast, but the cost shows up a few quarters later in growth. That's a strong argument the next time a company wonders whether marketing really matters.

## Art and design

Next, art, where Caravaggio, the dramatic Italian master of light and shadow, had quite a weekend. Two scholars announced two "new" Caravaggios in twenty-four hours. On Saturday, Gianni Papi presented a small portrait of a young man that sold at auction in Vienna a few years ago as an anonymous Tuscan work. On Sunday, the respected scholar Maria Cristina Terzaghi announced in La Repubblica that a painting found in the back room of a Paris gallery, "Young Man with a Vase of Roses," is Caravaggio's original, from around fifteen ninety-six. Until now it was known only from copies, including one at the High Museum in Atlanta. She traces it to the famous Borghese collection in Rome. Be a little skeptical: Papi's evidence hasn't been published yet, and even for Terzaghi's painting, the trail from Rome to Paris still has a missing link. But that's how attributions work, deciding who really painted something, and if they hold, these pictures go from minor to museum-level overnight.

## Travel

Now travel, with news that could touch every flight you book from here. On Sunday, Israel's Tax Authority proposed a new tax on every flight leaving the country, based on the plane's weight and how far it flies. It's meant to cover the cost of pollution, noise and climate damage, and the authority points out that aviation is barely taxed now: there's no VAT, value added tax, on international tickets, and jet fuel is exempt from fuel tax. Britain, France and Germany already tax departing flights. The estimated cost per passenger: about twelve shekels on a domestic flight, about fifty-eight to Europe, and about two hundred seventy-four to the US or East Asia, roughly four to five percent of the ticket price. It would raise about one point one billion shekels a year. Israeli airline Air Haifa has already pushed back: "This tax would mean higher ticket prices for Israeli citizens." For now it's only a proposal ahead of next year's budget, so nothing changes yet, but if you're planning a New York trip in twenty twenty-seven, keep an eye on it.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with good news for whole-milk fans. A small University of Toronto trial in The Journal of Nutrition gave seventy-four adults with overweight or obesity either a low-dairy diet or three servings a day of full-fat dairy foods for twelve weeks. The full-fat groups didn't gain more weight or body fat, and their cholesterol didn't get worse. Their blood pressure actually improved, and they got more calcium, protein and vitamin D. Two caveats: the study was small and short, and it was funded partly through a dairy research program. The lead researcher's advice is refreshingly simple: "Keep it simple, eat a variety of foods and not too much of anything." So you don't need to fear the real yogurt.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

The big deal of the day is Israeli, and it's another sign of where the money in cyber is going. Vanta, the American company whose software helps businesses prove they're secure and compliant, meaning they follow the rules and standards that customers and auditors demand, has agreed to buy Pillar Security, an Israeli AI security startup. CTech reports the price at about one hundred fifty million dollars in cash and shares. The companies confirmed the deal but not the number.

First, who's who. Vanta is a big name: more than twenty thousand customers, and it passed three hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue in April, growing sixty-three percent a year. Three quarters of Forbes' list of the top fifty AI companies use it. Pillar was founded in twenty twenty-three by CEO Ziv Karliner and Dor Sarig. It helps companies find all the AI systems and agents they're running, test them against attacks, and protect them while they run, which is called runtime security. Its researchers found security holes in popular AI coding tools like Cursor, Codex and Gemini's command-line tool, which the makers then fixed.

Now, why this is a great result. Pillar raised only nine million dollars in total, with a seed round led by Shield Capital. Selling for around one hundred fifty million is more than sixteen times the money invested, in about three years. It has just over twenty employees, and all of them are expected to join Vanta, with Pillar becoming Vanta's Israeli R and D center, research and development. Karliner and Sarig stay on to lead the AI security business.

Why Vanta wants it: companies are writing AI policies, but they can't prove those policies are actually followed. Vanta's CEO, Christina Cacioppo, put it in one line: "A policy document doesn't get you there." Pillar's technology turns the rules into real controls.

Compare it with the deal everyone's waiting on. Palo Alto Networks is in advanced talks to buy Clutch Security, the Israeli startup that controls the credentials AI agents use, for about five hundred million dollars, still unsigned as of our last check. Security Boulevard adds some numbers: Clutch has raised twenty-eight and a half million dollars and is nearing ten million in annual recurring revenue, so the reported price is about fifty times its revenue. That's an extraordinary multiple, the kind buyers pay when they're afraid of missing a category. The pattern is clear. Big platforms are shopping for Israeli teams that secure AI agents, and they're paying for capability, not revenue.

## People moves

Now people moves, and we start with a tough one in Israel. Axonius, the Israeli-founded cyber unicorn that helps companies see every device and app they own, is cutting sixty-six jobs worldwide, about nine percent of its staff. Forty-six of them are in Israel, about twenty-two percent of its two hundred seven Israeli employees, who've been called to pre-dismissal hearings, the legally required meeting before an Israeli employer can fire someone. CEO Joe Diamond said, "We've made changes to our engineering team, as we reshape how we build around a new AI-driven way of working." Diamond became interim CEO in February, when co-founder Dean Sysman moved to executive chairman, and was made permanent in May. It's Axonius's third round of cuts in under a year: about one hundred people a year ago, forty in February, and now this, even though the company passed two hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue in May, growing thirty-five percent. Remember, last week we told you Axonius had just hired a new chief revenue officer and CTO. So the message is: growth at the top, fewer engineers, more AI.

On the hiring side, iboss, an American company that secures how employees and AI agents use the internet and data, named Pedro Diaz as chief revenue officer and president of go-to-market. He comes from Tanium, a well-known endpoint security company, where he was CRO. And iboss hired Brian Black as VP, vice president, of sales engineering, the technical team that demos and sets up the product for customers. Black comes from Deep Instinct, the Israeli-founded AI security company. Two senior go-to-market hires on the same day usually means a company is getting ready to push hard on sales, and that marketing will be expected to feed that push.

No new chief marketing officers at cyber companies crossed the wires since yesterday, so we'll keep watching.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons from this week.

First, Vanta's positioning is a masterclass. The press release headline is "Vanta to Acquire Pillar Security to Turn AI Policy Into Practice." Notice there's no technical jargon there. It speaks to the buyer's real fear: we have a policy, but can we prove it to the board or an auditor? When your product is complex, sell the outcome in five words.

Second, events. This is AI and Cyber Week at Tel Aviv University, one of the biggest cyber conferences in the world, with more than nine thousand attendees from over eighty countries and over fifty side events, roundtables and workshops. For cyber marketers, weeks like this are prime time: companies time announcements, host dinners and run side events to get in front of investors, government buyers and global press all in one place. If you want to see how Israeli cyber companies market themselves live, this is the week to watch their LinkedIn feeds, and to show up if you can.

Third, channel marketing. On Monday, CRN, a major trade publication for IT resellers, published its twenty twenty-six Stellar Startups list, and companies immediately put out press releases about making it. One example: Reach Security, a company in exposure management, finding and fixing weak spots before attackers do. Reach sells only through channel partners, resellers and service providers who sell the product on its behalf. For a company like that, an award in the partners' own trade press isn't vanity. It's a sales tool that helps partners pitch you to their customers. The lesson: pick awards your buyers and partners actually read.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Vanta's new Israeli R and D center. A big American company with an Israeli team that's about to launch new AI products in January will need someone here to tell that story. Product marketing for AI security is a natural fit, and the roles may not be posted yet, so it's worth reaching out to the Pillar founders or Vanta's marketing team directly.

Second, iboss. A new revenue chief and a new sales engineering head usually means new campaigns and more demand generation. iboss isn't Israeli, but with a leader from Deep Instinct on board, Israeli networks may be the way in.

Third, use AI and Cyber Week. Look at who's speaking and which startups are pitching, especially Seed to Series B companies with Israeli founders, and use the event as your opener.
