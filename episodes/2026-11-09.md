Good morning Sophie. It's Monday, November ninth, and this is your Morning Brief. Gadi Eisenkot officially has the mandate, and he's pitching what he calls a "national emergency government." Trump says new strikes on Iran are "highly unlikely" if talks move forward, and he says part of why Republicans lost the midterms is "a great dislike" for Israel. And in tech, Nvidia's board is investigating its CEO, Jensen Huang, after he admitted an affair with a woman a US memo says was recruited by Chinese intelligence.

## Israel

We start here at home. On Sunday at five in the afternoon, at the President's Residence in Jerusalem, President Herzog formally handed Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief who leads the Yashar party, the mandate to form Israel's next government. Herzog told him, "I expect you to do everything in your power to establish a government that will serve the entire people."

Eisenkot's speech was striking. He said Israel is "in a state of emergency, approaching a point of no return," and called for a national emergency government, meaning a broad coalition built around a crisis, not around normal party politics. "Creative and unconventional political steps will be required," he said, and "all of us will need to compromise." His door, he said, is open to any party that accepts four principles: treating Hamas as a terrorist group whose military and governing power must be destroyed, Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, the values of the Declaration of Independence, and mandatory service for everyone. He even invited Likud, Netanyahu's party, to join, as long as it cuts ties with what he calls extremists and serves under him. Netanyahu's name wasn't in that invitation.

Now the math, because it's tricky. Eisenkot got sixty-seven recommendations, but recommending someone is not the same as joining his government. His own change bloc has forty-eight seats out of one hundred twenty. Ra'am, the Arab Islamist party, has eight, and Haaretz reports it will back him even without a signed deal. But several hawkish members of his own camp say they won't rely on Arab parties at all.

So the other path runs through the ultra-Orthodox. Here's the idea: forty-eight seats, plus the seven of United Torah Judaism, the Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox alliance, is fifty-five. If Shas, the Sephardi ultra-Orthodox party, with six seats, also comes along, or at least doesn't vote against, that's sixty-one, a bare majority. That's why Avigdor Liberman's move matters so much. For years, Liberman built his whole brand on forcing the Haredim into the army. On Friday he said he's open to "painful compromises" on enlistment to form a government without Netanyahu or the Arab parties. He's been hammered for it, and on Sunday he insisted he still wants Haredim to serve. Channel 13 even reports a secret back channel between Liberman and the Haredi parties going on for months.

Shas calls the reports that it might join Eisenkot "total fake news" and says it belongs to the right-wing bloc. And there's a twist inside Shas. Its leader, Aryeh Deri, announced he's leaving the Knesset but staying on as party chair. Shas fell from eleven seats to six in last month's election, its worst result in three decades. Deri tried to resign as chair, but the party's rabbis refused. He can't serve as a minister anyway, because of past convictions for bribery and tax fraud.

On the other side, Netanyahu keeps calling for a "government of national unity," but one that includes his right-wing and religious partners. He calls the Arab parties "terror supporters." Kan, the public broadcaster, reports that an envoy he sent to explore a deal with Eisenkot was "rejected out of hand." From Washington, Trump weighed in too, saying Eisenkot and Netanyahu are "very similar in terms of their policy."

One more piece of the fight over legitimacy. Likud and its allies claim the election was "stolen" through fraud in Arab towns, without showing evidence. On Sunday night, Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara said, "I am not aware of any basis that could, even remotely, influence the election results." Likud says it will appeal the results anyway.

What's next: Eisenkot has twenty-eight days, with a possible fourteen-day extension. Watch the rabbis of United Torah Judaism this week. If they say yes to talks, everything speeds up.

Two quick items. Tensions in mixed cities: police arrested eleven people after a mass brawl in Lod, following a far-right "sovereignty march" through an Arab neighborhood. And the army says two hundred eighty-five ultra-Orthodox men enlisted last week, with more draft days planned through December.

In Gaza, a terrible weekend of weather. Heavy rain on Saturday flooded tens of thousands of tents where displaced families live, more than seventy thousand according to Channel 12. Gaza's Hamas-run civil defense says five people, including three children, died when a sand embankment collapsed onto tents near Deir al-Balah. Israel's UN mission says Britain is rallying around fifty countries to urge Israel to loosen limits on supplies before winter. Israel's position is that reconstruction can't start until Hamas disarms. And Channel 12 reports that Trump, in a call last Thursday, pressed Netanyahu to move forward on his twenty-point Gaza plan now that the election is over, whoever ends up governing.

## World

Now to the world, starting with Iran. On Sunday, Trump told CNN that new military action is "highly unlikely" if talks over Iran's nuclear program and reopening the Strait of Hormuz make progress. He said it five times in a twenty-minute phone call. But he added a big condition: "It depends on whether or not they sign a very strong agreement." He also said America has three aircraft carriers in the region and has picked out targets, including power plants and thirty-eight bridges, that he'd rather not destroy. Secretary of State Marco Rubio says Iran is making "outrageous and ridiculous" demands. Today, Trump meets Qatar's prime minister, Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, in the Oval Office. Qatar is the main go-between with Iran, so that meeting is the one to watch.

The war is spreading in the Gulf too. On Sunday, the Houthis, the Iran-backed rebels who control much of Yemen, fired missiles at Riyadh. Saudi forces say they shot them down, but debris blew out windows across several floors of the Aramco Tower, the state oil giant's office building in the city's financial district. No one was hurt. Here's the background: fighting between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia restarted in July, breaking a truce from twenty twenty-two. A missile hit Riyadh's main airport in October, killing thirteen people. And a day before this attack, Houthi health officials said Saudi airstrikes near Sanaa killed at least thirty-three people.

Back in Washington, Trump gave a long interview to "60 Minutes," the CBS news magazine, about the midterms, where Democrats won both the House and the Senate. He blamed weak candidates, the Iran war and gas prices: "They didn't like the war. They didn't like the fact that gasoline went up." Then he added, "And there's a great dislike, incredibly, for Israel." He also said Israel will need to repay America for the war's costs, admitted his deportation policy was "too tough," and, asked about forgiveness, said, "I do hate some of my enemies, and I always will." For Israel, hearing an American president link his party's losses to Israel is a real warning sign.

In Europe, Poland is in mourning. On Saturday afternoon, a gunman opened fire in Kraków's Main Square, one of the most visited squares in Europe, killing six people: four Poles, a British visitor and an Austrian. Sixteen were wounded, including two police officers. The suspect, a twenty-four-year-old Polish man, was shot and arrested, and his parents and sister were later found dead at the family home. Officials say he was inspired by far-right ideology, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it "an act of terrorism." Poland held national mourning on Sunday and today.

And New Zealand is stuck in limbo. After Saturday's election, Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's center-right coalition has sixty-seven of a projected one hundred thirty-one seats, just one more than it needs. But about fourteen percent of votes are special votes, ballots like those cast from overseas, which are counted later and usually lean left. The final result comes November twenty-seventh, so it really is, in Luxon's words, "too close to call."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where there's an arrest in a killing that shook Brooklyn. Katie Suarez, twenty-eight, was stabbed twice in the neck on October thirtieth while jogging on the Shore Parkway Greenway in Bath Beach, two days before the marathon. She died on November second. Police have now charged Sayed Mohassib, twenty-seven, with second-degree murder. He was held without bail on Saturday night. According to the criminal complaint, video shows him walking toward the path before the attack and running away after, and police found the knife in a Staten Island landfill. Here's why it's become a political story: he was working as a teacher's aide at a Brooklyn public school. amNY reports he had briefly been an NYPD officer and wasn't rehired after supervisors raised concerns. Local lawmakers are demanding an investigation into how the Department of Education hires and screens staff. Council Member Joann Ariola said, "this city owes parents an explanation."

On a lighter note, Mayor Mamdani's office says the city has now helped more than three thousand small businesses get through licenses and permits, including ninety-seven that opened for the first time, and has recommended over fifty cuts to outdated rules and fines.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, on a heavy anniversary. Tonight marks eighty-eight years since Kristallnacht, when Nazi mobs smashed and burned Jewish homes, shops and synagogues across Germany. Synagogues around the world are marking it by keeping their lights on through the night.

The anniversary landed in a tense week. In Philadelphia, the bike ride we told you about went ahead on Sunday. Organizers had called six Jewish-owned restaurants around Rittenhouse Square "Zionist establishments" that "must go." Police say about three hundred pro-Palestinian protesters, two hundred on bikes, faced about two hundred pro-Israel counterprotesters. There were at least three arrests and two officers had minor injuries. The Jewish Federation of Greater Philadelphia called it "Jew-hatred, plain and simple." The organizers say they've been misrepresented and call themselves a nonviolent cycling group.

In Berlin, three people were arrested after an anti-Israel group raised a Palestinian flag on the Reichstag, Germany's parliament building.

And a sermon people are talking about in New York. Rabbi Ammiel Hirsch, senior rabbi of Stephen Wise Free Synagogue in Manhattan and president of the New York Board of Rabbis, told his congregation on Friday night that the golden age of American Jews is over. "October seventh, twenty twenty-three, was its symbolic end," he said, and "we are reverting to the historical norm." He called anti-Zionism "a hateful ideology of destruction" and warned, "We are in for years of turbulence." It's a dark message, but it captures how many American Jews felt watching these midterms.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the biggest story is a scandal at the most valuable chip company in the world. On Sunday, Nvidia's board said it's launching an independent investigation into its CEO, Jensen Huang. Here's what happened. A memo that circulated among US policymakers earlier this year claimed that Alice Huang, no relation, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore chip company, had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, its main spy agency, to get close to him. This weekend, Jensen Huang admitted to staff that he had an affair with her. "I was unfaithful to my wife," he wrote. "There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." The board says it has "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were compromised, and that it has "confidence in Jensen." Independent directors, board members who don't work for the company, will run the review with outside lawyers. Why it matters: AI chips are at the center of the trade war between America and China, and Nvidia is one of the biggest tech employers here in Israel.

One more quick one. Tesla is ending the Cybertruck, its steel, angular electric pickup. Elon Musk confirmed production will "wind down" next year, less than four years after it went on sale, to make room for Cybercabs, Tesla's planned driverless taxis. It sold about eleven and a half thousand in the first nine months of this year, a tiny number for Tesla.

## Business story

Our business story is a comeback, and it's Israeli. JFrog was founded in Israel and makes the software that big companies use to store, track and secure everything their developers build, before it goes live. When Anthropic released an AI model that can find security holes in software, investors panicked that AI would make tools like JFrog's unnecessary. Its stock lost about half its value.

Then on Friday, JFrog's stock jumped about thirty-five percent in one day, to roughly one hundred one dollars, bringing it to about twelve and a half billion dollars, just below its all-time high. The reason was its quarterly results. Revenue was one hundred seventy-nine million dollars, up thirty-one percent from a year ago, and well above its own forecast. Its cloud business grew sixty percent. It raised its guidance, its forecast for the year. And it signed its biggest deal ever, a seventy-six million dollar multiyear contract with a global telecom company.

Here's the clever part, from a marketer's point of view. CEO Shlomi Ben Haim flipped the story. Instead of "AI will replace us," his message became: AI agents write much more code, so there's much more to store, check and secure. In his words, AI "changed everything in terms of the attack surface and the governance requirement." The attack surface means all the places a hacker could get in. Same product, new narrative, and the market bought it. The lesson for you: when a new technology threatens your category, the strongest move is to show how it makes you more necessary, with numbers to back it up.

## Art and design

Next, art, with a prescription you'll like. Art Fund, the British art charity, and Professor Daisy Fancourt of University College London propose a "Recommended Dose of Art": at least sixty minutes in a museum or gallery every month. Their report says regular visits could nearly halve the risk of depression over ten years, and that seeing original artworks in person lowers cortisol, the stress hormone, about twenty-two percent more than looking at reproductions. Their survey found eighty-nine percent of British adults don't get that dose. As a future art agency founder, that's a pitch you could use with clients.

In Washington, a federal judge, Tanya Chutkan, ordered a fourteen-day pause on Trump's planned two hundred fifty-foot triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery, hours after a planning commission approved it eight to four. Critics, including a group of Vietnam veterans, say it would block the symbolic view between the cemetery and the Lincoln Memorial.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness. A study published in BMC Public Health compared about one hundred heart disease patients in Iran with about four hundred people without it, and looked at five lifestyle habits. One stood out: physical activity and recreation. Every extra point on the activity score was linked to thirty-eight percent lower odds of heart disease, more than stress management, healthy eating or social life. One caveat: it's a case-control study, comparing sick and healthy people after the fact, so it shows a link, not proof. Still, combine it with the art dose: walk to a gallery this week and you've ticked two boxes.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

It was another quiet weekend for new cyber rounds, so today we'll do two things: check on the big pending deal, and catch up on October's acquisitions, because SecurityWeek's monthly roundup counted thirty-seven of them, and a few tell you exactly where the market is going.

First, the pending one. Palo Alto Networks' talks to buy Clutch Security, the Israeli startup that protects the keys and credentials AI agents use, for about five hundred million dollars, are still not signed. Nothing new since last week, but if it closes, expect it to be one of the biggest Israeli exits of the quarter.

Now, in case you missed it, the October deal that's my favorite story of the month. HexAI, a tiny Israeli startup, was bought by Hex-Rays, the Belgian company behind IDA Pro, a famous tool security researchers use for reverse engineering, taking finished software apart to see how it works. HexAI was founded just last year by Elon Gliksberg, at twenty-five, later joined by a former Unit 8200 colleague, Amit Schendel, as CTO. Its platform lets researchers build AI agents that hunt through code for hidden flaws, malware and backdoors, secret ways into a computer, even on computers cut off from the internet. The price was reportedly thirty to thirty-five million dollars. And here's the part that matters: it had six employees. It stayed in stealth and bootstrapped, meaning built with no outside investors at all, and then sold. For six people, that's a remarkable result. Hex-Rays says it plans to grow the Israeli team.

The second trend: AI labs are now buying security companies themselves. In October, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, took over Opaque Systems, a confidential computing startup, meaning it keeps data encrypted even while it's being processed. It was an acquihire, a deal mostly to hire the team: the CEO, CTO and some engineers joined Anthropic, and Opaque is winding down. And Cohere, the Canadian AI lab, signed a letter of intent, a preliminary agreement before final terms, to buy AISLE, an American startup whose AI found eighteen of the twenty-four security flaws in a recent update to curl, a piece of software running on billions of devices. Cohere is turning it into a new unit, Cohere Cyber, with a research hub in Prague, as part of its pitch for sovereign AI, meaning AI that countries and companies can run under their own control.

Why this matters for you: cyber startups used to have a short list of buyers, the big security platforms. Now AI labs are shopping too. More buyers means more exits, and more pressure on startups to explain clearly why they're the one worth buying.

## People moves

Now people moves, and honestly, no new marketing chiefs at cyber companies crossed the wires since our last episode. So let's look at a different kind of people story, the one about keeping them.

Calcalist reports that Wiz, the Israeli cloud security company Google bought for thirty-two billion dollars, just flew its employees to Khao Lak, a luxury beach resort area in Thailand, for a five-day trip. The surprise of the week was a private concert by Katy Perry, estimated with production at three to four million dollars. The whole trip is estimated at tens of millions of shekels. It fits Wiz's history: it once ran a private version of "Survivor" in Mauritius for staff. Perry has been criticized online for performing, as she was after a similar private show in Australia in twenty twenty-four that drew pro-Palestinian protests.

Why it matters to you: this is employer branding, a company marketing itself to the people it wants to hire and keep. In Tel Aviv's tight market for cyber engineers, a story like this travels through every WhatsApp group in the industry, which is the point. It also signals that after the Google deal closed, Wiz wants its people to feel they're still in a startup that celebrates big. The risk is the flip side: in a year of layoffs at companies like Nice, a headline about a four million dollar concert can look out of touch.

We'll keep watching Torq, after its CMO left for Abnormal AI last week, and Terra Security, whose new first CMO, Tal Kapulnik, is likely building a team.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three things to know this week.

First, mark your calendar. Cyber Marketing Con, one of the biggest conferences for people who market cybersecurity, runs November fifteenth to eighteenth in Austin, Texas, with virtual access too. It has dedicated summits for cyber CMOs, CEOs and sales leaders, and its focus is go-to-market strategy. Even if you don't attend, the session recordings and the speaker list are a great map of who's hiring and what they're talking about. It's also a natural networking excuse: "I watched your talk" is a much warmer opening line than a cold message.

Second, a fresh number every cyber marketer will see in sales decks this quarter. Ahold Delhaize, the giant grocery company behind Stop and Shop and Food Lion in America, says an October cyberattack will cut its fourth-quarter sales by up to two hundred fifty million euros, and it lowered its forecast for the year. Security vendors love numbers like this, because they turn an abstract risk into a cost a finance chief understands. The smart way to use it is carefully: cite it as a real example, don't use it to scare. Buyers have heard enough fear-based marketing. What works now is fear plus proof, here's what happened, and here's exactly how we'd have reduced the damage.

Third, notice the pattern in October's deals. HexAI, AISLE and Opaque were all bought for one clear capability, not for big revenue. That's the same lesson we saw with Clutch. For a marketer at a young company, the job is to make your one capability unmistakable. If a buyer or a customer can't say in one sentence what you do better than anyone, the market will lump you in with forty competitors.
