## Israel

We start here at home, where the coalition talks are about to get a lot longer. On Saturday, Gadi Eisenkot, the Yashar leader and former army chief who holds the mandate to form a government, told his negotiators to invite Likud to "a marathon series of talks." The first meeting is set for today. Yashar said the invitation reflects a "genuine commitment to forming the broadest possible government."

Why change the format? Until now, the two sides have met for an hour here and there, and the meetings have produced short statements and not much else. A marathon, meaning back-to-back sessions, is a way for Eisenkot to show the public he is serious about unity, and to put the burden on Likud if it fails. His deadline, remember, is in early December.

Likud has its own conditions, and they're tough. It wants an end to "all personal attacks and incitement," and an end to what it calls the "boycott" of Netanyahu, meaning the refusal by Eisenkot's partners to sit with him. It also wants a joint declaration on a unity government of "all parties that support the existence of the State of Israel as the nation-state of the Jewish people," which in practice leaves out the Arab parties. And its proposed guidelines include rejecting a Palestinian state, keeping Israel's "security zones" in Syria, Lebanon and Gaza, and a broad agreement with ultra-Orthodox leaders on the draft.

Eisenkot's camp isn't budging on the biggest point. Naftali Bennett called for a "Zionist unity government headed by Eisenkot," and said his party "will not join" Netanyahu or his bloc "under any circumstances." Eisenkot himself has ruled out serving under Netanyahu. If broad talks fail, he's ready to bring a minority "national emergency government," a government formed to handle the war, to a Knesset vote.

The other side of the puzzle is still the ultra-Orthodox, the strictly religious Haredi community. United Torah Judaism says it's waiting for a revised draft proposal from Eisenkot. But senior rabbis from Degel HaTorah, one of the two factions inside United Torah Judaism, reportedly rejected one idea being discussed: capping exemptions from military service at three percent of each age group. That gap, between rabbis who want yeshiva students exempt and a public that wants everyone to serve after two years of war, is the hardest knot in these talks. And Avigdor Liberman reportedly warned again that a government depending on Arab parties wouldn't last more than a few months.

What's next: watch whether today's meeting actually turns into several days of talks, or ends after an hour like the first one.

Now a painful weekend in Arab Israeli society and East Jerusalem. On Saturday, a man was shot dead in Reineh, an Arab town near Nazareth, the third suspected homicide in twenty-four hours. In Tira, in the center of the country, a woman was killed when a car exploded, and her two children, aged seven and two, were lightly hurt. And in the Shuafat refugee camp in East Jerusalem, a thirteen-year-old boy, Qusay al-Julani, was shot dead outside a school. A resident told Haaretz a man on a motorcycle called the boy over and shot him twice. Police say early findings point to a criminal motive, not a nationalist one. Arab community leaders have warned for years that police aren't doing enough against crime organizations, and weekends like this one are why that issue keeps coming up in the coalition talks.

## World

Now to the world, and the Iran war spread further across the Gulf this weekend. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it attacked American air defense radar systems at two bases in Bahrain, the small Gulf kingdom that hosts the US Navy's Fifth Fleet. Bahrain said an industrial facility was hit and a fire broke out, and it condemned the attacks. Meanwhile, the Houthis in Yemen kept firing at Saudi Arabia for another night. Saudi Arabia even issued a danger alert for the Mecca region, then lifted it minutes later, and the Houthi spokesman insisted there was "no threat to Mecca."

On diplomacy, Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, told Reuters a "fair solution" is possible, but said Iran's enriched uranium, the nuclear fuel at the heart of this war, must not leave the country. That's one of the key sticking points in any deal.

And the sea war is getting worse, not better. ABC News, using data from the British navy's shipping office and the International Maritime Organization, reports that October and November have seen the highest rate of attacks on ships in and around the Strait of Hormuz since the war began. The UN maritime agency counts thirty-four attacks in October and November so far, compared with about fifty over the seven months before. Why? Analysts say the US has been keeping oil moving with ship-to-ship transfers, moving oil from one tanker to another at sea, with tracking systems switched off, to get around Iran's blockade, and Iran is trying to make that workaround, in one analyst's words, "more dangerous, costly, and ultimately unviable." Remember last week's number: insuring a single trip through the strait now costs about sixty-four million dollars.

Now Ukraine, where the week after the Trump, Xi and Putin summit has been brutal. On Friday, World Children's Day, Russia hit the city of Kryvyi Rih with what Ukraine calls a double-tap strike, hitting a spot, then hitting it again when rescuers arrive. A seven-year-old child and a paramedic were killed, and dozens were hurt. The same day, Russia struck three bridges in Kyiv in a massive air attack that killed at least nine people. Putin had vowed revenge for Ukraine's repeated strikes on the Kerch Bridge, the bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea. Zelensky answered that Putin must understand the Crimean bridge "will not stay," and warned that Russia is now even doing triple strikes to "kill as many people as possible."

Here's the part that worries all of Europe. During Friday's attack, two Russian cruise missiles and a drone fell in Poland. Poland is a NATO member, so any Russian weapon landing there raises the question of whether it's an accident or a test of the alliance. On the same day, the European Union approved one billion euros in new military support for Ukraine, and its top diplomat, Kaja Kallas, said, "If Moscow wants peace, its actions tell a different story."

And one quieter but important story. At a UN weapons treaty meeting, one hundred twenty-eight countries agreed to open a path toward an international agreement on lethal autonomous weapons, often called killer robots, weapons that can choose and attack targets without a human deciding. The talks are set to run over two years. With drones and AI now central to the wars in Ukraine and the Gulf, this is the first real attempt to write rules for them.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and if ChatGPT gave you errors last night, you weren't imagining it. When hundreds of millions of people use one tool for work, three hours offline is a business story, not just a tech glitch.

Another AI story is about privacy. Signal's president, Meredith Whittaker, has warned for months that AI agents built into the operating system can see everything on your screen. And an Israeli exit, when a startup is sold or goes public, that you may have missed. ## Business story

Our business story is about Wix, the Israeli website builder, and a pay package that tells you a lot about where tech is heading. Wix is one of Israel's best-known tech companies, about four thousand employees, with revenue of five hundred seventy million dollars last quarter, up thirteen percent.

This year was painful. Wix announced it would cut about one thousand jobs, around a fifth of its workforce, its biggest layoff ever, as AI tools that build websites in seconds squeezed its core business. Now, Calcalist reports, the board wants shareholders to approve new pay for its co-CEOs, Avishai Abrahami and Nir Zohar: a target of nine and a half million dollars a year in stock for each, nineteen million combined, for twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight.

Here's the twist: it's actually about fifteen percent less than their current stock awards, and most of it depends on performance. Sixty percent comes as shares they only receive if Wix hits targets. The main target is the Rule of Forty, a test where growth plus cash flow margin should add up to about forty percent. Another part pays only if the share price climbs back to at least ninety-two dollars. It closed at about sixty-three and a half dollars last week. Their new cash bonus depends on hitting a target for EBITDA, a common measure of operating profit.

And the bet behind it all is AI. Wix bought Base44, a vibe-coding startup, meaning you describe an app in plain words and AI builds it, last year, and Base44's annual recurring revenue has reached about two hundred fifty million dollars, up from one hundred fifty million in May. Shareholders vote on December twenty-third in Tel Aviv. The lesson: after mass layoffs, boards are tying leaders' pay much more tightly to results, and investors will be watching whether the AI bet pays off.

The lesson for an art agency: Instagram is where collectors find and even buy work, but the youngest buyers want private, trusted spaces, not public showing off.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and good news if you can't always do long workouts. Researchers at Western Norway University of Applied Sciences followed more than ten thousand university students, aged eighteen to thirty-five, for a year. They found that how often people exercised was more consistently linked to anxiety and depression a year later than how hard or how long they worked out. The less often students exercised, the higher their risk, and the benefit kept growing up to about ten hours a week. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. But the practical idea is simple: several short sessions spread through the week may do more for your mood than one heroic workout. The World Health Organization's target is one hundred fifty minutes of moderate activity a week; try splitting it into five thirty-minute walks or classes.

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a quiet weekend for brand-new cyber deals. I couldn't confirm a single new funding round or completed acquisition in the last forty-eight hours, so today we'll go deeper on the story from yesterday that matters most to you here in Israel: CrowdStrike's talks to buy Zscaler.

Quick reminder of where it stands. Bloomberg and Reuters reported on Friday that CrowdStrike, the Texas company best known for protecting laptops and servers, is in talks to buy Zscaler, the company that secures how employees connect to the internet and to company apps. Nothing is signed. Zscaler was worth about thirty-two billion dollars at Friday's close, and its shares jumped about seventeen percent in after-hours trading, the trading after the market closes. One new detail from Reuters: any deal would likely need the backing of Jay Chaudhry, Zscaler's founder and CEO, who is also its largest shareholder. In other words, this doesn't happen unless the founder says yes.

Now the Israeli angle, from Calcalist on Saturday. CrowdStrike has more than five hundred employees in Israel, and it built that team by buying Israeli startups: six since twenty twenty, including Preempt Security, Bionic, Flow Security, Adaptive Shield and Seraphic Security, several of them for hundreds of millions of dollars. Zscaler has about two hundred people here, and its Israeli purchases include Trustdome, Canonic Security and Avalor, which it bought for about three hundred fifty million dollars. Put together, that would be one of the biggest foreign cyber employers in the country.

What's next? Bloomberg's sources say a deal could be announced soon, but analysts urge caution, and no price has been reported. If it happens, expect a long integration. When Palo Alto bought CyberArk and Google bought Wiz, each deal took about a year to close.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons from the debate over CrowdStrike and Zscaler, and one from an Israeli company.

First, the case against. Richard Stiennon, a well-known cyber industry analyst, wrote on Saturday that CrowdStrike "should NOT acquire Zscaler." His argument is about how security is actually bought. In many companies, it isn't a central purchasing department choosing one vendor for everything. Each security team, the endpoint team, the network team, the cloud team, picks its own tools. So bundling two products together doesn't guarantee you'll sell both. He points to big companies that tried to own many categories at once and struggled, and sums it up in a rule: "Never attempt to dominate two categories." For a marketer, that's a reminder to know who your real buyer is. If the person signing is a network team lead, a message about one big platform may not land.

Second, the case for, and it's the platformization story we keep coming back to. Buyers complain about having too many security vendors, and the giants are racing to be the one platform: Palo Alto with CyberArk and now Applied Compute, Google with Wiz. Techzine points out that CrowdStrike and Zscaler already work together, their products have been connected since twenty nineteen, so customers already see them as a pair. That's a classic marketing setup: a partnership tests the story before an acquisition makes it official.

Third, partnership versus ownership. A newsletter analysis by Omar's Upskiller makes a smart point: in a partnership, each company keeps its own pricing and its own sales team, so the customer still gets two quotes, two contracts and two account managers. An acquisition lets one company control the roadmap, the customer relationship and the price. But the same analyst warns that cross-selling, selling a second product to customers you already have, is limited when customers already use both, and that some customers deliberately avoid putting everything with one vendor, especially after CrowdStrike's twenty twenty-four software update crashed about eight and a half million Windows computers. That's the trust problem a combined company's marketing would have to solve: "one platform" sounds simple, but it can also sound like "one point of failure."

Fourth, a very different playbook from an Israeli company. Cato Networks, the Tel Aviv company founded by Shlomo Kramer, which sells SASE, secure access service edge, a cloud service that combines networking and security in one, launched a feature last Tuesday called AgentSense. In plain words, it watches AI agents, like coding assistants on a laptop, and blocks them when an email, a web page or a document tries to trick them into doing something unsafe. That trick is called prompt injection, hiding instructions for an AI inside ordinary content.

The marketing is what's interesting. First, pricing: Cato is including it at no extra charge for customers who already have its AI usage licenses. That's a classic way to grow a platform: give the new feature away to make existing customers stickier, and make competitors look expensive. Second, a challenge: Cato is offering ten thousand dollars each to the first three people who can break through it and redirect a protected agent into doing something malicious. It's a bug bounty, a cash reward for finding flaws, used as a marketing stunt, and it says "we're so confident, try to beat us." Third, it's lowering the barrier to entry: an analyst at Futurum quotes Cato saying there's "no need to buy SASE first" for its AI endpoint product. The same analyst, Fernando Montenegro, liked the technology but said buyers will want data on false alarms, not just on blocked attacks, which is a good reminder that bold claims need proof points.

The takeaway for you: in interviews, if someone asks how you'd market a platform, talk about both sides. The simplicity story for the executive who wants fewer vendors, and the proof story, uptime, resilience, independent testing, for the team that fears putting all its eggs in one basket.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, CrowdStrike and Zscaler in Israel. If a deal is announced, both Israeli teams will go through a long integration, and integrations need people who can explain a combined product, rewrite messaging and run joint launches. Watch for product marketing and partner marketing roles over the coming months.

Second, Cato Networks. An Israeli company launching new AI security features every few weeks, with a pricing and challenge-based go-to-market, needs product marketers who can turn technical launches into stories and campaigns.

Third, Bay, the Israeli startup that monitors AI agents, which raised fifty million dollars in its first nine months, counts Wix and Micron among its customers, and said it plans to grow sales, marketing and customer operations in the US and Europe. An early-stage company with famous angel investors and a hot category is exactly where a growth marketer can build the function from scratch.
