## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Friday, November twentieth, and this is your Morning Brief. Here at home, Gadi Eisenkot is reportedly ready to put a government to a vote even without a majority, and his own allies are pushing back. In China, Trump, Xi and Putin promised to talk about nuclear weapons, and not much else. And Anthropic's first day on the stock market: a big jump, then a slide, and a closing price well above where it started.

## Israel

We start here at home, with a bold plan and a ticking clock. Times of Israel and Haaretz report that Eisenkot plans to bring a government to a Knesset vote about a week before his mandate, his official window to form a government, runs out. And he may do it even if he doesn't have a majority.

First, the clock. President Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate on November eighth, with twenty-eight days, so the deadline is around December sixth. Herzog can add up to fourteen more days, but Haaretz says Eisenkot's party, Yashar, isn't counting on that. If he fails, the mandate could pass to someone else, most likely Netanyahu, and the road could lead to yet another election.

Now the trick. To approve a new government, the Knesset doesn't need sixty-one votes in favor. It just needs more yes votes than no votes. That's why abstaining, choosing not to vote either way, can decide everything. The seat math: Eisenkot's bloc of Zionist parties has forty-eight seats, Netanyahu's bloc fifty-three, and the Arab parties nineteen. According to the reports, Yashar expects the Democrats and Ra'am to vote yes and the Joint List to abstain. Ynet says Ra'am would support the government from outside, without formally joining the coalition. Eisenkot calls it a "national emergency government," and his argument is simple: with the Iran war still running, Netanyahu has to be replaced, at least for a while, even if another election follows.

The problem is inside his own camp. Avigdor Liberman, who has seven seats, says he'd vote against. He wants a government with the two ultra-Orthodox parties instead. Naftali Bennett, with twelve seats, said a minority government "would not be a good option" and wants to try a unity government with Likud first. And Channel 12 says Bennett warned allies that a government backed by Arab parties would be "like the Levin speech on steroids," a reference to the twenty twenty-three speech that launched the judicial overhaul. On the other side, Yair Lapid called keeping Netanyahu in office "madness."

Likud called the reports "shocking," and said the bloc is "unserious about unity." Eisenkot's negotiator, Chili Tropper, answered that Likud "repeatedly refused to schedule meetings." What's next: a second round of unity talks between Yashar and Likud on Sunday. And the bloc plans to nominate retired Supreme Court justice Yosef Elron for state comptroller, the official who audits the government. Why it matters: if Eisenkot goes ahead, Israel could get its first government that depends on Arab votes since the Bennett-Lapid government of twenty twenty-one, and that is exactly the fight Netanyahu wants to have.

Next, a hate crime that shook the north. Early Thursday morning, in Ed-Dahi, an Arab village near Afula, three masked people approached the local mosque. The imam, Sheikh Khaled Zubi, says they broke a window and threw in a Molotov cocktail, a bottle filled with fuel and set on fire. He found the fire when he came for morning prayers. On the walls they sprayed "Revenge," "Tefillin revenge," and the name of a young settler killed in a car incident in the West Bank in March. Police think it may be revenge for the alleged desecration of a synagogue in Afula last month, in which a fifteen-year-old is accused. The Shin Bet has joined the investigation, which points to a nationalist motive. No arrests had been reported. Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas called it "an act of terror and a desecration of a holy site." Ahmad Tibi blamed "the incitement of Netanyahu, ministers and members of Knesset." And Lapid called it "neither Jewish nor Israeli."

And on Iran, a big question about chemical weapons. Earlier this month, the New York Times reported that Israeli intelligence told Washington that Iran may be developing missiles that could carry chemical weapons. This week, Mossad chief Roman Gofman presented the intelligence in Washington. But Axios reports some in the administration think it's "driven by an agenda," and some American officials compare it to the claims about Iraq's weapons before the war in two thousand three. Trump told Fox, "if it does happen, we wipe them out." But he also told Channel 12 he's "not rushing," and might wait until after January first. Channel 13 reports Netanyahu told a security meeting that an operation against Iran is "unavoidable." Iran's foreign ministry called the claims "fraudulent." Also, the army struck a Hezbollah missile site in Lebanon's Beqaa Valley, the farming region near Syria, for the first time in months, Haaretz says.

## World

Now to the world, and the meeting everyone watched. On Thursday in Shenzhen, Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin met together for about seventy minutes, the first time the three leaders have ever done that. So what came out of it?

A joint statement, reported by China's state news agency, said "there can be no winners in a nuclear war and that it must never be unleashed." The three agreed to start a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks, but with no deadline, no format and no promise of a treaty. Remember why this matters: New START, the last treaty limiting American and Russian nuclear weapons, expired in February, and nothing has replaced it. They also pledged to increase the supply of fuel like gasoline and diesel, which matters with the Strait of Hormuz still choked, and to cooperate on trade and AI. Iran wasn't mentioned at all.

Trump was thrilled. "On a scale of zero to ten, I think it was a twelve," he told NBC. On nuclear cuts, he said getting a country with about four thousand warheads to give up half is "a very hard thing," but "I love the concept." Xi went further, saying nuclear weapons "should be comprehensively prohibited." Others were less impressed. Germany's chancellor Friedrich Merz called it a photo op and said the format "will not bring peace." And Republican Senator Thom Tillis, pointing to the twenty-three American troops killed in the Iran war and reports that Russia helped Iran with intelligence, said, "With friends like these, who needs enemies?" Next up: Xi is expected at the G20, the summit of the world's twenty biggest economies, in Miami next month.

The summit didn't calm Ukraine. Ukraine hit the Kerch Bridge, the bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea, for the third time in a week, reportedly with Neptune missiles, and stopped rail traffic across it. Russia struck two major bridges in Kyiv the same day. And Putin, right after the summit, said, "We will not make agreements after strikes on the Crimean Bridge. We will respond." That rules out the limited ceasefires his own security council had discussed.

And the Iran war keeps spreading in the Gulf. Houthi cruise missiles from Yemen hit the eighty-story tower of the Public Investment Fund, Saudi Arabia's giant state investment fund, in Riyadh, blowing out windows across several floors. Nobody was hurt. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed two more tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz, though its claims are rarely confirmed. And the captain of an Emirati tanker hit on Tuesday is still missing.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where a very New York sight is going away. On Thursday, Mayor Mamdani signed Romanch's Law, which ends the city's horse-drawn carriages. It's named for Romanch Mahajan, an eighteen-year-old tourist who was thrown from a carriage this summer and died. The carriages must stop by June first, twenty twenty-eight, and no new licenses will be issued. About two hundred people work in the industry, and the city has to write a plan for them by April, including job training at LaGuardia Community College, buying back their medallions, the licenses drivers own, and studying vintage electric cars to replace the horses in Central Park. Mamdani said, "we have a responsibility to the workers whose livelihoods depend on it." The drivers' union, the Transport Workers Union, isn't buying it. Its president, John Samuelsen, called the law "an unconstitutional abomination," because some owners paid hundreds of thousands of dollars for their medallions with no guaranteed payback, and NY1 reports the union filed a lawsuit Thursday afternoon.

Also in the city: on Wednesday, several dozen protesters outside Penn Station demanded the arrest of Yoav Gallant, Israel's defense minister during most of the Gaza war, who was in town to speak at a pro-Israel institute's event in midtown. The International Criminal Court has a warrant out for him, which Israel rejects, but the US isn't a member of the court, so it can't be enforced here. There were no arrests.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a small college that changed its rules. At Sarah Lawrence, a liberal-arts college in Yonkers, just north of the city, Jewish students tried last year to start a chapter of J Street U, the campus group of J Street, the liberal pro-Israel organization. The student senate rejected it, and some senators compared J Street to "a white supremacist organization." This fall, JTA reports, the dean of students changed the system: any club that meets the published requirements is now approved automatically. "Recognition no longer requires a Student Senate vote." The twist: there's still no J Street chapter, because the student who led the effort graduated in May. Organizer Asher Modell says the college "wouldn't have done it otherwise." It comes as the University of Michigan's Hillel, the Jewish campus organization, cut ties with its own J Street chapter this fall. Even pro-Israel groups are fighting over who counts as pro-Israel on campus.

And in Washington, every member of the Congressional Jewish Caucus signed a letter warning that a provision in the big annual defense bill, meant to protect military chaplains from "censorship," could lead to "unwanted proselytization," meaning pressure on soldiers to convert. Their worry is Jewish service members who may not have a Jewish chaplain nearby.

## Business story

Our business story is about a man who wants to sell ideas by the million. Bill Gross founded Idealab in nineteen ninety-six, an incubator, a company that builds and launches startups, modeled on Thomas Edison's lab. In October he launched Idealab dot AI. He fed an AI system news stories going back to two thousand, every patent since nineteen eighty, product recalls, complaints from Reddit and X, and his own thirty-one methods for finding business ideas. After about a month and roughly a million dollars in computing costs, it produced one point two million business ideas, and he made them free. He says a hundred thirty-one thousand people in two hundred two countries have searched them.

So where's the money? Gross wants big companies to pay two hundred fifty thousand dollars a year to run the system on their own patents and secrets. His test case was Caltech, his old university. Its tech transfer office, the team that turns research into products, gave him about ten thousand patents, and the system came back with three thousand eight hundred sixty-seven ideas Caltech "should either build or license." For scale, Caltech signed fifty-nine licenses in a whole year. His pitch: "What if the input isn't the news? What if the input is you?" Fortune's fair doubt: big companies often keep patents on the shelf on purpose, to block competitors. The lesson: give the product away to build attention, then charge the big customers.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and the wine question. A study of more than three hundred forty thousand adults in Britain, followed for over thirteen years and presented at a major heart conference, found that heavy drinking of any kind raised the risk of dying by twenty-four percent. Moderate wine drinkers had a lower risk of dying from heart disease, while even small amounts of beer, cider or spirits were linked to a higher risk. But it's an observational study, and the researchers are clear: it does "not establish that wine itself protects," and it's no reason to start drinking. Wine drinkers often drink with meals and have healthier habits overall. The takeaway: less is better, and if you do drink, a glass of wine with dinner beats shots on an empty stomach.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a lighter day for cyber deals, and I couldn't confirm a new acquisition in the last forty-eight hours, so let's go through the rounds that matter, starting with an Israeli one.

CyberRidge, an Israeli company founded by Professor Dan Sadot, an expert in optical communications, got ten million euros from the European Innovation Council, the EU's program that funds risky deep-tech companies. What does CyberRidge do? Most of the internet's data travels as light through fiber-optic cables. CyberRidge's product, called Carmel, uses photonics, technology that works with light itself, to spread a signal across hundreds of wavelengths and hide it inside optical noise, with keys that are never stored. The pitch is data that's "impossible to record rather than simply harder to decrypt." Why does that matter? Because of a threat called "harvest now, decrypt later," where attackers steal encrypted data today and wait for quantum computers to crack it in a few years. That puts CyberRidge in post-quantum security, protection designed to survive quantum computers. And it slots into existing network equipment, so telecom companies don't have to rebuild anything. It has raised about twenty-six million dollars in total, including a sixteen million dollar Series A last year. Ten million euros isn't huge, but EU backing is a strong signal for selling to European telecoms and governments.

Second, StepSecurity, a Seattle company, raised a twenty million dollar Series A, led by the investor Elephant. It protects the software supply chain, all the outside code and tools a company uses to build its own software. Think of attacks where hackers slip malicious code into a popular open-source package, free code anyone can use, and it spreads to thousands of companies. StepSecurity blocks that on developer laptops, in AI coding agents, and in CI/CD pipelines, the automated systems that build and ship code. The founders come from Microsoft, Uber and Plaid. The round was oversubscribed, meaning more investors wanted in than there was room for, and the growth is striking: annual recurring revenue grew five times in twenty twenty-four, five times in twenty twenty-five, and more than five times again this year. More than twenty thousand open-source projects use its free tool. Twenty million is a normal, healthy Series A, and here's the part for you: the money goes partly to expanding sales and marketing, and the company says it's hiring in marketing.

Third, the race to watch AI agents keeps getting more crowded. Metano came out with a twelve million dollar seed round led by Sierra Ventures. It finds all the AI agents running inside a company, maps what each one can access and do, and blocks risky actions as they happen. The team comes from ServiceNow, Zscaler, Palo Alto Networks and Microsoft. And Pretxt came out of stealth with five million dollars, led by Deciens Capital. Its CEO, Brian Soby, founded AppOmni, a well-known SaaS security company. Pretxt calls its category "AI Supervision": it checks what agents do against company rules and hands tricky cases to a human. Two new companies in one week, each naming the problem a little differently. That's the marketing battle in AI security right now.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and three moves worth studying.

First, giving AI agents a personality. Wallarm, an application security company, launched what it calls "Agentic Operators," seven AI agents with names: Wally, Radar, Sift, Tune, Trim, Lens and Bard. Its tagline is "Agent-run, team-approved," and the CEO, Shayne Higdon, said, "Security teams don't need another stream of alerts. They need the work behind those alerts to get done." Why does naming agents work? Because it turns an abstract feature into a team the buyer can picture hiring. The risk: if every vendor names its agents, it starts to sound like a gimmick.

Second, the road to re:Invent, Amazon's huge cloud conference in Las Vegas, which starts November thirtieth. Watch how companies time news before it. Teleport, which secures access to infrastructure, just earned an official AWS security badge for identity, and reminded everyone it's sold through AWS Marketplace, Amazon's online store for business software. That matters because big companies can buy through their existing Amazon budget, and Amazon's own sellers can help co-sell, sell alongside the startup. Both Teleport and Wallarm announced their booth numbers in their press releases. That's event marketing: create news a week before, so you have something to talk about at the booth.

Third, partners as a growth engine. iCOUNTER, a threat intelligence company, launched a global partner program for resellers and MSSPs, managed security service providers, the companies that run security for other businesses. It offers deal registration, which protects a partner's deal from competing sellers, plus training, co-marketing and market development funds, money a vendor gives partners for marketing. And notice another pattern from this week's CyberWire briefing: security companies are lining up announcements with AI platforms. Expel, a security monitoring company, says its service now covers Claude, and Checkmarx and ArmorCode each announced work with an OpenAI program. Partnering with a famous AI brand borrows its attention. One more industry note: at the Cybersecurity Marketing Society's awards this week, Kickstand was named best cyber PR agency, chosen by senior cyber marketers.
