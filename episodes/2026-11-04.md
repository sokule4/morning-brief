## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Wednesday, November fourth, and this is your Morning Brief. America voted, and it was a big night for Democrats: they've won the House, and they took Senate seats from Republicans in Texas, North Carolina and South Carolina, with the Senate still too close to call. On the same day, Trump announced that American military aid to Israel will end this year. And here at home, President Herzog starts talking to the parties today about who gets the first shot at a government. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in America, where the results are still coming in as you listen. Here's what we know so far. Decision Desk HQ and NBC News project that Democrats have won control of the House of Representatives, the lower chamber of Congress. Going in, Republicans held two hundred eighteen seats and Democrats two hundred fourteen, so Democrats needed a net gain of just three. The first House flip of the night, a seat moving from one party to the other, came in western North Carolina, where Democrat Jamie Ager won an open seat. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said the country is "on the brink of a dramatic change of direction," and he's promised to bring several bills to the floor within his first hundred hours.

The Senate is where the real drama is. Republicans started with fifty-three seats out of one hundred. Democrats needed a net gain of four for a majority, because in a fifty-fifty tie, the vice president, JD Vance, casts the deciding vote. So far, Democrats have flipped three seats.

The biggest shock is Texas. Democrat James Talarico, a state lawmaker, beat Republican Ken Paxton, the state's attorney general, who had pushed out longtime senator John Cornyn in a primary with Trump's support. The race was called at ten fourteen p.m. New York time, with Talarico up about sixteen points at that stage of the count. Texas hasn't elected a Democrat to the Senate since Lloyd Bentsen in nineteen eighty-eight. In North Carolina, former governor Roy Cooper beat Michael Whatley, the former head of the Republican National Committee, for the seat Thom Tillis is leaving. And in South Carolina, a state Trump won by eighteen points, pediatrician Annie Andrews beat Darline Graham. Graham is the sister of Senator Lindsey Graham, who died suddenly in July; she was appointed to finish his term. South Carolina hasn't elected a Democrat to the Senate since nineteen ninety-eight.

Democrats also held on where they had to. Jon Ossoff won in Georgia, Chris Pappas won the open seat in New Hampshire, and Juliana Stratton won in Illinois. Count it up and the Senate stands at fifty-fifty for now, with Michigan, Maine, Alaska, Ohio and Iowa still counting. One more Democratic flip and they take the majority.

Trump's mood showed early. Before the results, he posted, "For some reason, the sitting president doesn't win the midterms," and added, "But we gave this a very strong shot." As the night went on, his only public comments were angry posts attacking Fox News's coverage, under the headline "They are AGAINST me!" Why it matters: a Democratic House can block Trump's agenda and open investigations, and if the Senate flips too, his last two years look very different. That includes his choices on Iran and on Israel.

Which brings us to the other huge American story, and this one lands right on Israel. On Election Day morning, Trump told Fox News that the United States will stop giving Israel about four billion dollars a year in military aid, starting next year. "We've already agreed that by the end of this year we won't be paying Israel," he said. "Now we'll help Israel if they need it." Asked why, he said, "Because they don't need it." He said Netanyahu brought him the idea, called it a kind of "barter," and said Israel will keep buying American weapons.

Some background. Since twenty sixteen, aid has run under a memorandum of understanding, a formal deal between governments, worth thirty-eight billion dollars over ten years. Most of it is foreign military financing, money Israel must spend on American-made weapons, so a lot of it flows back to American defense companies. Netanyahu has said before that he wants to wean Israel off aid; in January he told The Economist he'd taper it off over ten years. What's new is the speed. Trump openly tied it to the election, calling the aid "almost too easy a target" for critics like Michigan's Democratic Senate candidate, Abdul El-Sayed. The Times of Israel reports Israel's top financial and security officials were caught off guard, though Calcalist says some Finance Ministry officials welcome it. The army has been bracing for this. In January, Ynet reported the IDF was planning a ten-year buildup worth about three hundred fifty billion shekels, and that the Air Force has long relied on American jets and munitions bought with aid dollars, so a fast cutoff could squeeze those plans.

The opposition pounced. Gadi Eisenkot said Israelis learned about a strategic shift from the media, and wrote of Netanyahu, "He can no longer be trusted, and he must be replaced." Yair Golan called it "yet another massive failure." Avigdor Liberman said the price of Trump's endorsement of Netanyahu was three point eight billion dollars a year. What's next: the State Department hasn't explained what this means in practice, and Congress would normally have a say. For you: the defense bill now lands on Israel's own budget, and that eventually means taxes and public spending.

Now our own politics, and today is a big day. At noon, the head of the Central Elections Committee, Justice Noam Sohlberg, hands President Herzog the official results. At five p.m., Herzog's consultations begin, the formal meetings where each party tells him who it recommends for prime minister. Herzog is breaking tradition and starting with the smallest parties: Religious Zionism, Shas, Yisrael Beytenu and Zehut tonight, and tomorrow United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit, the Democrats, Ra'am, the Joint List, Yachad, Eisenkot's party Yashar, and finally Likud at eight thirty p.m.

Ynet's count: Eisenkot is expected to get forty-eight recommendations and Netanyahu forty-six. Three parties haven't said: the Joint List, Ra'am and the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism. Neither side has sixty-one. Herzog is expected to hand someone the mandate between Friday and next Tuesday, and that person gets twenty-eight days, plus up to fourteen more, to build a government.

The fight over legitimacy is getting sharper. Netanyahu and his allies accuse the Arab-majority parties, which won nineteen seats, of fraud. He's calling for a "broad national government" without them, and says they "support terrorism and deny the existence of the Jewish state." Haaretz reported that Likud was weighing not recognizing the results at all. Yair Lapid called it a "grave and dangerous attempt to undermine the election results." On Tuesday, Eisenkot, Bennett, Lapid, Liberman and Golan met in Herzliya and all backed Eisenkot.

Behind that, there's a real clash inside the system. The Shin Bet chief, David Zini, wants to question four senior Arab politicians "under caution," meaning as suspects: Ra'am's Mansour Abbas, Ahmad Tibi, Sami Abu Shehadeh, and one unnamed senior lawmaker. The suspicion is that money from Qatar and Turkey funded campaigns to sway the vote. Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara says there's substantial evidence on the unnamed lawmaker, but she wants to see evidence on the other three first, and she refused to treat the case as a security offense from day one, which would have allowed wiretaps and limits on access to lawyers. Justice Sohlberg said the allegations need investigating, but suspicions alone aren't enough to challenge the results, which he said reflect the voters' will.

Quickly on Iran. After Monday's American strikes on Qeshm Island, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf warned that Iran's response to any American "miscalculation" would be unpredictable, saying "Iran will not submit." But President Masoud Pezeshkian says the supreme leader has given his government new authority over talks with Washington, and Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Monday night with a draft deal. Trump told Axios he's "in no rush" to restart the war, and oil fell more than seven percent on Monday. Israel's public broadcaster, Kan, reports Israel asked Lebanon, through American mediators, to set up army roadblocks in Hezbollah areas in case fighting with Iran resumes.

In the West Bank, tensions keep simmering. On Tuesday, settlers reportedly set fire to vehicles and part of a building in the South Hebron Hills, leaving graffiti about a recent eviction at a site called Givat Or Yehuda. Vehicles were also burned in the Palestinian village of Majdal Bani Fadil, near Nablus. No one was hurt. Separately, the Shin Bet and police arrested six Palestinians from the Jenin area who they say were trying to build rockets, a worry because rockets from the West Bank could reach Israel's center. Indictments are expected.

Two more world stories. In Ukraine, Russian bombs hit an aid distribution center in the Zaporizhzhia region, killing at least twenty-nine people, with forty-eight still missing. President Zelensky called it "utterly horrific." American, Russian and Ukrainian officials are set to meet in Abu Dhabi next week. And in Yemen, the Houthis have pushed within about fifty miles of Aden, the temporary capital of the internationally recognized government, their closest since twenty fifteen.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where it was a comfortable night for Democrats. Governor Kathy Hochul won a second full term, beating Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive. With about half the vote counted, she led by around thirty-five points. Compare that to twenty twenty-two, when she beat Lee Zeldin by only six. Blakeman ran on tax and utility cuts and an immigration crackdown, and attacked Hochul's alliance with Mayor Zohran Mamdani. Hochul tied Blakeman to Trump and ran on universal childcare and subway safety. Mamdani praised her, saying she "has led with a recognition that our highest obligation is not to our factions but to our futures."

Attorney General Letitia James was leading her Republican challenger, Saritha Komatireddy, by about thirty-four points, and Comptroller Tom DiNapoli easily won again.

The House races near the city matter for control of Congress. In the Hudson Valley, Republican Mike Lawler, the incumbent, meaning the person already holding the seat, was slightly behind Democrat Cait Conley, an Army veteran, shortly after ten p.m., with most votes still uncounted. In the deep-red North Country, a Democrat, Blake Gendebien, was leading. And on Long Island, Democrats Tom Suozzi and Laura Gillen were comfortably ahead. In Westchester, voting machine problems meant some paper ballots were bagged to be counted by hand.

Just across the river in New Jersey, the AP projects that Democrat Rebecca Bennett beat Republican Tom Kean Junior, the son of a former governor, in the seventh district, the House seat pundits saw as most likely to flip. Bennett is thirty-nine, a former Navy helicopter pilot and health tech executive who switched parties after Trump won the twenty sixteen primary, and she's never held office before. She outraised Kean eight point two million to five point eight million, in what was on track to be the most expensive House race in the country. One Democratic operative summed up the mood: "It's certainly a Democratic wave."

Remember those five proposals on the back of your ballot? They're charter amendments, changes to the city's constitution, from Mayor Mamdani's efficiency commission. All five look set to pass. With about eighty-eight percent counted, the first four, cutting red tape for bike lanes, outdoor dining, construction projects and hiring city contractors, were at sixty-one to sixty-seven percent. The fifth, setting goals for the city's rainy day fund, its savings for bad times, was at about seventy-two percent. Republicans and the New York Post's editorial board opposed them, and one watchdog said Mamdani's ads came close to telling people how to vote.

And Mamdani threatened to sue the Department of Homeland Security over ICE agents using city-owned parking lots for enforcement operations, in a letter to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, where election night had a lot to say. Three of America's best-known Jewish politicians won easily. Jon Ossoff held Georgia against Representative Mike Collins, whom Ossoff had called a "notorious bigot, antisemite, and extremist" over his ties to fringe figures; Collins accused Ossoff of caving to radicals by voting to halt some arms shipments to Israel. Ossoff is now widely seen as a twenty twenty-eight presidential contender. Josh Shapiro won a second term as governor of Pennsylvania, beating Stacy Garrity. And J.B. Pritzker won a third term in Illinois. In Florida, Debbie Wasserman Schultz won early in a redrawn district. And in a Palm Beach County district with a large Jewish population, Democrat Jared Moskowitz held his seat against Scott Singer, the Trump-endorsed former mayor of Boca Raton. Both men are Jewish and both ran as strong supporters of Israel. Moskowitz argued the race would help "put brakes on what the administration has been doing."

The race Jewish voters watched most nervously, Michigan's Senate seat, was still being counted. Abdul El-Sayed, the Democrat, has alarmed many Jewish Democrats with his positions on Israel, and AIPAC, the big pro-Israel lobby, backed Republican Mike Rogers. Last weekend, Representative Haley Stevens, a pro-Israel Democrat, endorsed Rogers, splitting Michigan's Jewish community. JTA notes that if Democrats gain seats, the number of members of Congress who sharply criticize Israel is likely to grow.

The aid announcement also split American Jewish groups. Jewish Insider rounded up the reactions. AIPAC said it supports the current aid deal, which expires next year, and wants any change folded into negotiations. The Republican Jewish Coalition said the move was expected and that the relationship is "stronger than ever." Democratic Majority for Israel said, "Serious countries don't run foreign policy by phone-in." And the Zionist Organization of America, usually close to Trump, said it's concerned that ad hoc aid may not replace what was agreed. On the other side, CAIR, the Council on American-Islamic Relations, said ending free aid isn't enough and that America should stop arming Israel completely.

And one phrase kept coming up this campaign: "politically homeless." JTA looked at how many Jewish Democrats now describe themselves that way, uneasy about anti-Israel voices on the left, but repelled by the Republican Party. The polls show the feeling is real, but voting habits haven't moved much. In September, Jewish voters favored Democrats seventy-one to twenty-three percent in one survey. An AP-NORC poll in July found only fifteen percent of Jews felt Democrats support them well, and just sixteen percent said that about Republicans. One Michigan cantor said El-Sayed's rhetoric made her feel "like a stranger in my own party," but she still couldn't vote Republican. If that sounds like conversations you've had with friends back home, you're not alone.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now to tech. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, filed an updated version of its S-1, its IPO paperwork, and one line got everyone's attention. The company says it "may imminently meet" its own definition of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." In plain words, it could soon reach RSI, recursive self-improvement, AI helping to build better AI. Anthropic says no current model has crossed that line yet, and that it doesn't expect to let Claude design and build new generations of models without meaningful human supervision. It's considering updates to its Responsible Scaling Policy, its own safety rulebook. TechCrunch notes skeptics' point: an IPO filing is also a marketing document. The same filing, according to reports on it, shows about seventeen billion dollars in quarterly revenue, but an operating loss of five hundred sixty-seven million after a profit the quarter before, as computing costs climb. The listing is expected later this month.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is leaning into the Gulf. It signed four agreements in Abu Dhabi, covering government services, energy and investment, as Abu Dhabi aims to become what it calls the first "fully AI-native government." Sam Altman told a big energy conference there that AI now mostly outperforms top scientists, and that energy is the industry's biggest challenge.

Now Israeli tech, and a tough one. Nice, the Israeli customer-service software giant, is cutting about five hundred jobs, roughly five percent of its ten thousand employees. It's an AI story. Nice sells the software call centers run on, and AI tools now threaten to replace parts of that business. Customers delayed contracts in the first half of the year. Calcalist points out Nice promised in July not to cut its Israeli workforce of about a thousand, though it expects little impact here. Nice is also selling its Actimize financial-crime division, with Brookfield reportedly close to a roughly two billion dollar deal. Its market value is about six and a half billion dollars, down from around twenty billion at the Covid peak.

And a threat to Israeli science. Ynet reports that draft rules for Horizon Europe, the EU's huge research funding program, would require countries outside the EU to respect "European values," including the Genocide Convention. Israel has been part of the program for years; in the twenty fourteen to twenty twenty round alone, Israeli researchers and companies got about one point eight billion euros. The European Parliament is expected to vote by the end of November, but negotiations with EU governments come after that. One Israeli official said, "If this passes, it will be the greatest achievement ever for the BDS movement." Another believes it can be stopped.

## Cyber deals and hires watch

Now your daily cyber watch, and the big theme today is securing AI agents. Let's start with the deals.

The biggest one came from CrowdStrike, the American cybersecurity giant behind the Falcon platform. On Tuesday, it agreed to buy two AI security startups. The first is Fiddler AI, from Palo Alto, founded in twenty eighteen. It does observability for AI agents, meaning it records and explains what an agent did and why. CrowdStrike CEO George Kurtz called it "the flight recorder for every agent." The second is Dreadnode, founded in twenty twenty-three, which attacks AI models and agents before launch to find weaknesses, like prompt injection, where hidden instructions trick an AI into misbehaving. CrowdStrike didn't share prices, but the Wall Street Journal reports about four hundred million dollars for Fiddler and ninety-five million for Dreadnode. For context, Fiddler had raised about one hundred million, so that's roughly four times what investors put in. Dreadnode had raised a fourteen million dollar Series A. Both deals should close by early next year. CrowdStrike also launched an AI "kill switch," which lets companies shut down an AI agent that goes rogue, cut its network access and stop it from restarting. And notice the go-to-market move: it announced integrations with Anthropic's Claude Cowork, Amazon's AI tools and Lovable, the AI app builder, the same day. CrowdStrike's stock has more than doubled this year.

In case you missed it, three rounds from last week that we haven't covered. Infisical, a San Francisco company that does secrets management, meaning it safely stores the passwords and keys software uses to talk to other systems, raised a forty million dollar Series B at a six hundred million dollar valuation, led by the investor Elad Gil. That valuation is strong for a Series B, and it's growing because AI agents now need credentials too. Northstar Security came out of stealth with a seven million dollar seed round led by Wing Venture Capital. It builds a map of a company's security and IT data that AI agents can use. And Crux Labs emerged with just over six million dollars to test AI agents against the judgment of human experts.

Now the Israeli angle, and it's a point of pride. Fortune's new Cyber 60 list, its annual ranking of the most promising venture-backed cyber companies, was published last week. Twenty-nine of the sixty were founded or co-founded by Israelis, almost half. Among the most mature companies: Cato Networks, Cyera, Island, Torq, Upwind and Eon. In the middle stage: Noma Security, Reco, Descope, 7AI, Clutch and Linx, among others. And in the earliest stage, twelve of twenty, including Artemis Security, Air Security, Bold Security and Corma Labs. Remember some of those names; we'll come back to them.

One of them, Bold Security, is worth a closer look. It was founded in twenty twenty-four by the team behind Polyrize, a data security startup Varonis bought. Bold protects data on endpoints, meaning employees' laptops and devices, using small AI models that run on the device and understand what a file actually is before blocking a risky action. It's raised forty million dollars from Bessemer, Picture Capital and Red Dot, and its customers include HiBob and Shutterfly. The CEO is Nati Hazut.

Then a reality check from the giant of Israeli cyber. Check Point, the Tel Aviv company that made its name on the firewall, the digital barrier between a company's network and the internet, and is worth about fifteen and a half billion dollars, reported third-quarter revenue of six hundred seventy million dollars, down about one percent from last year, and slightly below forecasts. The stock fell around nine to ten percent in early trading on Monday, and the bank TD Cowen downgraded it, cutting its rating to the equivalent of neutral. The interesting part is inside the numbers. Subscriptions grew eleven percent to three hundred thirty-nine million, driven by email security, AI security and CTEM, continuous threat exposure management, which keeps checking where a company is vulnerable. But hardware and license sales fell from about one hundred thirty million to one hundred five million. CEO Nadav Zafrir says revenue hit bottom this quarter, and admitted, "The recovery is taking longer than anticipated." We'll come back to Check Point's sales problems in a minute.

Now people moves. The hire most relevant to you is at Artemis Security, one of those Israeli-founded early-stage names on Fortune's list. Artemis builds an AI-powered security operations platform that detects and responds to attacks on its own, and it says it raised seventy million dollars in seed and Series A money in six months, from Felicis, First Round and Two Sigma. Its CEO is co-founder Shachar Hirshberg, and the team is over fifty-five people. It just named Arun Singh as its chief growth officer, an executive who usually owns marketing and other growth levers together. Singh was CMO at Drata, where he helped define a new category called trust management, and before that ran marketing at Abnormal AI, the email security company, while its annual recurring revenue grew more than twelve times in three years. He's also worked at Salesforce, Okta and Cloudflare. A startup that hires a growth chief with that background is usually about to spend heavily on marketing.

Noma Security, the Israeli-founded AI security company that raised one hundred million dollars last year, made two senior hires. Bobby Shoker is the new CFO, chief financial officer, the executive in charge of money, planning and investors. He was CFO at Veza, Snorkel AI and Fungible, and has helped companies through an IPO and three acquisitions. That kind of hire often signals a company preparing for a bigger round, or an exit. And Alberto Rodrigues joins as chief customer officer, running everything after the sale: customer success, technical support and account management. He held the same job at Oligo Security and at Armis, where Noma says he kept net revenue retention, how much existing customers grow their spending, above one hundred twenty percent. Both report to Noma's co-founder and CEO, Niv Braun.

In Britain, NCC Group, a big cybersecurity consultancy, promoted its CFO, Guy Ellis, to CEO, as Mike Maddison steps down after four and a half years. NCC is now a pure cyber company after selling off a side business, with cyber revenue of about two hundred thirty-five million pounds, up almost four percent. Ellis says AI makes "trusted independent cyber expertise" more needed than ever.

Now marketing and growth trends, and three lessons from this week. First, back to Check Point. It's been restructuring its sales organization, and an analyst at KeyBanc surveyed its channel partners, the resellers and integrators who sell Check Point's products to customers. Only thirty-nine percent said their results met expectations, down from fifty percent last quarter. When a company sells mostly through partners, partner marketing, the training, incentives and campaigns you run for resellers, isn't a side job. It's the engine.

Second, look at how CrowdStrike packaged its news. It didn't just announce two acquisitions. It wrapped them into a story about controlling AI agents, launched a product with a memorable name, the kill switch, and lined up big partner logos on the same day. One announcement, many headlines.

Third, the Fortune list itself. You'll see a wave of "named to the Fortune Cyber 60" press releases this week, like Bold's. Lists like this are cheap credibility for young companies, and sales teams put them straight into decks and emails. And the category everyone wants to own right now is clear: security for AI agents. Noma, Reco, Air, Artemis and CrowdStrike are all fighting to be the name buyers think of first. The threat story behind that pitch keeps getting louder, too: Britain's cyber chief, Richard Horne, warned this week that AI-powered attacks on critical infrastructure are likely by twenty twenty-eight, exactly the kind of quote vendors drop into their campaigns.

And one data point on AI in go-to-market. ElevenLabs, the AI voice company, studied its customers' deployed voice agents and found that in conversations longer than two minutes, voice agents succeeded about thirty-two percent more often than text chatbots, and wrapped up faster, around four and a half minutes versus seven. If you're building sales or support flows, that's an argument for testing voice, not just chat. Starbucks is thinking the same way: it's exploring AI ordering at the drive-thru, possibly recognizing returning customers and personalizing the menu, with tests planned for next year.

Finally, worth a look for you. First, Artemis Security. Israeli-founded, seventy million raised in six months, and a brand-new chief growth officer who will almost certainly build a marketing team fast. That's exactly the moment to reach out. Second, Bold Security. Forty million raised, early stage, a fresh Fortune listing and a product that's easy to explain, which means it needs content and campaigns now. Third, Noma. With a new CFO and customer chief, it's clearly scaling, and scaling companies hire product marketers and demand generation people, the marketers who fill the sales pipeline, right behind the finance and customer leaders.

## Business story

Time for our business story, and it's about the other winner of election night: the betting apps. Two years ago, prediction markets, sites where people trade on the outcome of real events, were still a fringe thing in America. Kalshi, founded by two MIT graduates, had to beat its own regulator, the CFTC, the agency that oversees futures trading, in court in twenty twenty-four just to offer bets on elections. Polymarket was operating mostly outside the United States. Then they called the twenty twenty-four election for Trump while many polls showed a toss-up, and they went mainstream.

These midterms were their first big test since. The numbers are huge. Kalshi says it handled about one point four billion dollars in midterm trading. One research group counted more than twenty-eight thousand midterm markets. Nearly one hundred million dollars was bet on the Texas Senate race alone, and more than one hundred million on the Los Angeles mayor's race. Forbes noted that before the Iran war began in February, Kalshi gave Democrats less than a forty percent chance of sweeping Congress. By about nine p.m. New York time last night, both Kalshi and Polymarket had it at ninety-eight percent.

But there's a catch. Last month, traders gave Brazil's President Lula only about a twenty percent chance, and he won. In August, Kalshi traders gave a Wisconsin candidate a ninety-five percent chance in a primary, and she lost by under half a point. Kalshi's founders replied that "five percent is not zero percent." Researchers also found that some candidates bet on their own races, and that political markets can be moved with fairly small amounts of money. Kalshi now bans politicians and campaign workers from political bets. A Vanderbilt law professor, Yesha Yadav, called this election "a real test for the new wave of prediction markets."

Here's the marketer's takeaway. These companies sell certainty, a single number that feels smarter than a poll. That's a brilliant product story, until the number is wrong in public. The ones that last will be the ones that teach users what a probability really means, not just the ones with the loudest odds.

## Art and design

Now art, and the most expensive November in years is about to hit New York. On November eighteenth, Sotheby's will sell twelve works from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, built by an Argentine couple whose fortune came from sugar, and expected to bring around four hundred fifty million dollars. The star is Van Gogh's "Chestnut Trees in Blossom," painted in May eighteen ninety, about two months before he died. It shows three women with Japanese-style parasols under flowering trees, and until a preview tour in Asia last month, it hadn't been shown in public since a nineteen sixty-four exhibition at the Guggenheim. The estimate is about one hundred eighty million dollars. Van Gogh's auction record is one hundred seventeen million. Sotheby's has guaranteed it, promising the sellers a minimum price, so it's expected to sell. You can see it for free in New York from November seventh to seventeenth, alongside a Cézanne "Harlequin" estimated at one hundred twenty million. The night before, Christie's has its own heavy hitters: a Klimt landscape estimated at sixty to eighty million, and a seven-work group called "Heroes and Lovers," worth close to one hundred million dollars, with four Picassos. The seller is anonymous, but the New York Times reported two experts believe it's the artist Jeff Koons.

And it's a huge week for galleries too. Jasper Johns shows twelve recent drawings at Matthew Marks starting today. Tomorrow, Yoshitomo Nara, the Japanese artist famous for his moody, big-eyed children, opens his first major New York solo show in nearly a decade at David Zwirner, including thirteen bronze busts inspired by ancient Olmec heads. ## Wellness

And your wellness idea for today: pick up the pace. A study published in September in the British Journal of Sports Medicine followed almost sixty-five thousand adults in Britain who wore activity trackers. People taking fewer than five thousand steps a day, but who walked briskly during their most active half hour, had about a twenty-five to thirty-three percent lower risk of dying during the study than slow walkers with the same step count. Brisk means around one hundred steps a minute, fast enough that talking takes a little effort. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. And fun fact: the famous ten thousand steps target reportedly came from a nineteen sixties marketing campaign for a Japanese pedometer. So today, take one walk at a pace that feels slightly rushed.
