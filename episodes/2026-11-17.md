## Israel

We start here at home, where today is the first real test of who controls the new Knesset. This afternoon, lawmakers vote on replacing Likud's Amir Ohana as Knesset speaker, the person who runs parliament and decides what comes up for a vote. Eisenkot's bloc, which forced the vote with sixty-seven signatures, is backing Matan Kahana of Yashar, Eisenkot's party.

There was a last-minute twist on Monday. Bezalel Smotrich, the far-right finance minister, offered to vote for Kahana, but only on two conditions: that Kahana resign if a future government wants someone else, and that no Arab lawmakers serve as deputy speakers, the members who run sessions when the speaker is away. Kahana called the demand "anti-statesmanlike" and said no. Smotrich answered that Kahana "chose Arabs over Jews." The context: Arab parties won a record nineteen seats, and a Knesset committee had just recommended nine temporary deputy speakers, including Ra'am's Walid Taha and the Joint List's Ahmad Tibi, over objections from Likud and Otzma Yehudit.

Now the story that's driving everything: the arrests of three Ra'am activists on election day. Quick recap: Channel 12 reported that Netanyahu's chief of staff, Ido Norden, passed a complaint to the police the day before the vote, pointing them to a source who used to work in the Prime Minister's Office. On Monday, Hebrew media named that source as Daniel Greenberg, a former adviser in that office. The three men, from Bedouin communities in the south, were reportedly arrested without a warrant, and later released.

Then the story moved fast. Police Commissioner Daniel Levy ordered a review of how his officers handled the arrests. According to Channel 12, he told closed-door meetings, "it is possible that the Prime Minister's Office fooled us." Then the attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara, and the state attorney, Amit Aisman, the country's chief prosecutor, asked the police to pause their internal review until it's coordinated with them, and are discussing whether to open a criminal investigation. Channel 12 also reported that Netanyahu himself met Greenberg in October, with Norden present.

Each side tells it differently. Greenberg says there was no plot. In an interview Monday night, he said a Joint List lawmaker told him about "many millions of shekels" being funneled to Ra'am and gave him names. He refused to name that lawmaker, and said his meeting with Netanyahu wasn't planned. Likud says Norden acted "in accordance with his legal duty," and accuses Eisenkot's camp of using the affair to justify relying on Arab parties. On the other side, Joint List chair Yousef Jabareen asked the attorney general for a criminal probe of Netanyahu's office, and Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas called it "clear evidence of an assault on democracy," saying it may have cost his party thousands of votes.

The political fallout: a senior official in Eisenkot's bloc told Kan that the chance of a unity government with Likud is now "down to zero," calling what happened "no less than an attempted coup." Netanyahu, meanwhile, keeps calling for a national unity government and says the ultra-Orthodox parties will be part of any coalition he leads. And Avigdor Liberman, now chair of the Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, says he'll summon the heads of the army, Mossad, Shin Bet and police over what he called "far too many discrepancies."

Why it matters: if the speaker vote passes today, Eisenkot's bloc controls the Knesset agenda even before it has a government. That's when the next stage begins, real coalition talks, which Mansour Abbas says start right after the speaker is chosen.

Two quick items. Liran Avisar Ben-Horin of the B'Yachad party became the first woman to chair the Finance Committee, the powerful committee that oversees the state budget. And on the economy: Finance Ministry officials say they weren't told in advance about Trump's announcement that US military aid would end. Israel gets three point eight billion dollars a year under the current agreement, and the Defense Ministry says it needs another twenty-five billion shekels.

On the security front, the army confirmed a strike on a weapons shipment headed for Hezbollah in Lebanon's Beqaa Valley, the eastern farming region near the Syrian border, reportedly the first strike there in months. And in Gaza, the army says it killed three Hamas operatives in a strike at Nasser Hospital, including a man it says took part in the October seventh kidnapping of Adina Moshe. Hamas says six people were killed.

## World

Now to the world, and the Iran war. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, arrived in Muscat, the capital of Oman, for talks. Oman has been the go-between with Washington throughout the war. Iranian officials say they've nearly finished a deal with Oman on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz: an agreed route, a temporary corridor and joint mine-clearing. Iran's parliament speaker said, "We have reached a mutual understanding with Oman regarding the shipping corridor." It still needs approval in both countries.

The same day, though, the strait got more dangerous. Iran's Revolutionary Guard says six tankers hit mines over Sunday and Monday, and Iran's navy reported a fourth ship in twenty-four hours late Monday. None of that is independently confirmed. What is confirmed: UKMTO, the British military's shipping hotline, said an outbound tanker was hit by an "unknown projectile," causing an engine-room fire, with all crew safe. And a third American aircraft carrier group, the USS Theodore Roosevelt, has reached the region, joining two others, as part of more than twenty Navy ships enforcing the blockade of Iran's ports.

Meanwhile, Trump left Washington for Asia, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. First stop is the ASEAN summit, the gathering of Southeast Asian nations, in Manila, where he's meeting Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos and says he'll give "a major speech" on the economy. Then it's Shenzhen in China for APEC, the Asia-Pacific economic summit, where Beijing confirmed the first three-way meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin on Thursday. Trump also said he might meet North Korea's Kim Jong Un while in China, though that's far from set.

In Europe, Ukraine launched its largest overnight drone attack on Russia since the war began. Russia says it shot down drones over Moscow, Crimea and the Black Sea, and the Kremlin admitted "minor damage" after a bridge over the Kerch Strait, the bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea, was hit. And Estonia says it will close all its border crossings with Russia from December first, after accusing Russian military intelligence of ordering an arson attack on an Estonian drone maker.

And one health story with a long tail. The Pan American Health Organization, the World Health Organization's office for the Americas, says the US has lost its measles elimination status, a label meaning the disease no longer spreads continuously inside the country. The US held it for twenty-six years. Al Jazeera reports that measles-associated deaths in the US have reached ten. To get the status back, the country needs at least twelve months without ongoing spread.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where Mayor Mamdani kept a big campaign promise. On Monday, he and Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch announced they're disbanding the Strategic Response Group, the NYPD unit that handles protests, which critics accused of being too aggressive. In its place there will be three things: a civilian team of de-escalation workers, with no weapons and no power to arrest, who'll talk to protesters; a thirty-officer police team focused on dialogue; and a smaller unit of specially trained officers who can make arrests in rare cases. The unit's counterterrorism work moves to the NYPD's intelligence bureau.

There are new rules too. No tear gas or long guns at protests, no kettling, which means surrounding protesters to trap them, and pepper spray is no longer a general crowd-control tool. The city says the NYPD's overall headcount won't change. Tisch said the changes will make the department "stronger" and "more effective." The police union disagreed sharply: its president, Patrick Hendry, said the mayor is "on their side, not yours." Why it matters: Tisch staying on board gives Mamdani cover, but the first big protest will be the real test.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, starting in Manhattan. Hunter College's student government canceled a Cultural Arts Night that was supposed to include five campus groups, among them Hillel, the Jewish student organization, and Students for Justice in Palestine. It first said the event "should never have moved forward," then cited "safety concerns." Hillel says it had nothing to do with the decision. City Council member Eric Dinowitz, who chairs the Council's Jewish Caucus, called for a civil rights investigation by CUNY, the city university system, and UJA-Federation backed him.

And in Jerusalem, a story about who gets to pray how. Kol HaNeshama, a Reform synagogue, from the liberal stream of Judaism, in the Baka neighborhood, about two hundred eighty families, many of them English-speaking immigrants, has faced five incidents over several weeks this fall: break-ins, vandalism, eggs thrown at worshippers on Rosh Hashanah and a window broken before Yom Kippur. Police arrested three minors, but the congregation says it's heard nothing since. The response has been moving: Orthodox rabbis signed letters of support, and a solidarity gathering drew about two hundred people from across the religious spectrum. But its rabbi, Oded Mazor, blames inflammatory rhetoric, including from Itamar Ben-Gvir, and notes that Jerusalem's mayor supported them privately but stayed away in public.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and tonight is Nvidia's big test. After the US market closes, Nvidia, the company whose chips power most of the world's AI, reports its quarterly results, the first since Jensen Huang resigned. The consensus estimate, the average analyst forecast, is about one hundred nine billion dollars in revenue, nearly double a year ago. Analysts will watch the outlook for early next year and the gross margin, the share of each sale left after production costs, which Nvidia already said would dip because components cost more.

The pressure isn't just from Wall Street. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent questioned whether Nvidia's earnings were inflated by illegal sales to China, accused it of a "culture of noncompliance," and urged it to hire an outside CEO. And from the other direction, a "Bring Jensen Back" campaign asking the board to reinstate Huang has passed one hundred seventy-five thousand signatures, reportedly with backing from Elon Musk and Epic Games' Tim Sweeney. Options traders are betting on a stock move of more than ten percent either way.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's rival AMD completed its eight point two billion dollar all-stock deal for World Labs, the startup founded by Fei-Fei Li, the Stanford researcher often called the "godmother of AI." World Labs builds spatial intelligence models, AI that understands three-dimensional space, which matters for robots. Li joins AMD as chief scientist.

And one tech story from here that raises hard questions. The Guardian, citing leaked documents, reported that the Israeli army uploaded about three and a half million images of Palestinians to Amazon's cloud, starting in early twenty twenty-four, to run them through Amazon's facial recognition software. A military source said the software was used early in the war to try to locate hostages and identify suspected operatives. Amazon called its meetings with soldiers "routine customer engagement," and the army declined to comment. These are the Guardian's claims, not independently confirmed.

## Business story

Our business story is the end of a long struggle over an Israeli airline. Arkia, the Israeli airline, has a new owner. On Monday, the Nakash brothers, the New York family behind Jordache jeans who've controlled Arkia for about twenty years, agreed to sell to Ronen Elad, an Israeli investor.

Who is he? Not an aviation person. Elad owns a group of industrial companies: electrical cables, defense drones, wet wipes, plastic food packaging and metal processing, plus a stake in Shilav, the baby products chain. His group puts in thirty million dollars for about eighty-two percent of Arkia, ten million at signing and the rest after regulators approve. The Nakash family adds eight and a half million, and the government contributes seven and a half million in compensation for losses during the Iran war.

The most interesting player here is the workers. Arkia's employees own a stake through their own company, and earlier this year they effectively blocked a sale. This time they backed Elad, and that tipped it: he beat rival bidders, including Ami Cohen, who owns Blue Bird Airways. He promised to keep the workers' veto rights and protect their fifteen percent stake. Their chairman, Avi Edri, called it "a historic and unique day for the Israeli economy," and the Histadrut, Israel's biggest labor federation, welcomed it. The lesson: in a unionized company, the people who do the work can decide who owns it. What's next: approval from the Competition Authority, the regulator that guards against monopolies, and from the aviation regulator.

## Pop culture

Now pop culture, and the biggest casting news in years. Robert Pattinson is the new James Bond. Amazon MGM Studios, which now controls the franchise, announced it Monday. Pattinson is the seventh actor to play 007 on film, after Sean Connery, George Lazenby, Roger Moore, Timothy Dalton, Pierce Brosnan and Daniel Craig. He called it "the honour of a lifetime." Denis Villeneuve, who directed the "Dune" movies, is directing, and Steven Knight, the creator of "Peaky Blinders," is writing. Production is expected next year for a twenty twenty-eight release. Remember, Craig's last film, "No Time to Die," ended with Bond's death, so this is a true restart. For months the rumored favorites were Callum Turner, Josh O'Connor, Jack Lowden and Nicholas Hoult. Villeneuve said Pattinson "has the timeless soul of 007."

## Travel

Now travel, and a record that's literally in the air. On Sunday, El Al flight ten, a Boeing seven-eight-seven Dreamliner, flew from New York's JFK to Ben Gurion in eight hours and fifty-seven minutes. El Al says that's the fastest it has ever flown the route, forty-four minutes faster than the average, and it landed fifty-three minutes early.

The secret is the jet stream, the fast river of wind high in the atmosphere that blows west to east and gets stronger in winter. A plane riding it covers more ground without flying any faster through the air around it, which saves time and fuel. It's also why flying home to Israel from New York is usually quicker than the trip there, which often fights headwinds. So if you're flying to New York this winter, plan on the longer leg going out.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with news for your morning coffee. Researchers at the University of Copenhagen followed sixty-one healthy adults who swallowed a small electronic capsule that measured what happens inside the gut. The regular coffee drinkers, one to five cups a day, had more diverse gut bacteria, and more contractions in the colon, the movements that keep digestion going and can help prevent constipation. The caveats: it's a small study, it shows a link rather than proof that coffee caused it, and the team says it doesn't yet know whether coffee's other effect, lower acidity in the small intestine, is good or bad. Their next study will control how much coffee people drink. Still, if your morning cup helps keep things moving, there may be a reason.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

After a quiet weekend, the money is back, and the lead story is Israeli. Corma, an Israeli AI security startup, raised a one hundred twenty-five million dollar Series A at a valuation of one billion dollars. That makes it a unicorn, a startup worth a billion or more, about three months after it came out of stealth in August with a sixty million dollar seed round. The new round was led by NEA, one of Silicon Valley's biggest venture firms, with existing investors Sequoia, Khosla Ventures and Coatue. Total raised: one hundred eighty-five million dollars.

What does it do? Most security startups build a product that plugs into a company's defenses. Corma builds its own AI, a foundation model, the big base model other tools are built on, trained from scratch for defense, rather than using models from Anthropic or OpenAI. On top of it, it runs AI agents that work alongside a company's security team, using the tools the company already has. Its own description is "a frontier AI research lab focused on protection," and it says its goal is "superintelligence" for cyber defense.

Who's behind it? Co-founder and CEO Alon Pluda is a graduate of Psagot, an elite IDF intelligence technology program, and served as a technical officer in Unit 8200. Globes reports he started Corma in twenty twenty-five after leaving Miggo Security, which he co-founded, following a dispute with his co-founders. The team is tiny: about twenty to twenty-five people in Tel Aviv and San Francisco, including researchers from Google and DeepMind. Globes estimates annual recurring revenue in the tens of millions of dollars, with customers among Fortune 100 companies in healthcare, finance, energy and telecom. It says demand jumped so fast that it expanded its server capacity twenty-four times over.

Is that big? Very. A billion-dollar valuation at Series A, with a team of about two dozen, is rare even in Israeli cyber. TechCrunch says interest surged after a wave of serious security incidents on October twenty-first. The competition is fierce: Kevin Mandia's new startup Armadin, plus Anthropic, OpenAI and Microsoft, are all chasing AI defense.

Second, a very different Israeli money story. Cyabra, the Israeli company that detects fake accounts and coordinated disinformation campaigns online, signed a deal with unnamed institutional investors for up to forty million dollars over three years, plus canceling about three million dollars of debt. Cyabra went public on Nasdaq this spring through a SPAC, a shell company that merges with a startup to take it public, and Globes reported its share price halved soon after its debut. This deal is structured as a private placement, shares sold directly to investors rather than on the open market, and the investors' shares convert into regular stock at fifteen cents a share. The release says the money should also help Cyabra keep its Nasdaq listing. So this isn't a celebration round; it's a lifeline that buys time, and it still needs shareholder approval.

And one completed deal: Cohere, the Canadian AI company that builds models for businesses, closed its purchase of AISLE, the startup whose AI finds and fixes software security flaws. We told you about its letter of intent, the preliminary agreement, signed in October. What's new: the business now operates as Cohere Cyber, and Cohere says Prague will be its global center for AI security. About fifty people join from AISLE.

And an update on the biggest pending deal for Israeli cyber. Remember Proofpoint, the email security giant owned by the private equity firm Thoma Bravo, agreeing in October to buy Varonis, the Israeli-founded data security company, for six point three billion dollars in cash? On Monday, Proofpoint launched a debt sale of nearly two billion dollars, meaning it's borrowing from investors, to help pay for the deal and push back the due date on an existing loan. Earlier reports had it lining up about one point two five billion. Why it matters: money is being put in place, which suggests the deal is moving forward. About a thousand Varonis employees work in Israel, mostly in Herzliya, so how Proofpoint merges the two marketing and sales teams will matter a lot here.

## People moves

On people moves, I couldn't confirm any new marketing chiefs at cyber companies in the last two days, but there are two leadership stories worth knowing.

At Cohere Cyber, the leadership is now set. Ondrej Vlcek, AISLE's co-founder and the former CEO of Avast, the antivirus company, becomes senior vice president of Cohere Cyber. Stanislav Fort, who led AISLE's research, becomes vice president of AI security. When an AI lab puts a former antivirus CEO in charge of its security business, it's telling the market it wants to sell security, not just research it.

And Cyabra says that within ten days of its deal closing, it plans to add experienced public-company executives to its management team and board. That's a sign investors want stronger leadership around the founders.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and this week is a big one for your field. CyberMarketingCon, the conference of the Cybersecurity Marketing Society, is running right now through Wednesday in Austin, Texas. It has more than seven hundred attendees and eighty speakers. Look at its tracks and you see how cyber marketing teams are organized today: demand generation, which means creating sales leads; product marketing; field and channel marketing, which covers events and partners; brand, PR and analyst relations, which means managing what research firms like Gartner say about you; and GTM engineering, the people who build the tools and automations behind go-to-market. There's also a closed-door day just for CMOs. If you want to know what hiring managers care about this quarter, watch the session recordings and LinkedIn posts coming out of Austin.

Three lessons from today's news.

First, Corma is a lesson in category creation, inventing a new category so you're not compared with competitors. It doesn't call itself a security vendor. It says it's a "frontier AI research lab," and its CEO says, "We don't offer a standard security product like other companies." That framing puts Corma next to OpenAI and Anthropic in a buyer's mind, not next to fifty security startups, and it helps justify a billion-dollar price with only about two dozen people. The risk: a big promise like "superintelligence" has to be backed by proof points, hard evidence, fast.

Second, Cyabra is a lesson in repositioning, changing how the market sees you. It used to talk about fighting fake news and bots on social media. In this announcement, it calls itself a "sovereign AI information-warfare platform" for intelligence agencies and Fortune 500 companies. Information warfare means fighting with propaganda and fake accounts, and sovereign AI means AI a country controls itself. Same core technology, new language aimed at government and defense buyers, where budgets are bigger and growing. When a company changes its words like this, watch whether its customer announcements follow. Cyabra's recent ones, a national investigative agency in Southeast Asia and a major media company, suggest they are.

Third, Cohere is a lesson in brand architecture, how a company names and organizes its brands. Instead of keeping AISLE as a separate name, it folded it into "Cohere Cyber." One parent brand, a clear product line. For a marketer, that means one story to tell, but you lose a startup brand that researchers already knew.

And a reminder: Palo Alto Networks reports results tomorrow after the US market closes. Listen for how it talks about AI security demand.
