## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, November twenty-first, and this is your Morning Brief. Here at home, Gadi Eisenkot laid out three possible roads to a government, while reports say Washington and Jerusalem are weighing a new round of strikes on Iran. In The Hague, the world's top court ruled for the first time ever that a country committed genocide. ## Israel

We start here at home, where Eisenkot spent Friday trying to show he has a plan. In a long post on X, he laid out three paths to forming a government, and he said he's in a hurry because of the war with Iran.

Path one: a government with support from the ultra-Orthodox parties, the religious parties that represent Haredi voters. Eisenkot wrote that "serious discussions are underway" with them. Path two: a unity government with Likud, Netanyahu's party. He said there's a "wide gap" between what Likud says and what it does, but he'll "give this track another chance." His negotiators meet Likud's team again on Sunday. Path three, only if the first two fail: what he calls a "national emergency government." That's the minority government we talked about yesterday, which would survive because the Arab-led parties either vote for it or abstain.

Why lay it out like this? Because his own camp is split. Avigdor Liberman, whose party has seven seats, told JTA a government backed by the Haredi parties is "the only practical option," and that he would "categorically" vote against one that relies on Arab parties. Eisenkot also warned that anti-Arab rhetoric could lead to violence against Arab citizens, who are about twenty-one percent of the population. A quick reminder of the clock: President Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate with sixty-seven Knesset members backing him, including all nineteen from the Arab-led parties. His deadline is December sixth, and Herzog can add up to fourteen days. Why it matters: every path has a price. The Haredi path means a deal on the army draft, the unity path means sharing power with the man he wants to replace, and the emergency path means a fight over Arab votes that Netanyahu is eager to have.

And that draft question exploded on the street this week. Late Thursday night in Beit Shemesh, the city west of Jerusalem with a large ultra-Orthodox population, police stopped a sixteen-year-old for driving without a license. A rumor spread that he was a yeshiva student being arrested for dodging the army. Hundreds of people surrounded the police, blocked the arrest, threw stones and smashed a patrol car window, and one man was filmed kicking an officer. Police used stun grenades, which make a loud bang and flash to scatter a crowd. The police said the teenager used "a violent and agitated mob" to try to escape. Here's the background. For decades, Haredi men studying in yeshivas were exempt from military service. In twenty twenty-four, the Supreme Court ruled there was no legal basis for that blanket exemption, and the army began sending draft orders. Many Haredi leaders tell young men not to report, and arrests of draft evaders, people who ignore their call-up, now spark protests like this one. Eisenkot said police "must act decisively and immediately to stop the rioters." That's notable, because he's courting those same Haredi parties for path one.

Now the biggest security story. CBS News reported Friday that the US and Israel are weighing a new offensive against Iran in the near term. According to its sources, Israel is worried that Iran is rebuilding its missile factories quickly and moving them underground, and American forces in the region, tens of thousands of troops and two aircraft carriers, could act within two to four days if ordered. The Times of Israel reports Netanyahu is pushing for a four- to seven-day air campaign. But President Trump says he's not rushing, and told Axios he won't decide on next steps until after the start of the new year. The army's spokesman, Effie Defrin, said there's no change to instructions for the public. And Liberman, who now chairs the Knesset's foreign affairs and defense committee, said there's no security reason to strike right now, and that if Netanyahu decides to, it would be politically motivated. That's the fear in Eisenkot's camp: that a new round of war could freeze coalition talks just as his clock is running.

## World

Now to the world, and a ruling that made legal history. On Thursday, the International Court of Justice, the UN's top court in The Hague, which settles disputes between countries, found that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. Genocide means acts meant to destroy a national, ethnic or religious group. It's the first time since the court was founded in nineteen forty-five that it has found a country guilty of genocide. The vote was nine judges to six, and there's no appeal.

Here's the background. The Rohingya are a mostly Muslim minority in Myanmar, the Southeast Asian country once called Burma, and many of them have been denied citizenship for decades. In twenty seventeen, after Rohingya militants attacked police posts, Myanmar's army launched what it called "clearance operations." Around ten thousand people were killed early on, and more than seven hundred thousand fled to neighboring Bangladesh, where many still live in huge camps. In twenty nineteen, The Gambia, a small West African country backed by the Organization of Islamic Cooperation, brought the case. Myanmar always denied genocide, saying it was fighting armed groups. Aung San Suu Kyi, the Nobel Peace Prize winner who led the government then, defended the army in court herself.

The court ordered Myanmar to punish those responsible, let the Rohingya return safely, give back property and pay compensation, with a plan due within two years. The problem: the court can't enforce its rulings. Myanmar is now led by Min Aung Hlaing, the general who led the twenty twenty-one coup, when the army seized power. The Gambia's lawyer, Arsalan Suleman, called it "a landmark victory for the Rohingya people." And why it matters here: experts say the ruling could shape South Africa's genocide case against Israel at the same court, especially on how judges prove intent. That case is years from a verdict. Israel strongly rejects the accusation.

He said he has "a very good relationship" with Putin, and that Putin is "having a hard time right now." On the midterms, which Republicans lost badly, he said it was because "I wasn't on the ballot." And on Iran, he said Iran wants a deal but its terms are unacceptable.

Moscow called it retaliation for Ukraine's attacks on the Kerch Bridge to Crimea, and missiles hit bridges over the Dnipro River in Kyiv. Poland, a member of NATO, the Western military alliance, said it's investigating whether Russian missiles and a drone crossed into its airspace. ## New York

Next, back home in New York, where Mayor Mamdani admitted a deadline is impossible. On Friday, he released the city's first full plan to close Rikers Island, the jail complex on an island in the East River that's become a national symbol of violence and neglect. In twenty nineteen, the City Council voted to close it by twenty twenty-seven and replace it with four smaller jails, one in each borough except Staten Island. That's not going to happen in time. Mamdani's new target is twenty thirty-two. The Brooklyn jail would open in twenty twenty-nine, the Bronx and Queens in twenty thirty-one and Manhattan in twenty thirty-two. To fit into fewer beds, the city wants to cut the jail population from about sixty-seven hundred to a little over four thousand, with more diversion programs, faster court cases and help with housing. The new dates need the City Council's approval, and Speaker Julie Menin praised the plan. A federal judge has already put an outside manager in charge of the jails because of what he called "abnormal and unacceptable" conditions.

And a lighter note: the mayor is on crutches. He tore his Achilles tendon, the tendon at the back of the ankle, during a Thursday night soccer game against the press corps. His team won ten to five, and he scored, but he admitted, "I did not stretch." He'll be in a boot for six to eight weeks.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and what could be one of the biggest Israeli AI exits ever. Calcalist reports that Amazon is close to buying Decart, a young Israeli AI company, for more than seven billion dollars, possibly almost seven and a half billion with bonus payments. The deal could be signed as soon as this weekend. Decart was founded in twenty twenty-three by brothers Dean and Orian Leitersdorf, who are twenty-eight and twenty-three, with Moshe Shalev. It makes software that lets developers move AI work between different kinds of chips, and it builds world models, AI systems that generate interactive 3D environments and video in real time. It raised more than four hundred million dollars from investors like Sequoia and Benchmark and was valued at four billion in May. The interesting part is the chase: Anthropic was in talks to buy it earlier this year and walked away, and Nvidia passed too, before Amazon stepped in. The founders reportedly have to stay five years, with a big chunk paid in year five. Why it matters: Amazon is racing to make its own AI chips more attractive than Nvidia's, and buying Israeli talent is one of its fastest ways to do it.

## Business story

Our business story is about a basketball player who became a startup investor. Omri Casspi was the first Israeli to play in the NBA and won a championship with the Golden State Warriors. After he retired, he started Swish Ventures, a Tel Aviv venture capital fund, meaning a fund that invests in young startups in exchange for a piece of the company. This week he got his first exit, the moment an investor gets paid because a company is sold. Palo Alto Networks is buying Applied Compute, an AI startup founded last year by former OpenAI researchers, for four and a half billion dollars in stock. Casspi's funds invested in it. Globes estimates his profit in the tens of millions of dollars, but his stake hasn't been disclosed.

The bigger story is how fast Swish grew. His first fund was thirty-six million dollars in twenty twenty-two. Last September he raised a two hundred fifty million dollar fund, and Swish now manages about eight hundred million. Calcalist says nine of its twenty-one portfolio companies are unicorns. His biggest bet is Cognition, the AI coding company, which he backed at a one hundred fifty million dollar valuation and is now worth about forty-eight billion. The lesson: in venture, access to the best founders matters most, and a famous name and a big network can open doors.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with a reason to move that goes beyond your mood. Researchers at Ohio State University presented a study this week at Neuroscience twenty twenty-six, the big brain science conference. They housed mice in pairs for six weeks, some with a working running wheel and some with a locked one. When the pairs had to compete for a treat or a single warm spot, the runners shared more and fought less. Their brains also showed more activity in some areas linked to social behavior. It's mice, and it's a conference presentation, not yet a published paper, so don't overread it. But it fits a lot of research in people: regular exercise seems to make us calmer and easier to be around. So if the week left you a little short-tempered, a walk or a run this Shabbat might help you and the people around you.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

Today's money story could be the biggest in cybersecurity history. On Friday, Bloomberg first reported, and Reuters confirmed, that CrowdStrike is in talks to buy Zscaler.

Quick refresher on both. CrowdStrike, based in Texas, is best known for Falcon, its platform that protects endpoints, the laptops, phones and servers people use, and it has expanded into cloud, identity and security operations. It's worth about two hundred thirty-five billion dollars, and its stock has roughly doubled this year. Zscaler, based in San Jose, California, sells zero trust security, meaning every user and connection is checked every time. Its Zero Trust Exchange sits between workers and the apps they use, replacing old-style firewalls, which guard a company's network, and VPNs, the secure tunnels workers use to reach the office network. It's worth about thirty-two billion dollars, and its stock was down about thirteen percent this year. It was founded in two thousand seven by CEO Jay Chaudhry, who is still its largest shareholder and would almost certainly have to approve any deal.

The market reacted fast. Zscaler shares jumped about seventeen to twenty percent in after-hours trading, the trading after the market closes, and CrowdStrike fell. That's normal: the buyer usually pays a premium, a price above the current share price. No price or terms have been reported, neither company commented, and talks could still fall apart. There were reports last year that Broadcom made an offer, and Chaudhry denied he was looking to sell.

Is this big? Huge. If it happens at a premium, it would likely be the largest cyber acquisition ever, bigger than Google's thirty-two billion dollar purchase of Wiz and Palo Alto's roughly twenty-five billion for CyberArk, both of which closed earlier this year. The two companies are already partners: their products have been integrated since twenty nineteen.

There's an Israeli angle too. Zscaler has bought Israeli startups over the years, like Avalor, and has teams here. CrowdStrike, as we heard last week, has about five hundred people in Israel. So a merger would touch a lot of local teams. And there's a cautionary precedent. When CrowdStrike bought the Israeli company XM Cyber in October, for about six hundred million dollars in cash and stock, Calcalist reported that it took the technology and patents, and that only about fifty of roughly three hundred Israeli employees stayed on. XM Cyber disputed the workforce figures. A Zscaler deal would be a very different scale, but it's a reminder that a buyer doesn't always keep the whole team.

Other than this, the last forty-eight hours were quiet for confirmed new rounds and closed deals, and we covered VeroSecurity, StepSecurity, Metano and CyberRidge earlier this week, so I won't repeat them.

Why do these matter for you? Because CISOs are your buyers. When a well-known CISO moves, vendors who know them rush to get a meeting, and smart marketers track these moves as account signals.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and four lessons.

First, the CrowdStrike and Zscaler story is the ultimate platform play. For years, security buyers have complained about having too many tools from too many vendors. The giants answer with one word: consolidation, buying many tools from one company. Palo Alto calls it platformization, and CrowdStrike has pitched Falcon as the one platform for everything. Adding Zscaler would give CrowdStrike the network piece it's missing, so it could tell a buyer: your devices, your cloud, your identities and your network, all from us. For a marketer, the lesson is that the most powerful message in enterprise security right now is "fewer vendors." If you work at a startup, your answer has to be the opposite: we're the best at one painful problem, and we plug into the big platforms you already have.

Second, look at the calendar. AWS published its security guide for re:Invent, Amazon's huge cloud conference in Las Vegas from November thirtieth to December fourth. It has nearly one hundred twenty security sessions, and the first theme on the list is "securing the agentic enterprise," meaning companies full of AI agents acting on their own. When the biggest cloud provider leads with that phrase, you can expect half the booths in the expo hall to say it too. The smart move for a marketer is to plan meetings and content around those exact themes, then find one angle nobody else is using so you stand out.

Third, a lesson from Washington. The White House's top cyber official lost his job this week over outages that weren't attacks at all, including a Microsoft cloud failure and an air traffic control breakdown. That tells you buyers' fears are shifting from "will we get hacked" to "will we stay up." Resilience, meaning staying running when something breaks, is becoming a selling point in its own right, and companies that can show uptime, backup and fast recovery have a timely story to tell. If you're writing messaging this month, that's a word worth testing.

Fourth, a quieter lesson from Eve Maler. Small companies like FusionAuth and Identos can't outspend giants, so they borrow credibility. Adding a respected standards expert as an advisor gives them a name to put in sales decks, blog posts and conference talks, and it signals to technical buyers that the product is built the right way. Advisory boards are a marketing asset, and the best marketers actually use them.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, CrowdStrike and Zscaler in Israel. If a deal happens, both teams here will go through a long integration, with overlapping roles and new ones. Big mergers often push talented marketers out, and those people land at startups, which opens seats behind them. Keep an eye on both careers pages and on who leaves.

Second, FusionAuth. A new revenue chief and a famous advisor in the same month means it's building its go-to-market machine. A company selling to developers and getting ready to grow usually needs product marketing and demand generation next.

Third, re:Invent itself. Companies hire event and field marketers right before big shows, and many Israeli startups send teams to Las Vegas. If you see an Israeli company announcing a booth or a launch timed for re:Invent, that's a good moment to reach out.
