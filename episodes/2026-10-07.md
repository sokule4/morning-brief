Good morning Sophie, and welcome to your Morning Brief. It's Wednesday, October seventh. Today is a heavy day in Israel: three years since the October seventh attack. We'll start there, gently. Then the election race heats up after a big Supreme Court ruling, an Israeli fintech raises from the very banks it serves, and a Van Gogh that could break every record is coming to New York. Let's get into it.

## Headlines

We begin with the anniversary. Israel marks three years since the October seventh massacre today, with memorials, protests and, tonight, the national ceremony in Yarkon Park in Tel Aviv. It's organized by Kumu, a movement of families of the victims, and this is the third year in a row it's being held there. One of the two hosts is Eli Sharabi, the former hostage who lost his wife Lianne, his daughters Noya and Yahel in Kibbutz Be'eri, and his brother Yossi in Hamas captivity. His co-host is the actress and comedian Shani Cohen. It will be broadcast on all the major Israeli channels, and screened in communities around the world, so if you want to watch from New York, you'll be able to find it.

Ahead of the day, Israel's National Security Council, the government body that advises the prime minister on security, warned of a higher risk of attacks around the anniversary, pointing to Iran and Hamas. Nothing specific, but worth knowing if you or your family are traveling.

In Sderot, the border town that was hit hard that day, residents marking the anniversary are sounding defiant. One survivor told reporters that leaving Sderot, or leaving Israel, is exactly what the terrorists want. It's a feeling you'll hear a lot today.

Now to the story Israelis haven't stopped talking about all week: the flydubai flight. On September thirtieth, a flydubai plane flying from the UAE to Tel Aviv suddenly diverted and landed in Tabuk, in Saudi Arabia. Israel says it was an attempted hijacking. According to reports, one of the pilots attacked his fellow pilot in the cockpit and tried to take over the plane, which dropped more than seventeen thousand feet. Crew members and passengers, including Israelis, stepped in, and two off-duty pilots who happened to be on board took the controls and landed safely. No passengers were hurt. We also learned this week that Israeli fighter jets were ready to shoot the plane down if it had to come to that, and that the Shin Bet, Israel's internal security service, joined the interrogation in the UAE. Flydubai has suspended its flights to and from Israel while it investigates. The motive is still unclear, and of course, the conspiracy theories online are already flying.

Next, the election. Israelis vote in exactly twenty days, on October twenty-seventh. Quick background: this is the first time since 1988 that the Knesset, Israel's parliament, has served out its full four-year term. The big fight is between Prime Minister Netanyahu's Likud and the opposition, including a new alliance called Together, led by Naftali Bennett with Yesh Atid. The big news this week came from the Supreme Court. The Central Elections Committee, the body that approves who can run, had tried to ban the main Arab parties from the ballot. The court overruled that, so the Joint List and Ra'am can run. But the court signaled it would block one candidate, Balad's leader Sami Abu Shehadeh, so he dropped out himself, calling it the hardest decision of his political career.

Also in politics: in Modiin, Haredi protesters, meaning ultra-Orthodox Jews, clashed with police and physically blocked military police from arresting a young man who didn't show up for army service. The draft, meaning mandatory military service, and whether the ultra-Orthodox should be exempt, is one of the hottest issues of this election, and moments like this keep it on the front page.

On Iran: satellite images show renewed activity at an Iranian nuclear site that Israel previously struck, a sign Iran may be trying to restart work there. Meanwhile, the wider regional war with Iran rumbles on. In Yemen, Saudi-backed forces retook the port town of Mocha and nearby towns from the Houthis, the Iran-backed rebel group that controls much of Yemen. Those towns sit near the Bab el-Mandeb, a narrow sea passage that a huge share of world shipping goes through, which is why everyone cares. The Houthis hit back with attacks on two Saudi airports, injuring three people.

One diplomatic story to watch: Britain and Israel are in a fight over Britain's consulate in Jerusalem, a mini embassy that has historically served Palestinians. Israel is moving to shut it, and reports say that if it closes this week, the UK will expel Israeli diplomats. And in Gaza, Israeli airstrikes killed at least two people; the army says it was targeting militants.

Quickly around the world. Russia and Ukraine traded some of the biggest drone attacks yet: Ukraine launched more than six hundred and fifty drones toward the Moscow region, and Russian bombing in Kharkiv killed six people. A Russian drone also sank a ship in Romanian waters, which matters because Romania is in NATO, the Western military alliance where an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all.

In Canada, Quebec elected the Parti Québécois, a party that wants Quebec to become its own country. It won a minority government, meaning it has the most seats but not a majority on its own, and its leader is promising a new independence referendum, a public vote on leaving Canada.

In Germany, a former head of the country's foreign intelligence service, August Hanning, eighty years old, was arrested on suspicion of spying for Azerbaijan. And in France, nearly two thousand schools have been blockaded by students protesting understaffing, with thousands of arrests in two weeks.

And a happy one to end the headlines: this year's Nobel Prize in Physics went to Francis Halzen, the Belgian-born scientist behind IceCube, a giant detector buried in the ice at the South Pole that catches neutrinos, tiny ghost-like particles that fly through almost everything.

## New York

Next, back home in New York.

Mayor Mamdani and Prime Minister Netanyahu are still going at it. Quick recap: at the United Nations last month, Netanyahu called Mamdani, by name, the "antisemitic mayor" of New York. Mamdani, who has said Netanyahu should be arrested under an ICC warrant, the International Criminal Court, the world's war crimes court, responded on Theo Von's podcast. He called the moment strange, said he found out while meeting the Dutch prime minister, and said it was a distraction from responsibility. Commentators in Israel are now asking whether picking this fight actually helps Netanyahu with voters at home, three weeks before the election. Expect it to come up again.

On the ground in the city, the Mamdani administration broke ground on new bus lanes on two major Brooklyn streets, part of his promise to make buses faster. In the Bronx, the Department of Transportation proposed a bike network connecting Throggs Neck to Ferry Point Park.

If you take the NYC Ferry, heads up: fares go up on October nineteenth, though middle schoolers will get discounted trips.

On a sadder note, a Bronx man has been charged in an unprovoked stabbing on the J train in Woodhaven, Queens, that killed a father of four from Corona. Subway safety will stay a big conversation this fall.

In Washington Square Park, Robert De Niro and Rosie O'Donnell joined survivors of Jeffrey Epstein at a rally demanding that the Trump administration release all of the Epstein files.

And your cute moment of the day: the Bronx Zoo just debuted a snow leopard cub. Snow leopards are endangered, so every cub is a big deal, and from the photos, this one is very much worth a visit before you fly back on Sunday.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world.

In Britain, the Green Party passed a motion at its conference declaring that "Zionism is racism." That phrase has a history: it echoes a 1975 United Nations resolution that was later withdrawn. The head of the party's own Jewish group now says they're considering leaving the party. It's another sign of how hard progressive politics in the UK has become for many Jews.

Staying in Europe, a French lawmaker admitted to knocking over an Israeli flag at an October seventh memorial at the European Parliament. It caused an uproar, especially timed to this week.

In fashion news with a Jewish angle: Zara pulled a Halloween costume after critics said its striped design looked like the uniforms prisoners were forced to wear in Nazi concentration camps. Zara has had a similar controversy before, back in 2014, with a striped children's shirt.

And in sports: tension is building ahead of Israel's rematch against Ireland in soccer. Israeli journalists confronted the Irish Football Association over statements it made, and the games are being watched as much for politics as for football.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting in Israel.

The biggest Israeli round of the week went to Capitolis, a fintech company. We'll do the full story later in the show, but the headline is two hundred and twenty million dollars at a valuation, meaning what investors say the whole company is worth, of about one point nine billion dollars.

In AI security, Reco raised fifty million dollars, with AT&T as both an investor and a customer. Reco helps companies see which AI agents are running inside their systems. AI agents are AI tools that can take actions on their own, like sending emails or changing files, not just chat. As companies add more of them, someone has to track what each one can access, and that's Reco's pitch. The market for securing these agents is small today but expected to grow fast, which is why investors are piling in.

Also from Israel: HyperGuest, a hotel technology company, raised twenty-five million dollars from Apax Partners. And Fidelity sold its stake in NextVision, the Israeli maker of tiny cameras for drones, for roughly four hundred and twenty-six million dollars.

Globally, a few things worth knowing. Mistral, the French AI company that's become Europe's answer to OpenAI, released a very large new model meant to compete with the top American systems. Lambda, a company that rents out the powerful computer chips used to run AI, is preparing a huge round ahead of a plan to go public on the stock market. And Type One Energy raised two hundred million dollars to build a fusion power plant, aiming for 2034. Fusion is the dream of making energy the way the sun does, cleanly, and it keeps getting closer and more expensive at the same time.

One trend piece from TechCrunch that's interesting for anyone in marketing: more and more websites are blocking AI agents from visiting them. That's a real problem for companies building agents that are supposed to shop, book and research for you, and it's turning into a fight over who controls the open web.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, GTM, meaning how companies sell and market their products.

Let's start with the Reco round again, through a GTM lens, because it's a great example. Reco says the new money will go to expanding sales, partnerships, distribution channels and customer support. That's what a Series C, a company's third big funding round, usually looks like: the product works, now it's time to sell it everywhere. When an Israeli cyber company announces that kind of round, it usually means new GTM hires in the US and in Tel Aviv in the following months. Worth keeping an eye on their careers page.

In deals: SecurityWeek counted thirty-nine cybersecurity acquisitions in September. A few names you'll recognize. Palo Alto Networks bought Console, a startup that uses AI agents to handle security alerts automatically. Dragos, a big name in OT security, meaning protecting factories, power plants and other physical infrastructure, made an acquisition to expand. IBM bought a UK security consultancy. The pattern across almost all of these is the same: AI, and specifically AI agents, is the theme everyone is buying.

In breaches: a hacking group called ShinyHunters got into data on thousands of FBI employees, through a contractor working for Accenture who missed a security update. The FBI removed the contractor. In a twist, a suspected leader of ShinyHunters, known as Rey, was arrested in Jordan and is reportedly cooperating with investigators. And in Denmark, hackers abused legitimate access to steal records on eight point eight million people from the national population register, basically the entire country.

One for your Iran file: researchers say an Iranian state-backed hacking group pretended to be recruiters from Dubai Airports to trick targets in Iraq. Fake recruiter scams are a growing tactic, so if a too-good job offer lands in your inbox during your job search, check the sender carefully.

On the marketing side, an Israeli startup called enso, from a co-founder of RapidAPI, raised fifteen million dollars, according to Calcalist. Enso uses fleets of AI agents to test growth tactics, things like getting found in AI search answers, outbound email sequences, communities and newsletters. "Agentic GTM" is becoming a real category, and it's exactly the space where your sales and marketing background meets AI.

And in people moves, Alex Stamos, a well-known former security chief at Facebook, joined the AI coding company Cognition. When big security names move to AI companies, it tells you where the industry thinks the action is.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Capitolis, the Israeli fintech that convinced Wall Street to invest in the company that sells to Wall Street.

Capitolis was founded by Gil Mandelzis. It builds technology for big banks and investment firms to manage the money and assets they lend and borrow behind the scenes. A big part of that is something called securities lending, which is when big investors lend out stocks or bonds they own, usually for a fee. You never see it as a consumer, but it's an enormous market.

Here's what makes the story fun. This week Capitolis raised two hundred and twenty million dollars, and the investors include the same giant banks that are its customers: Citi, Bank of America and JPMorgan among them. When your customers literally buy into your company, that's about the strongest vote of confidence you can get.

And they're not just sitting on the cash. Capitolis is using it to buy a Boston company called eSecLending for two hundred million dollars, all cash. That's its fourth acquisition in five years. The deal adds about one hundred and twenty employees to Capitolis's roughly two hundred. Mandelzis says the goal isn't just to dominate a niche, it's massive growth.

The lesson for anyone in GTM: Capitolis sells to a tiny number of enormous customers. When you have twenty or thirty possible buyers in the whole world, every relationship is everything, and turning customers into investors and partners is a strategy, not an accident.

## Art and design

Next, art and design.

The big one: Sotheby's is bringing a private collection from Argentina to auction in New York in November, twelve paintings estimated at about four hundred and fifty million dollars total. They belonged to Nelly Arrieta de Blaquier and her family and were kept for decades at their estate near Buenos Aires. The star is a Van Gogh painting of a chestnut tree in pink and white bloom, painted in May 1890, just weeks before he died. It's estimated at one hundred and eighty million dollars, and Sotheby's thinks it could pass two hundred million. The current Van Gogh auction record is about one hundred and seventeen million, so this could smash it. There's also a Cézanne, Harlequin, estimated around one hundred and twenty million, which would be a record for him too. Five of the works are on view in London right now.

Speaking of London, Frieze London and Frieze Masters, two of the most important art fairs in the world, run October fourteenth to eighteenth in Regent's Park, with nearly three hundred galleries from forty-eight countries. One new section, The Code Universe, looks at the forces shaping pop culture since the late 1970s. For anyone in the art world, Frieze week is when deals and relationships happen.

In gallery news, Thaddaeus Ropac signed the painter Denzil Forrester, who's seventy and famous for paintings of London nightlife, and he'll show at Frieze. And in New York, Luhring Augustine is closing its Tribeca gallery, another sign of how tough the middle of the gallery market is right now.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today. Today will be emotionally heavy for a lot of people, especially if you're watching coverage of the anniversary. A simple tool: get outside for a twenty-minute walk, ideally without headphones, and notice five things you can see and hear. It's one of the easiest ways to bring your stress down, and it works just as well on a busy New York street as on the Tel Aviv boardwalk.
