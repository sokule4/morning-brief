## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Monday, November twenty-third, and this is your Morning Brief.

The AI stock crash on Friday was so sharp that the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange hit its emergency brake for the first time ever. And in your Cyber Watch, Palo Alto Networks is in talks to buy Terra, a young Israeli AI security startup, for what could be more than half a billion dollars. Let's go.

## Israel

We start here at home, where the "marathon" coalition talks turned out to be more of a long jog. On Sunday afternoon in Herzliya, negotiators from Likud and from Yashar, the party of Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief who holds the mandate to form a government, sat down for about four hours. That's a lot longer than last week's one-hour meetings.

Who was in the room? For Likud, faction chairman Ofir Katz and the lawyer Michael Rabello. For Yashar, Chili Tropper, Yoav Horowitz and Guy Gutman. Notably missing: Naftali Bennett's party, B'Yachad, which sat in on last week's round. Before the meeting, Katz said Likud wanted talks "not just for appearances."

What came out? A short joint statement saying they discussed "core issues" and would meet again, with no date set. The topics were the big three: the draft for Haredi men, the judicial overhaul, and a state commission of inquiry into October seventh. Nothing was agreed. Eisenkot's office said the hardest question, who would be prime minister, didn't even come up.

There was one clear clash, according to Channel 12 and Ynet: Otzma Yehudit, Itamar Ben Gvir's far-right party. Yashar won't sit with it; Likud hasn't ruled it out. A Times of Israel headline also says Likud is demanding Netanyahu be the only prime minister, with no rotation, as the price of unity, though that's reported, not confirmed by Likud.

Here's why both sides keep talking. The seat math is stuck. Eisenkot's bloc has forty-eight seats. Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three, including thirteen for the ultra-Orthodox, the strictly religious parties Shas and United Torah Judaism. The two Arab parties, with nineteen seats, back Eisenkot. Nobody has the sixty-one needed for a majority.

So the real action may be with the ultra-Orthodox. Channel 12 reports Yashar made "significant" progress with United Torah Judaism over the past twenty-four hours. Sources in Degel HaTorah, one of the two parties inside United Torah Judaism, spoke of "positive progress," but said the sides are still "far from exchanging" written proposals. Their rabbis rejected Eisenkot's earlier offer, which would have exempted three percent of yeshiva students in each age group from the army. Eisenkot is expected to bring a new draft plan this week.

Inside Eisenkot's camp, the voices are pulling in different directions. Yair Golan of the Democrats says a minority government is "the only realistic option," and that anyone seeking unity with Netanyahu "is stabbing his own voters in the back." Avigdor Liberman wants "painful compromises" with the Haredim instead, and opposes a minority government. And Bennett said over the weekend he backs a unity government with Likud, but only under Eisenkot, and ruled out switching sides.

Also this week: Eisenkot's bloc plans to bring a bill by Oded Forer of Yisrael Beytenu for a preliminary reading, the first of several Knesset votes a bill needs, as early as Wednesday. It would set term limits, a cap on how long someone can serve, of eight years for prime ministers, and bar anyone who's been indicted, formally charged with a crime, from forming a government. It's clearly aimed at Netanyahu, and Channel 12 says it probably won't move without big changes.

What's next: the clock. Eisenkot's twenty-eight days run out around December sixth, and the president can add fourteen more.

Now the economy, because today matters for your wallet. The Bank of Israel announces its interest rate decision today. Last month it held the rate at three point two five percent. The backdrop is rough: the shekel has fallen more than eight percent against the dollar since the October twenty-seventh election, among the world's worst performers, as investors worry about political deadlock. A weak shekel makes imports and trips abroad more expensive, so listen for what the bank says about that.

On security, Channel 12 reports that army intelligence warned Netanyahu last week, in a closed meeting, that a strike on Iran by Israel alone could open "multiple fronts" at once: Lebanon, Syria and Gaza. That reportedly clashes with the view of Netanyahu and Mossad chief Roman Gofman that an operation could last three to five days. Liberman said an attack now would be "completely motivated by politics." The army says there's no change to public safety guidelines.

And in the West Bank, settlers set fire to Palestinian homes and cars near Ramallah overnight into Sunday. Eisenkot called it "Jewish terror," and Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar condemned "a handful of extremists" and promised to enforce the law.

## World

Now to the world, and the Iran war in the Gulf. Over the weekend, Bahrain said an Iranian strike hit a civilian industrial site in Sitra, causing a fire but no injuries. CBS counts it as the fourth attempted attack on Bahrain in about a week. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed it hit American positions there and destroyed two radars for Patriot missiles, the American air defense system, calling it revenge for a US strike on an Iranian radar site. Iran's army also claimed it hit an American F-16 fighter jet. US Central Command answered that "all US fighter jets in the Middle East are safe and accounted for."

At sea, it keeps getting worse. UKMTO, the British navy's shipping office, said two tankers were hit by unidentified projectiles near Oman on Saturday. Al Jazeera counts about twenty-five tankers hit in and around the Strait of Hormuz in a single week. Before the war, about one hundred thirty-five ships a day passed through the strait. In one recent week, only two hundred sixty-three passed in total. War-risk insurance, the extra cover ships buy to sail through a conflict zone, has jumped from a fraction of a percent of a ship's value to as much as seven and a half percent. Oil is around eighty-three dollars a barrel, more than fifteen dollars above pre-war levels.

Why is Iran escalating? Analysts told Al Jazeera it's about pressure. The US is blockading Iran's economy, and Iran's deputy foreign minister said, "We consider the economic siege a hostile act." Qatari mediators were in Tehran, but Iran says its conditions to reopen the strait haven't been met.

And Trump? On Saturday he told Fox he wants a deal with Iran that's "going to be permanent," one that would hold even under a future president. He's also said he's "not rushing" and will "see what happens after the new year starts," and the Times of Israel reports he's inclined to restart the offensive if there's no deal by year's end. On Truth Social, he claimed Iran stepped up its attacks because Democrats won the midterms.

Now Ukraine. On Sunday, Russian drones killed at least four people in the Kyiv region. In the town of Dymer, part of a five-story apartment building collapsed, and rescuers feared about ten people were trapped. It's another sign that last week's Trump, Xi and Putin summit, whose short peace declaration left out Ukraine entirely, hasn't changed anything on the ground.

And in Washington, the first big fight of the new Democratic majority. After the midterms gave Democrats both chambers of Congress, Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland is challenging Chuck Schumer for Senate Democratic leader. The vote is a secret ballot set for December first, and there are thirteen new Democratic senators who haven't voted in one before. Senator Elissa Slotkin backs Van Hollen. Sherrod Brown and Mazie Hirono back Schumer, who says he has the votes. The Washington Post reports even Trump weighed in. Why it matters: whoever wins sets how hard Democrats fight Trump for the next two years.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the big question this week is the wind. Thursday is the one hundredth Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, and Fox Weather says a developing coastal storm could bring gusty winds. The city's rule is strict: if sustained winds top twenty-three miles an hour, or gusts top thirty-four, the giant balloons stay on the ground. Below that, handlers can fly them lower. The parade starts at eight thirty in the morning, New York time, and it has eight new floats and balloons this year, including a Hogwarts holiday float and a salute to service dogs, plus some characters returning after decades away.

And a very New York award. The M42, the crosstown bus on Forty-second Street, won the "Pokey Award" for the city's slowest bus for the second year in a row. Transit advocates found it averaged four point seven eight miles an hour, nine percent slower than last year. Council member Lincoln Restler said it "crawls slower than I bike and sometimes even walk." The good news: the Q27 in Queens won the award for most improved.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, starting in France. A new survey by the American Jewish Committee with the French pollsters IFOP and Fondapol shows how afraid French Jews have become. Twenty-nine percent say they've taken down the mezuzah, the small scroll case Jews hang on the doorpost, up ten points from the last survey. Forty-eight percent have stopped wearing religious symbols in public. Two thirds say France isn't safe for Jews, and sixty-four percent are considering leaving, up from fifty-two percent in twenty twenty-four. Politics shapes it too: sixty-nine percent say they'd consider leaving if Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the leader of France's hard left, became president, compared to thirty-one percent if the far-right's Marine Le Pen won.

And a hopeful story from Morocco. Morocco's ambassador to the US, Youssef Amrani, announced a new nationwide campaign to protect Jewish heritage, saying it's "not simply part of the kingdom's past." Since twenty ten, Morocco has restored more than one hundred seventy Jewish cemeteries and about twenty shrines. The new phase includes an inventory of every site, an AI project with Ariel University to read worn gravestones, and an online map for tourists and pilgrims.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a Friday traders won't forget. On November twentieth, the Nasdaq, the US stock index full of tech companies, fell more than five percent in a single day, wiping out all its gains for twenty twenty-six. The index of chip stocks fell almost eleven percent, with every one of its thirty companies down. The S&P 500 and the Dow briefly fell into a correction, meaning ten percent below their recent peak.

The trigger: Reuters reported that the SEC, the US agency that polices the stock market, is reviewing possible accounting issues at Nvidia, the company whose chips power most of the world's AI. The SEC declined to comment, and a review doesn't mean anyone did anything wrong. But after weeks of nerves, including Jensen Huang's exit as CEO, Nvidia fell as much as thirteen percent.

Here's the Israeli part. Globes reports that Friday triggered the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange's first-ever circuit breaker, an automatic pause in trading when prices fall too fast. The TA-125, Tel Aviv's main index of its biggest companies, is now about nineteen percent below its peak, and many tech stocks are down fifty to seventy percent. Avner Hadad of Phoenix, the Israeli investment house, wrote to investors: "There's something scary about this date: November twentieth," the same date as a big crash in two thousand eight. But he added, "I have a feeling that many new fortunes are being made during this period."

And one more sign of a cooling job market here: Intel is making another round of cuts, according to Business Insider, and Globes notes Intel Israel has shrunk from about twelve thousand people to nine thousand three hundred since twenty twenty-four. Globes counts nearly two thousand Israeli tech layoffs since the start of October.

## Business story

Our business story is about an old name in Israeli tech getting new owners at a big price. Elron Ventures has been around since the early days of Israeli high tech. It invests in young companies and has backed famous successes like Given Imaging, the maker of the pill-sized camera you swallow, and CartiHeal, a cartilage repair company.

Now the Arieli family's holding company is selling its fifty-eight percent stake, its controlling share, to BlueGate, a group backed by the Melnik family of New York, for one hundred eighty million shekels. That's a one hundred percent premium, double the price the shares traded at. Micah Lakin Avni, a well-known Israeli businessman, is set to become chairman. Investors liked it: Elron's shares are up about twenty-eight percent since the offer.

Why would anyone pay double in a market this nervous? Because buyers who believe in Israeli tech see a bargain when everyone else is scared, which is exactly Avner Hadad's point about new fortunes. Owning Elron means owning a ready-made portfolio of startups, plus the reputation and deal flow that come with a brand that's been around for decades.

## Trending

What people are arguing about this week: the Cambridge Dictionary picked "human" as its Word of the Year. The reason is AI. The dictionary says people looked up the word as chatbots and AI tools blurred the line between people and machines. ## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a study about what you eat while losing weight. Researchers at Washington University in St. Louis, writing in the journal Cell Metabolism, put forty-two adults with obesity and prediabetes, blood sugar that's high but not yet diabetes, on one of three diets. A ketogenic diet, very low in carbs and high in fat. A Mediterranean diet. Or a low-fat, plant-forward diet. The researchers cooked every meal, and everyone lost about ten percent of their weight.

The difference was inside. Fat in the liver dropped sixty-seven percent on keto, versus forty-five percent on the other two. And about half the keto group no longer had prediabetes, compared to twenty-nine percent on Mediterranean and seven percent on low-fat. The study was small and short, so it's not a reason to go keto tomorrow. The practical takeaway is that how you lose weight may matter, not just how much, and that's a conversation worth having with your doctor.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

The lead story is Israeli, and it fits a pattern we keep seeing. On Sunday, Calcalist reported that Palo Alto Networks, the California cybersecurity giant that bought CyberArk, is in talks to buy Terra Security, a Tel Aviv startup, in a deal worth hundreds of millions of dollars, possibly more than five hundred million.

What does Terra do? It builds AI agents for offensive security, meaning it attacks a company's systems the way a hacker would, so the company finds the holes first. Normally that's done with a pentest, a penetration test, where human experts try to break in once or twice a year. Terra's agents do it continuously: they find weaknesses and chain them together into real attack paths, with humans guiding the sensitive decisions.

Terra is very young. It was founded at the end of twenty twenty-four by CEO Shahar Peled and CTO Gal Malachi. It raised an eight million dollar seed round in April twenty twenty-five and a thirty million dollar Series A that September, led by Felicis, with Dell Technologies Capital and others. So about thirty-eight to forty million dollars raised in total, depending on the report.

Is five hundred million big? For a company that's about two years old, very. That's more than ten times the money it raised. The talks aren't final, and neither company has commented.

The bigger picture: Calcalist counts twelve Israeli startups Palo Alto has bought since twenty fourteen, with CyberArk the biggest at about twenty-five billion dollars. Earlier this month, we heard Palo Alto was also talking to Clutch and Beacon, two more young Israeli companies. Palo Alto seems to be shopping for AI security here like few others.

And one more Palo Alto number, also from Calcalist on Sunday. Even after cutting about seven hundred jobs at CyberArk, about a hundred of them in Israel, Palo Alto's total headcount grew. It had twenty-two thousand three hundred thirty-nine employees at the end of October, four hundred eighteen more than at the end of July, and it expects to keep growing over the next year. CEO Nikesh Arora has said he wants Palo Alto to stay the largest cyber employer in Israel.

Now, an update on the biggest story in cyber, CrowdStrike's talks to buy Zscaler. There's still no confirmation from either company. But analysts at Jefferies, the investment bank, wrote over the weekend that there's a "better than a coin flip likelihood" of a deal, at two hundred fifty-five dollars a share or more for Zscaler. They also said a deal would make CrowdStrike a "more formidable" rival to Palo Alto across many product areas, while warning that regulators would look closely and that Zscaler has to want to sell.

Other than that, it was a quiet weekend, and I couldn't confirm a new cyber funding round announced since Friday.

## People moves

On people moves, the hire worth knowing about is one you may have missed, and it's connected to today's lead story. On November fifth, Terra named Tal Kapulnik its CMO. She came from Eon, the cloud backup company, where she was VP, vice president, of marketing and built the brand and the marketing team from scratch. Before that, she ran organic marketing at Lemonade, the insurance company, and worked in product marketing for Google and YouTube in Europe, and at Edelman, the PR agency, in New York.

Her quote when she joined is a good one for any interview: she said CISOs "have become tired of the hype," and her job is to show security teams real results.

Think about the timing. Terra hired a senior marketing leader less than three weeks before acquisition talks became public. That's not unusual. A strong brand and a clear story make a young company look more valuable, and buyers notice.

Other than that, I couldn't confirm a new chief marketing officer at a cyber company over the weekend. SecurityWeek's weekly list of people moves should update later today.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons from today's news.

First, marketing is part of the exit, the moment a startup is sold. When a buyer like Palo Alto looks at a two-year-old company, it isn't just buying code. It's buying a category story, a list of customers, and a name that CISOs already recognize. Terra's message, continuous AI pentesting instead of a once-a-year report, is easy to repeat, and that matters when a buyer has to explain the deal to its own investors.

Second, the Jefferies note shows how competitors become the story. Notice how the analysts framed CrowdStrike and Zscaler: not by what the products do, but by how they'd compete with Palo Alto. In a platform market, every company's positioning gets defined against the biggest player. If you're marketing a smaller company, you need a clear answer to "why not just buy it from Palo Alto?"

Third, look at the calendar, because the next three weeks are event season. AWS, Amazon's cloud arm, holds re:Invent, its huge conference in Las Vegas, from November thirtieth to December fourth. Expect a wave of launches and AWS Marketplace deals, where companies sell their software through Amazon's own store. Then Black Hat Europe, the big hacker and security conference, is December seventh to tenth at ExCeL in London. One keynote title is worth noticing: "AI Security is Not AI Safety," by Heidy Khlaaf. That's a positioning fight in itself, about whether protecting AI systems from attackers is the same job as making AI behave safely. Companies are choosing sides in their messaging right now.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Terra. A new CMO who builds teams from scratch, and a possible sale to Palo Alto. If the deal happens, Terra's marketing would likely fold into Palo Alto. If it doesn't, the company will need to grow its own marketing fast. Either way, it's a name to know, and Tal Kapulnik is someone worth following.

Second, Palo Alto in Israel. It's growing, it's buying, and it says it wants to stay the biggest cyber employer here. Each acquisition adds teams that need integration, combining a bought company into the bigger one, and that includes product marketing and partner marketing.

Third, event season. Israeli startups heading to re:Invent and Black Hat Europe often hire event and field marketers right before big shows. If you see a company announce a booth, that's a good moment to reach out.
