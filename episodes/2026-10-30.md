## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Friday, October thirtieth, and this is your Morning Brief. Israel has finished counting every ballot, the soldiers gave Likud one more seat, and the deadlock hasn't moved, while the country's top election official has asked the Shin Bet to investigate suspected foreign money in the vote. Iran says four tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz, as the US tells its allies to get ready for possible strikes after Tuesday's midterms. And Kalshi, the betting-style prediction market, is now worth forty billion dollars, in the same week one US state charged its founders with crimes. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start with the final count. On Thursday the Central Elections Committee finished counting the double-envelope ballots, the votes cast away from home, mostly by soldiers. The result: Likud went from twenty-six seats to twenty-seven. But that seat came from Shas, the Sephardi ultra-Orthodox party, which drops to six, and Shas is in Netanyahu's own camp. So the big picture is exactly where it was. Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three seats. Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc has forty-eight. The two Arab parties have nineteen, a record, and the final split is reported as eleven for the Joint List, an alliance of Arab-led parties, and eight for Ra'am, the Islamist party that sat in Bennett's government in twenty twenty-one. Sixty-one is a majority, so nobody has one. Turnout was almost seventy-eight percent, the highest in decades. Defense Minister Israel Katz called the soldiers' vote "a great test of confidence" in the government's security policy. Official results come next Wednesday, November fourth, and then President Herzog starts meeting each party to decide who gets the first shot at forming a government.

The bigger story on Thursday was a probe. Noam Sohlberg, the Supreme Court justice who chairs the elections committee, wrote to Shin Bet chief David Zini asking him to investigate whether the vote may have been influenced by "funds from foreign entities acting against the Israeli interest." He asked for findings within a week, and said he couldn't vouch for the substance of the suspicions. He didn't name any country, but Hebrew media report the suspicion is that money from Qatar paid for groups that encouraged Arab citizens to vote. Qatar calls that "ridiculous and dangerous." Here's why it could matter legally: Israeli parties can't take donations from non-citizens, and paying people to vote is a crime. Driving voters to the polls, though, is perfectly legal.

Now the context, because this lands on top of Likud's claim that the Arab parties "stole" the election. On Thursday the police said they found "no supporting evidence" for that claim. Shin Bet sources stress the new probe is limited, and that nothing in it should stop the certified results from going to the president on schedule. And the elections committee's acting director says there's no basis for claims of widespread fraud. Still, each side is using it. Likud is calling it "Ra'amgate" and says it proves no Zionist party should partner with the Arab parties. Itamar Ben-Gvir called for arrests of Arab party leaders if there are legal grounds. Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of running an "illegitimate influence operation," and warned against using the probe to delay the swearing-in of the new Knesset. Ra'am's leader, Mansour Abbas, denied any link and said his party will cooperate fully. Could the result actually change? Very unlikely. Challenges go to the Jerusalem District Court within fourteen days of the final results, and Israel has never annulled a national election. The real impact is political: it makes it harder for Eisenkot's camp to lean on the Arab parties.

So what about a government? The Jerusalem Post reports that people close to Netanyahu are pushing a national unity government with Eisenkot, at first without the ultra-Orthodox parties. One version includes a rotation, a deal where two leaders take turns as prime minister: Netanyahu would stay for about two years, then hand over to Eisenkot. Another version offers Eisenkot only a senior ministry. The idea is that this government would pass the law drafting ultra-Orthodox men into the army, and the ultra-Orthodox parties would join afterward, without having to vote for it themselves. Eisenkot has promised not to serve under Netanyahu, and his camp calls the idea "complete suicide, both morally and politically." Meanwhile, Channel Twelve reports that Avigdor Liberman, a key opposition leader, has stopped answering calls from Bennett and Eisenkot. Bottom line: weeks of bargaining, and a real chance of yet another election.

One more Israel story you'll notice next time you fly. A month after the flydubai attack, when an Omani co-pilot on a Dubai to Tel Aviv flight allegedly stabbed the captain and tried to crash the plane, Israel issued strict new rules for every airline, foreign and Israeli. Crew lists must now be sent forty-eight hours before departure, instead of after takeoff, so they can be background-checked. All non-Israelis entering or leaving, including pilots, cabin crew and passengers, will have to fill out an online form forty-eight hours ahead. The Mossad and Shin Bet have marked certain countries as "red," with extra checks for arrivals from them. And the Shin Bet, which already handled security for Israeli airlines, now takes on foreign airlines too. So expect a bit more paperwork for visiting friends and family.

Now Iran. Iran's Revolutionary Guard says three oil tankers exploded and caught fire after hitting naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz within twenty-four hours, and Iran's army reported a fourth. Iran's line is that the ships used an "unauthorized southern route" with their radar switched off. That's the lane the US has been encouraging, and which Trump declared free of mines in August. Iran insists ships may only use routes it controls near its own coast. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, was about one hundred fifteen dollars a barrel on Thursday morning, up more than twelve percent in a month.

On the diplomatic side, the Wall Street Journal reports that Vice President JD Vance sent a letter to Mohsen Rezaei, the secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, to restart talks. Trump said he approved it. Iran says it received the letter but hasn't answered. A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Qatar's new ideas are "unacceptable." At the same time, according to the AP, US officials have told Middle Eastern allies to prepare for possible new strikes on Iran after the November third midterms, and for Iranian retaliation. Trump hasn't made a final decision.

And inside the Pentagon, the US Defense Department's headquarters, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth fired Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, the head of strategy, plans and policy on the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the group of top military commanders who advise the president. A Pentagon official said he was accused of leaking classified information about the Iran war. A former official told The Hill that "now is not the time" to lose that job.

In Washington, the midterms are four days away. Democrats lead the national polling average by about eight points, fifty-one to forty-three. Democrats need a net gain of three seats to take the House, and four for the Senate, which is harder. One race to watch is Texas, where Democrat James Talarico narrowly leads Republican Ken Paxton. Democrats haven't won a Senate race in Texas since nineteen eighty-eight. Also this week, a judge unsealed new charges against former FBI director James Comey under the Espionage Act, the law against leaking national defense secrets. It's the third federal criminal case against him in thirteen months. Prosecutors say he helped leak classified information to the New York Times in twenty sixteen. Comey says the information had been cleared for release, and that the deadline to charge him has passed.

Two quick ones from around the world. Christine Lagarde, president of the ECB, the European Central Bank, which sets interest rates for the twenty countries that use the euro, will step down on February seventh, nine months early. The timing lets EU leaders pick her successor before France's presidential election, which polls suggest Marine Le Pen, leader of France's nationalist far right, could win. And in Ukraine, a Russian drone hit a crowded platform at the main train station in Dnipro, killing thirteen people.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the governor's race may come down to the city. Early voting ends Sunday and Election Day is Tuesday. In twenty twenty-two, Governor Kathy Hochul won the five boroughs seventy to thirty, lost the rest of the state by eight points, and won overall by six. So her campaign is all about city turnout, which is why she's been campaigning with Mayor Mamdani, who's popular with younger Democrats. The latest statewide polls have her well ahead of Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive: fifty-four to forty in the Siena poll, fifty-three to forty-two in Marist. Blakeman's own advisers say he needs at least thirty-two percent of the city vote to be "in the game." He predicts thirty-six. The polls have him at twenty-three to twenty-six. And turnout is surging: more than two hundred ninety-four thousand New York City voters cast ballots in the first four days, up seventy-two percent from twenty twenty-two. Blakeman's take on Hochul's late attack ads: "you don't attack a Republican and raise his name ID this late in a campaign unless you're in real trouble."

In case you missed it, a New York icon is closing. The Plaza Hotel, on Fifth Avenue at Central Park South, open since nineteen oh seven, will close in January for at least four years to restore its landmark facade. The city's Buildings Department says its most recent inspection found the facade "unsafe," which is why there's a sidewalk shed around it. The last big closure was from two thousand five to two thousand eight, for a four hundred fifty million dollar restoration. The human side: a bride named Christine Carney had put down a deposit for a May twenty twenty-seven wedding there, just two weeks before the announcement, and learned from the concierge that it's off. She called it "quite unforgivable." If you know anyone with a Plaza wedding on the books, they're venue shopping now.

And Halloween is tomorrow. The Village Halloween Parade, the biggest in the world, steps off at seven p.m. from Canal Street and heads up Sixth Avenue to Fifteenth Street. This year's theme is "Body and Soul," and the grand marshal is the performance artist Joey Arias. Anyone in costume can march; no ticket needed. Then on Sunday, the marathon, and clocks fall back an hour in the US.

## Jewish world

Now to the Jewish world, starting in Thailand. Thailand's prime minister, Anutin Charnvirakul, who is also interior minister, signed an order revoking the Thai citizenship of Rabbi Yosef Chaim Kantor, the Chabad rabbi who has led Bangkok's Jewish community since nineteen ninety-three. Chabad is the Hasidic movement that runs Jewish centers for travelers and locals all over the world, and Thailand, with all its Israeli backpackers, is a big hub. Kantor became a Thai citizen through naturalization, the legal process for a foreigner to become a citizen, in twenty thirteen. The government says he broke the conditions by using his US passport sixteen times to travel to and from Australia, most recently in January. The bigger backdrop: Thai investigators raided Chabad-linked offices in Bangkok this month, looking at more than sixty companies registered at one address, land purchases, donations and tax. No criminal findings have been announced, and officials say the inquiry is about "individual conduct," not religion or nationality.

Back in America, a warning to the Orthodox community. Avi Schnall, a New Jersey assemblyman who is also chief operating officer of Agudath Israel of America, a major Orthodox organization, said in a video that "several people" in the community were picked up by ICE or Border Patrol after voting while holding only a visa or green card. Only citizens can vote in federal elections. He called them "innocent mistakes" and urged anyone without citizenship not to vote. His claim hasn't been independently confirmed, and the number of people wasn't given.

A related bit of New York politics: a new poll of Jewish Democratic primary voters in the city found Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch, who is Jewish, narrowly ahead of Mayor Mamdani, forty-four to forty-two, in a hypothetical twenty twenty-nine mayoral race. Among all Democratic primary voters, though, Mamdani leads fifty-four to seventeen. And upstate, Congressman Mike Lawler, in a toss-up race, meaning too close to call, says the polls are missing "over thirty thousand Orthodox Jews coming out to vote."

The "Nobody Wants This" fight has a new voice: the mom. Tovah Feldshuh, who plays Adam Brody's mother on the show, responded to his GQ interview, where he called the Gaza war a genocide and said he wants "a free Palestine." Feldshuh told the New York Post, "To accuse the Jewish people of genocide is wild," and called it "right out of Hitler's playbook. You take the victim and you make the victim the perpetrator." That follows the show's creator, Erin Foster, saying she loves Adam but disagrees with him.

And two stories about history and memory. In Argentina, a court ordered the Royal Spanish Academy, the institution that publishes the official Spanish dictionary, to remove one definition of "judío," the word for Jewish, which describes a greedy person or a moneylender. Jewish groups say the entry is still online, and the Academy defends it. And this week, a Torah ark curtain and a Torah binder looted from Berlin's Fasanenstrasse Synagogue on Kristallnacht went home to Berlin's Jewish community. After the war, an American soldier traded cigarettes and Crisco oil to a Nazi doctor for them. His son gave them to Temple Chai in Phoenix, where they hung for decades. The rabbi there, Bonnie Koppell, called the trip home "wildly touching."

## Tech, startups & AI

Next, tech, where Amazon closed out the big earnings week, and the theme is the same: AI costs a fortune. Amazon Web Services, AWS, the company's cloud business that rents computing power to other companies, grew thirty-nine percent to about forty-six billion dollars for the quarter, better than Wall Street expected. But total revenue, about two hundred two billion dollars, came in slightly below forecasts, and so did profit. Amazon still plans about two hundred twenty billion dollars of capex this year, mostly on data centers. Its free cash flow, the cash left after all that spending, swung from plus fifteen billion to minus forty-two billion dollars over the past year. The stock fell about five percent after hours. Add it up, and Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are expected to spend more than seven hundred billion dollars this year building for AI.

Now an unusual deal. Thrive Holdings is buying ADT, the home alarm and security company, for about seven point six billion dollars, or fifteen point three billion including debt. Thrive Holdings is a spin-off of Thrive Capital, the venture firm founded by Josh Kushner, Jared Kushner's brother, and OpenAI is both a partner and an investor. The model: buy old-economy companies, keep them for the long term and use AI to revive them. OpenAI supplies the models and engineers. Thrive says it already owns more than seventy-five companies. Neither side has announced specific new products yet, but the bet is that home security, cameras and sensors, gets much smarter with AI.

In Israeli tech, a mixed day for Mobileye, the Jerusalem company that makes chips and software for driver assistance and self-driving cars, controlled by Intel. Revenue was four hundred ninety-two million dollars for the quarter, down two percent from last year, but above forecasts, and it raised its outlook for the year. The stock still slipped. It recently hit an all-time low, and the company is now worth about six billion dollars. Part of the worry is leadership: founder and CEO Amnon Shashua announced in the summer that he'll step down, and there's still no successor. Mobileye is also pushing into robotaxis, driverless cars you hail like an Uber, with public rides with safety drivers now running in Hamburg, Germany.

And a startup raise that's close to your world. Bites, founded in Israel in twenty twenty, with headquarters in New York and a development center in Tel Aviv, raised a forty million dollar Series A led by PeakSpan Capital. It makes ninety-second training lessons for employees who don't sit at desks, like store workers, hotel staff and factory crews. The lessons arrive on WhatsApp, text message or Teams, in more than one hundred fifty languages, with no app to download. Customers include Unilever, Coca-Cola and Shake Shack. One number investors love: net revenue retention, how much existing customers spend this year versus last, is one hundred sixty-one percent. In plain words, customers keep buying more.

## Cybersecurity & GTM

Now cybersecurity and go-to-market. In case you missed it, the biggest Israeli cyber deal of the month: Proofpoint, the American email and data security company owned by the private equity firm Thoma Bravo, is buying Varonis for six point three billion dollars in cash. Varonis was founded in Israel in two thousand four by Yaki Faitelson, and it finds and protects a company's sensitive data. It has about twenty-eight hundred employees, about a thousand of them in Israel. Shareholders get forty-nine seventy-five a share, a fifty-two percent premium, the extra over the market price, compared with the price before the first sale rumors in June. Proofpoint's CEO, Sumit Dhawan, summed up the pitch: "As AI becomes embedded across the enterprise, the data it can access determines both its value and its risk." Notice that line. "AI is only as safe as the data it can touch" is becoming the core message of data security marketing.

Next, a stealth startup with a famous name attached. Calcalist reports that Ribon, founded by Idan Nurick, a co-founder of Paragon, the Israeli company that sells spyware to governments, has raised about two hundred million dollars at a valuation above one point five billion, while barely saying anything in public about what it does. It's just recruited Ehud Schneorson, Paragon's other co-founder and a former commander of Unit 8200. When a company hires a big name before it even launches, that hire is the marketing.

Now a cautionary tale about AI agents. Wired reports that an Accenture staffer connected a personal account of Claude Code, an Anthropic AI coding agent, to a secure Defense Department system used by several intelligence agencies. It reportedly ran there for about two months and appears to have sent information about the system, including login credentials, to a risky outside website. The system holds Controlled Unclassified Information, sensitive but not secret government data. The Pentagon says it doesn't comment on its networks but takes all concerns seriously, and Accenture disputes the allegations. For anyone marketing security, this is the new category in one story: "shadow AI," tools employees plug in without permission.

And a big consumer breach in Japan. Hackers stole about two point six million records from So-net and Plala, internet providers owned by Sony, including about fifty-four thousand email passwords.

Two marketing moves to steal ideas from. First, Liquid I.V., the hydration powder brand owned by Unilever, hired Jenny Danzi as CMO. She spent seventeen years at PepsiCo, most recently running marketing for Poppi, the prebiotic soda PepsiCo bought for almost two billion dollars. Liquid I.V. is now Unilever's biggest wellbeing brand, and it's chasing Gen Z through sports and music, like a McLaren Formula One campaign with drivers Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri. The lesson: brands that want culture hire people who've already built a cult brand.

Second, Sephora's holiday campaign stars Seth Rogen, not as a beauty expert, but as a clueless gift shopper getting help from a Sephora advisor. It's called "We All Sephora," made with the agency Gut New York. As a teaser, Sephora's store at The Grove in Los Angeles temporarily changed its sign to "Sethphora." The insight: a lot of holiday beauty shoppers are buying for someone else and feel lost, so the ad speaks to them.

## Business story

Now our business story, about two MIT students and the company that turned betting on the future into a forty billion dollar business. Tarek Mansour was born in California to Lebanese parents and grew up partly in Lebanon. Luana Lopes Lara was born in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, and spent eight years at a Bolshoi Ballet school in Brazil, then danced in "Swan Lake" in Austria before going to MIT to study computer science. They met there, interned at trading firms like Citadel and Bridgewater, and in twenty eighteen started Kalshi. The idea: a market where you can buy a contract that pays out if something happens. Will it rain in New York tomorrow? Will the Fed cut rates? Who wins the election? These are called prediction markets.

What made Kalshi different was regulation. In twenty twenty, it became the first platform approved by the CFTC, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal agency that oversees futures trading, to trade directly on real-world events. It then sued the CFTC to allow election bets and won in twenty twenty-four. Then came sports, and that's where it exploded. Sports are now about ninety percent of activity. Last December, Forbes named Lopes Lara, at twenty-nine, the world's youngest self-made woman billionaire, passing Taylor Swift.

Now this week. On Thursday, Kalshi announced it raised one billion dollars at a forty billion dollar valuation, co-led by Sequoia and Wellington. Five months ago it was worth twenty-two billion. Kalshi says it has more than thirty million accounts and over seventy-five percent of all prediction market trading, and it wants to become a full exchange that competes with CME Group and the owner of the New York Stock Exchange.

But one day earlier, Utah filed criminal charges against Kalshi and both founders personally: twelve felony counts, felonies being the most serious class of crimes, of promoting online gambling, plus other charges. Utah bans all gambling. An undercover state investigator opened an account and bet about a dozen times on NFL and baseball games. The state attorney general, Derek Brown, said, "Gambling is illegal in Utah, whether you place the bet, run the game, or promote it." Kalshi calls it "crazy overreach" and says only federal regulators should oversee it. And the CFTC agrees: it has sued at least ten states, Utah included, saying it alone has authority. The takeaway for you: Kalshi's growth came from a brilliant positioning move, calling itself a regulated exchange, not a sportsbook, a betting company. Now that label is exactly what's being tested in court. Whether prediction markets are finance or gambling is one of the biggest business fights of the year.

## Celebs, culture & fashion

Now celebs, culture and fashion, and sad news first. Sir Patrick Stewart, Captain Jean-Luc Picard on "Star Trek: The Next Generation" and Professor Charles Xavier in the "X-Men" movies, has died at eighty-six. His spokesperson said he died in the care of his wife, Sunny Ozell, after a "brief and poignant journey" with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, a brain disease that affects memory, movement and thinking. He started as a Shakespeare actor in England and spent sixteen years with the Royal Shakespeare Company before Hollywood. He was also due to return as Professor X in "Avengers: Doomsday" this December. The tributes were beautiful. ## Art & design

Thiebaud is best known for his candy-colored paintings of cakes and pies, so his portraits are rarer and more personal.

## Wellness

Your wellness idea today is about your coffee. A Tufts University study of more than thirty thousand American adults found that the calories people get from coffee rose about forty percent between two thousand three and twenty eighteen, from about forty-three to sixty calories a day, even though people weren't drinking more coffee. The biggest driver wasn't sugar. It was non-dairy creamer, which more than doubled, and which can be high in saturated fat. The study didn't measure health effects, and it relies on what people reported eating. But the practical move is easy: check the label on your creamer, and if it's mostly oil and sugar, try a splash of regular milk or an unsweetened oat milk instead.
