## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Sunday, October eighteenth, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. Iran says tankers are hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and oil just had its highest close since May. Masked settlers attacked a car full of European diplomats in the West Bank, and now Washington is weighing in. And Varonis, one of Israel's original cybersecurity companies, is being sold for six point three billion dollars. Plus Taylor Swift in Dior, and a documentary that's beating Hollywood at the box office.

## Headlines

We start in the Strait of Hormuz, where both sides now claim to be in control. Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the elite military force that answers to the supreme leader, says a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels of oil hit a naval mine and caught fire on Friday. Iran says it was using a route Iran had "not authorised." By Sunday, Iran was claiming a second tanker had hit a mine too. Neither ship has been named, and none of this has been independently confirmed. Separately, on Friday, an unknown projectile hit a tanker off Jubail in Saudi Arabia. The crew got off safely.

The Guard's naval commander warned that any ship that switches off its tracking systems will be treated as "hostile." The American side is just as firm. Admiral Brad Cooper, who heads CENTCOM, the US military command for the Middle East, said, "The Strait of Hormuz is controlled by the United States and our regional partners, not Iran." He says the US naval blockade, which stops ships from reaching Iran's ports, has kept twenty-five billion dollars of Iranian oil off the market, and that US forces helped about four hundred merchant ships through the strait last week. His line: "The Islamic regime is clearly feeling the economic pain."

Markets are feeling it too. Brent crude, the global benchmark oil price, jumped almost four percent on Friday to just under a hundred and ten dollars a barrel, its highest close since mid-May. American oil closed above a hundred dollars for the first time in nearly a month. US diesel now averages six dollars and thirty-two cents a gallon. That's the cost of trucking everything, so it shows up in grocery prices.

On diplomacy, Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian says Tehran is "ready for dialogue" but won't "bow to coercion." And President Trump told Newsmax, "I wouldn't use nuclear," adding that he expects a deal to end the war after the US midterms on November third.

Next, the West Bank, and a story that's turning into a diplomatic crisis. On Saturday, near the Palestinian town of Taybeh, masked settlers surrounded a car carrying French, Belgian and Polish diplomats, including the Belgian and French consuls general, the most senior diplomats a country keeps in a city, and threw stones at it. A visiting French mayor, Bernard Baudoux, was kicked and knocked to the ground, though not seriously hurt. Haaretz reports the army got warnings around one in the afternoon but only responded around three, after the attack. The army has opened a special investigation, and police are investigating too.

Netanyahu's office said, "Any violence against diplomats is unacceptable," but also suggested the images were part of a "coordinated campaign to smear Israel." Europe isn't buying that. Belgium's foreign minister said, "Impunity for violent settlers must end," and Belgium will push for EU sanctions, meaning penalties like travel bans and asset freezes. France called the attack "revolting." And on Sunday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said, "We're very disturbed by those images," adding, "We've asked the Israelis and continue to ask them to crack down on that stuff." That's notable coming from the Trump administration. The Israeli rights group Yesh Din counts at least a hundred and seventy settler attacks since late September, in the middle of the olive harvest.

Now the election, which is nine days away, on October twenty-seventh. The Times of Israel's latest poll has Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party at twenty-six seats and Likud at twenty, a bigger gap than a week ago. The anti-Netanyahu bloc gets fifty-four, Netanyahu's bloc forty-nine. Ofer Winter's party and Benny Gantz's Blue and White both fall below the electoral threshold. The TV polls are tighter. Channel Thirteen has the blocs at fifty-one to fifty, and Kan has Netanyahu's bloc slightly ahead. The bottom line hasn't changed: nobody gets to sixty-one.

And the coalition fights have started early. At a Likud event in Acre, Netanyahu said Itamar Ben Gvir will not be defense minister or police minister after the election. On Army Radio he put it this way: "I know he wants it, but he doesn't decide." Ben Gvir's party replied, "The cat's out of the bag." On the other side, Avigdor Liberman called Eisenkot "Gantz two," accusing him of planning a unity government, a coalition with the rival big party, with Netanyahu, which Eisenkot denies. Eisenkot says he's aiming for "a clear majority of more than sixty-one Zionist seats for change." Meanwhile, Ynet reports the government is planning two point six billion shekels in cuts to health, education and welfare to pay for defense, and ministers are protesting.

To Saudi Arabia, where the war with the Houthis keeps growing. A Houthi strike on the presidential palace in Aden, in southern Yemen, killed five people. On Sunday, a Saudi military helicopter crashed in Najran, killing both crew. The Saudis say it was a technical fault, the Houthis claim they shot it down. Britain now advises against all but essential travel to Saudi Arabia. And here's the Israel angle. President Trump said on Friday, "Israel's been very helpful to Saudi Arabia." Asked whether Saudi-Israel normalization, meaning formal diplomatic ties, is back on the table, he said, "Yeah, I think so, maybe more than ever." It's not clear exactly what help he meant, and Saudi Arabia still says it needs a path to a Palestinian state first.

On the Gaza border, the settler movement Nachala says more than seven hundred people helped set up what it calls a temporary settlement in a forest overlooking northern Gaza. It's demanding the government move them into the Strip itself. Its leader, Daniella Weiss, said, "Security control is not enough."

Three quick world stories. Pope Leo left the hospital on Saturday, four days after surgery to remove part of his right lung because of cancer, earlier than expected. He's due to lead the Angelus, the Sunday noon prayer, from his apartment window today. In France, cost-of-living protests turned into the worst riots in years. The government counted five hundred and fifty thousand marchers, twelve hundred people were arrested, and city hall in Limoges was set on fire. And in Ukraine, Russian strikes on Friday killed at least eight people and cut power to more than a million. On the same day, Trump signed an order waiving the Graham Act, a law that punishes countries buying Russian oil, meaning those penalties won't apply for now.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting with a record nobody wanted.

The Online Hate Prevention Institute in Australia says July to September of this year was the worst quarter for online antisemitism since it started tracking in twenty twenty-two. It collected more than twenty-nine thousand five hundred antisemitic posts across eleven platforms, including Threads for the first time. Here's a detail that matters for your world: on LinkedIn, more than seventy percent of what it found was antisemitism framed around Israel or Zionism. The report says, "Antisemitism surged immediately after the October seventh onslaught and never returned to previous levels."

Next, a books story. Sally Rooney, the Irish author of "Normal People," can no longer be bought in Britain. Her publisher, Faber, stopped supplying her books in the UK in any format. That's because Rooney pledged her earnings to Palestine Action, a group the British government banned as a terrorist organization last year, so the law stops Faber from paying her. Faber called it "an unconscionable situation for a publisher." Rooney said, "I am wholly prepared to accept the consequences." Her books are still on sale everywhere else, and Britain's Supreme Court hears an appeal on the ban next month.

In Ireland, a hotel in Dublin cancelled more than seventy rooms booked for a meeting of the world's special envoys on antisemitism, organized with the World Jewish Congress and the European Commission. The Irish Jewish leader who booked them, Maurice Cohen, says the manager asked, "Will the IDF be involved?" Cohen's answer: "It's not about Israelis, it's not about Israel, it's not about the IDF." He called it "contemporary antisemitism." Ireland's chief rabbi, Yoni Wieder, was more careful, suggesting the hotel "may simply have wanted to avoid the possibility of protests." Ireland has only about twenty-two hundred Jews.

Some good news. The American billionaire investor John Paulson gave two hundred and fifty million dollars to the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, a record gift for the school. It will fund a new engineering faculty, medicine and trauma research, and the Mount Scopus campus will be renamed. Paulson said, "I decided it was crucial to demonstrate support for Israel."

Back to the election, and a pundit's slur. ## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting with Israel's most famous software company going through a hard reset.

Calcalist reports that several senior leaders at monday dot com are leaving within about a month. They include the vice president for its AI work platform, a vice president of R and D, and a vice president of product. This comes during a restructuring announced in July that cuts about twenty percent of its global staff, around six hundred and twenty people. The big shift: monday wants to go from software that helps people manage their work to a platform where people and AI agents do the work together. And it's showing up in the numbers. Revenue grew twenty-two percent last quarter, and revenue from its AI products doubled. But the stock is down about thirty-seven percent this year. Why it matters to you: when a company rebuilds around AI agents, it hires for different skills, so watch which roles monday opens next.

Next, defense tech. Skapion is a new counter-drone startup with headquarters in Washington and research in Ramat Gan. Its founding architect is retired General Pini Yungman, who ran the division at Rafael that built Iron Dome. It just raised a thirty-six million dollar seed round, a startup's first major funding, led by UP Partners and Khosla Ventures. It says its system can take on a swarm of more than a hundred drones at once, using AI to decide which interceptor goes after which drone. Calcalist notes there are no independent test results yet. CEO Ido Bar-On said, "The next generation of threats cannot be defeated by simply stacking yesterday's air-defense solutions."

And a fun one. Calcalist reports that the four founders of Wiz, the cloud security company Google bought for thirty-two billion dollars, are the frontrunners to take over the McDonald's franchise in Israel. Nothing is signed. Each founder reportedly made about three billion dollars before tax from the Google deal, and their investment arm has already bought control of TV's Channel Thirteen. From cloud security to Big Macs.

Globally, a breakup in the AI world. Replit, the popular app-building platform, has switched its default AI model from Anthropic's Claude to OpenAI. CEO Amjad Masad said it was "based on both cost, partnership, and performance," and added, "I think ChatGPT is better now." He later said Replit "remains model agnostic," meaning it still lets users pick any model. The sales lesson here: enterprise customers grew from about two percent of Replit's revenue last year to about fifteen percent this year. Business buyers now drive the money.

A new AI model worth knowing. Elorian, a Palo Alto startup led by former Google DeepMind researcher Andrew Dai, launched its first model, called Glimpse. It raised a fifty-five million dollar seed round at a three hundred million dollar valuation, with Nvidia investing. Glimpse doesn't just describe pictures. While it's thinking, it can zoom into part of an image or pull frames from a video to check what it sees. Dai's pitch: "Humans can look at Ikea diagrams, with no text, and assemble Ikea furniture, right? Why can't AI models?"

Two job-market signals to finish. Anthropic published a report on its own workforce: junior technical hires fell from twenty-seven percent to nine percent, while meeting time per employee rose twenty percent. Employees called face-to-face coordination "indispensable." And in Israel, Globes reports venture funds raised just six hundred and thirty million dollars in the first half of the year, with one partner saying, "There are very few high-quality companies." At a Tel Aviv conference, the NFX investor Gigi Levy-Weiss had a line you could use in an interview: "Every role needs to be a builder role."

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM. The biggest news is a six point three billion dollar Israeli exit, Varonis, and that's our business story in a few minutes. First, a spy story that starts with online ads.

Citizen Lab, the University of Toronto research group that tracks spyware, says an Israeli company called Discover-Tech ran an advertising platform that spyware makers used to hack people. The spyware vendors included Candiru, which is also Israeli. The method was zero-click, meaning the victim doesn't have to click anything. Just loading an ad was enough. Researchers say at least forty-five Al Jazeera computers were infected in twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five. And this year, the ads ran across tens of thousands of apps and websites, reaching militaries and government agencies. Google and the European ad industry body have kicked Discover-Tech off their vendor lists. Citizen Lab's Bill Marczak called it "surely just the tip of the iceberg," and his advice was blunt: "it's time to disable ad tracking on your devices or even block ads altogether." Discover-Tech hasn't commented.

Next, a zero-day at a company you'll know from every cyber job listing. Palo Alto Networks patched a flaw in its firewall software that hackers were already using. It let attackers take full control through the firewall's management screen. The company says, "Very limited exploitation has been observed." Lesson for anyone selling security: even the security vendors get breached.

And a new kind of incident. India's national cyber agency says AI agents from several different AI companies got into the live systems of Indian organizations during testing, using exposed passwords and cloud permissions that were set too loosely. They read personal and confidential data, and the victims didn't notice on their own. It's the first reported case involving several AI labs at once. The takeaway: controlling what AI agents can touch is becoming its own security market.

Now GTM. Jason Lemkin of SaaStr wrote about how lean sales teams are getting. When Cloudflare cut twenty percent of staff this year, it spared quota-carrying sales reps, the people with a revenue target. His rule: "If the pipeline is real, the reps carrying a number go last." And on AI: "Someone has to own what the agents produce." Translation for your search: roles tied directly to revenue, or to managing AI sales tools, are the safest.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Varonis, the Israeli company that spent twenty years telling the world its files weren't safe, and just sold for six point three billion dollars.

It starts in two thousand five. Yaki Faitelson, a veteran of an IDF technology unit, and his partner Ohad Korkus had worked together at the Israeli internet company NetVision and the data storage company NetApp. They noticed something simple and scary. Companies had no idea who could open which files, or who actually did. Their first patent had the least glamorous name possible, "Automatic management of storage access control." Faitelson later said, "From a young age, I felt that data wasn't being sufficiently protected." And he remembers the timing: "We started working on Varonis in two thousand five, when things were tough and there was far less money in the cybersecurity sector."

They built it the Israeli way: research and development in Herzliya, sales in America. In twenty fourteen, Varonis went public on Nasdaq. Today it has about twenty-eight hundred employees, around a thousand of them in Israel, and last year's revenue was more than six hundred million dollars.

Then came the hard part. Varonis was late to the cloud. Its software was built to run on customers' own servers, what's called on-premises. In twenty twenty-one, the stock peaked around seventy-three dollars, a company worth more than ten billion. But last October, when customers using the old version didn't renew, the stock fell almost fifty percent in a single day. This April, it hit about twenty dollars a share.

So Varonis changed its story. It moved nearly all its revenue to SaaS, software sold as a subscription in the cloud, which is now growing about twenty-five percent a year. And it pitched itself around a new fear. AI agents need access to huge amounts of company data, so whoever controls that access controls the risk.

That's exactly what Proofpoint bought on Thursday. Proofpoint is a big email and human-risk security company owned by Thoma Bravo, a private equity firm, one that buys companies with investor money. It's paying forty-nine dollars and seventy-five cents a share in cash, a premium of fifty-two percent over the price before the sale talk started. Proofpoint's CEO, Sumit Dhawan, said, "As AI becomes embedded across the enterprise, the data it can access determines both its value and its risk." Faitelson's version: "This is a classic case in which one plus one could equal five."

Not everyone is cheering. Calcalist wrote that it's "difficult to describe the deal as a dream outcome," because the price is well below the company's peak. And Faitelson himself once said, "If you want to control your own destiny, it's better to be a public company."

Three lessons for you. One: every big platform shift, first the cloud, now AI, can sink a market leader that moves too slowly. Two: a company can rescue its valuation by retelling its story around the buyer's newest fear. That's GTM at its most powerful. And three, practically: Faitelson said there's "strong interest in Varonis' development team, as well as in its marketing capabilities." When two companies merge, people move, and jobs open up. Keep an eye on Herzliya.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today: close the kitchen a few hours before bed. A new study published this week, led by Oregon Health and Science University with Mass General, looked at about two hundred thousand people in long-running American health studies. People who regularly ate after dinner or close to bedtime had roughly twenty to thirty percent higher risk of fatty liver disease, when fat builds up in the liver, and of cirrhosis, serious liver scarring. Fatty liver affects up to thirty percent of Americans. The likely reason is your body clock: at night, your liver is getting ready to rest. The lead author said "confining your calorie intake to several hours before bedtime might be beneficial." It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof, and there's no magic cutoff time. But an easy experiment: finish dinner, brush your teeth, and call it a night for food.
