## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, November seventh, and this is your Morning Brief. Three days after his party lost Congress, Trump has pushed out his chief of staff, Susie Wiles, and handed the job to Stephen Miller, the architect of his immigration crackdown. The US carried out what Trump calls "minor" strikes on Iran, its first since the election, even as Qatar pushes a two-day plan to calm things down. And here at home, President Herzog names his pick for prime minister tomorrow, while Avigdor Liberman makes a surprising U-turn on the ultra-Orthodox. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in Washington, with the biggest shake-up of Trump's team since the midterms. On Friday Trump announced on Truth Social, his own social media platform, that Stephen Miller will become White House chief of staff on Monday. The chief of staff is the president's top aide, the person who runs the White House day to day and controls who and what reaches the president. Miller replaces Susie Wiles, who ran Trump's twenty twenty-four campaign and had been chief of staff since his first day back in office, the longest anyone has lasted in that job under Trump. A US official told CBS News she resigned on Thursday night. Trump said she "has done an outstanding job" and will move over to his political operation. Wiles wrote on X, "Today is my last day as White House Chief of Staff," calling it "the honor of a lifetime."

Why does this matter? Wiles was seen as a calm manager who kept the peace inside the White House. Miller is one of Trump's most loyal aides and the man behind his hardline deportation program. Voters on Tuesday broadly rejected Trump's agenda, and CBS describes this move as Trump doubling down rather than softening. Expect a more combative White House as Democrats take over both houses of Congress in January.

Now to Iran. On Friday, Trump said US forces hit an Iranian air defense system. Iranian state media reported two explosions on Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that a large share of the world's oil passes through. Trump called it "minor," saying "we knocked it out in about fifteen minutes, maybe five." These are the first US strikes since Tuesday's election, where anger over the war and high fuel prices hurt Republicans. Late Friday, US Central Command also offered a reward of up to twenty million dollars for information about Iran's nuclear program, through Rewards for Justice, the State Department's bounty program.

At the same time, diplomacy is very much alive. Qatar, the main go-between, has given Iran new proposals, and Trump says, "We are talking to Iran. We have been." The idea on the table is a so-called bridge deal of two days of trust-building. On day one, the US would lift its naval blockade of Iran's ports and release some frozen Iranian money, while Iran would let inspectors from the IAEA, the UN's nuclear watchdog, visit sites bombed by the US and Israel. On day two, Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz under an arrangement with Oman that Washington hasn't approved. Only after that would the real talks start, including on Iran's roughly four hundred forty kilograms of uranium enriched to sixty percent, a short technical step from weapons-grade, the purity needed for a bomb. Iran's side: it wants the blockade ended, sanctions lifted, compensation for war damage, and an end to fighting across the region, including Israel's operations in Lebanon, before it discusses its nuclear program. Trump told reporters this week, "You'll know soon enough" whether he orders a bigger round of strikes. Keep an eye on the Gulf this weekend.

There's also an Israeli twist. Channel 12, Israel's leading TV news channel, reported on Friday that Prime Minister Netanyahu and Mossad chief Roman Gofman planned an operation inside Iran on the eve of our October twenty-seventh election. According to the report, other senior security officials opposed it, fearing a big escalation that could even delay the vote, and the plan was stopped after an Israeli operative made a mistake that tipped off the Americans. The report cited no sources and is unconfirmed, and Netanyahu's office denied it, saying he made clear at the time that an attack before the election was not on the agenda.

Now to our own political drama. Tomorrow, Sunday, President Herzog announces who gets the mandate, the first chance to form a government. Gadi Eisenkot is the clear favorite, with sixty-seven lawmakers recommending him against forty-six for Netanyahu. But recommendations aren't a government. Eisenkot's own four parties hold only forty-eight of the Knesset's one hundred twenty seats. The Arab parties, Ra'am and the Joint List, have nineteen more, but several of his partners refuse to rely on them.

That's where Avigdor Liberman comes in. Liberman leads Yisrael Beytenu, a secular right-wing party with seven seats, and for years his whole brand has been fighting the ultra-Orthodox parties and their exemption from the draft. On Friday he reversed course. He said he's ready for "painful compromises" with the Haredi parties, even on the enlistment law, to form a government without Netanyahu. "It's painful for me," he admitted, but "the Netanyahu era must end." He still rejects a minority government backed by the Arab parties, warning it "will last much less time" and could bring Netanyahu back stronger.

The Haredi parties are United Torah Judaism, the seven-seat alliance of Ashkenazi, European-rooted, ultra-Orthodox Jews, which refused to recommend anyone this week, and Shas, the six-seat Sephardi ultra-Orthodox party, which backs Netanyahu. United Torah Judaism's Ya'akov Asher welcomed the "flexibility," but said no one has offered a deal yet and the rabbis will decide. Eisenkot, though, didn't budge. He repeated his four conditions for any partner, including mandatory service for all Israelis, military, national or civilian, and recognizing Hamas as a terrorist group. He called Israel's situation "a genuine, tangible and dangerous emergency" and said talks had made "significant progress."

What's next: once Herzog decides, the candidate has twenty-eight days, plus a possible fourteen-day extension. If every path fails, someone else can try, or Israel heads toward yet another election. One more thread: Times of Israel reports that both investigations into alleged foreign election interference involving Arab parties are falling apart, amid tension between the Shin Bet chief and the police.

A quick world story. In South Africa, the ANC, the African National Congress, Nelson Mandela's old party that has ruled since apartheid, the old system of white minority rule, ended, is heading for its worst result ever in local elections, at about thirty-two percent with eighty percent counted. That's down from forty-six percent in the last local vote. Voters are angry about failing water and power and years of alleged corruption. The Democratic Alliance, the main opposition, is second with about twenty-four percent and leads in Johannesburg, and former president Jacob Zuma's breakaway MK party is third. Final results come by Sunday, followed by messy coalition talks in the big cities.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the subway system is already writing its wish list for the new Congress. In a memo released Thursday, the MTA, which runs the subways and buses, asked the incoming Democratic majorities for steadier federal funding and an exemption from Trump's tariffs on rail and bus parts. The MTA estimates tariffs will add about one billion dollars to its twenty-three billion dollars of planned train and bus purchases. It also warns that, without a fix, federal funding could drop about two hundred sixty-five million dollars next year. A striking stat: New York gets seventeen percent of federal transit formula money but carries forty-three percent of America's transit trips. MTA chair Janno Lieber says he hopes the city will be treated "much, much better" now that Chuck Schumer is on track to lead the Senate and Hakeem Jeffries, a Brooklyn congressman, is poised to become House speaker.

For something lighter, the New York Comedy Festival runs through November fifteenth, with more than one hundred shows across the city, from Madison Square Garden and the Beacon to Town Hall and Kings Theatre. Dave Chappelle was set to open it at the Garden last night, and the lineup includes Marc Maron, Ilana Glazer and Jordan Klepper.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, starting with an ugly story in Philadelphia. A group of activists is planning a bike ride tomorrow around Rittenhouse Square to promote a boycott of local businesses they say support Israel, including restaurants owned by the celebrated chef Michael Solomonov. Jewish groups say it targets Jewish-owned businesses, and the date makes it worse: November eighth is the eve of the eighty-eighth anniversary of Kristallnacht, the Nazi pogrom of nineteen thirty-eight, when synagogues and Jewish shops across Germany were smashed and burned. Organizers call the timing a coincidence. Leaders across the political spectrum condemned the ride. Mayor Cherelle Parker said, "Singling out businesses because their owners are Jewish is unacceptable." Governor Josh Shapiro's office called it "blatant antisemitism." The head of the city's Board of Rabbis urged people not to confront the riders, and instead to go eat at the targeted restaurants.

On that anniversary, Times of Israel spoke with George Shefi, a ninety-four-year-old survivor who fled Berlin as a child, and who is urging German police to stand firm against hate. And a sad note from Australia: Eva Geller, a ninety-year-old Holocaust survivor wounded in last December's attack at Bondi Beach in Sydney, has died. Fifteen people were killed in that attack.

On American politics, a new poll for J Street, the liberal pro-Israel group, found seventy-two percent of Jewish voters backed Democrats for Congress on Tuesday, and seventy-six percent support sanctions on violent settlers.

And some joy: Deni Avdija, the Israeli NBA star, is on fire. His Portland Trail Blazers are six and one, the best record in the Western Conference, the NBA's western half, and Avdija leads the team at about twenty-three points a game.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech and AI, where the money keeps flowing even as markets wobble. Bloomberg reports OpenAI has raised eleven billion dollars so far in a round valuing it at one point two five trillion dollars. The round could reach about twenty billion, but that's well below what OpenAI was aiming for in September: thirty billion at a one point four trillion valuation. Bloomberg also reports that potential buyers in Anthropic's planned IPO are talking about a valuation as low as one point one trillion dollars, down from the roughly two trillion investors expected a month ago. After this week's tech selloff, and voters turning on data centers, the huge buildings full of servers that power AI, the giant numbers are getting a reality check.

A related number from the research world. METR, an independent group that evaluates AI, says Anthropic's AI progress is now running about twice as fast as before, mostly because Anthropic's own staff use AI to code and analyze experiments faster. Critics noted Anthropic redacted much of the hundred-page report.

On the product side, Anthropic launched a personal finance feature in Claude for US subscribers, letting people connect bank accounts with read-only access. And Thinking Machines Lab, the startup founded by former OpenAI technology chief Mira Murati, is reportedly raising five billion dollars at a forty billion dollar valuation.

And an Israeli deal detail: Intel now says its purchase of Classiq, the Israeli quantum computing software company, is worth about one point nine billion dollars, with three hundred fifty million of that paid only if Classiq hits performance targets. Quantum computing uses the strange physics of tiny particles to solve problems ordinary computers can't.

## Business story

Our business story is about a movie app you might have on your phone. The New York Times Company is in exclusive talks to buy Letterboxd for about three hundred thirty million dollars, according to the Times itself. Letterboxd is often described as Goodreads for movies: you log every film you watch, rate it, write short reviews, and follow friends. It's free, with a paid subscription for extra features.

Here's why the deal is interesting. Letterboxd went from three million users in January twenty twenty-one to twenty-eight million by April this year, and more than half are under thirty-five. That's exactly the audience every legacy media company wants. Film studios A24 and Sony were also interested, but A24 was reportedly priced out. Letterboxd is projected to earn about fifteen million dollars in revenue this year, so the Times would be paying more than twenty times that. The Times has done this before. It bought Wordle, the word game, and The Athletic, the sports site, turning its subscription into a bundle of news, games, recipes and sports. A passionate film community would fit right in. The lesson for a marketer: in an age of AI-generated everything, a real, engaged community of people who love something is one of the most valuable assets you can own.

## Pop culture, celebs and fashion

Now pop culture, and the week's biggest fashion news. Balenciaga has a new creative director: Martine Rose, the British-Jamaican designer, starting November sixteenth. It comes right after Pierpaolo Piccioli left the house after just sixteen months, which we told you was coming last month. Rose launched her own label in two thousand seven, built it on the London subcultures she grew up in, and has worked with Nike and Supreme. She also has history at Balenciaga, hired as a consultant when Demna arrived in twenty fifteen. Kering, the French luxury group that owns Balenciaga and Gucci, praised her "connection to the new generation." It's a clear swing back toward the street-culture energy of the Demna years, after Piccioli's more elegant couture approach. And the designer carousel isn't done: Saint Laurent, McQueen, Diesel, Alaïa and Calvin Klein all have open creative director seats.

## Art and design

Next, art, and the countdown to New York's biggest auction week of the year, starting November sixteenth. The headline sale is at Sotheby's on the eighteenth: twelve paintings from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, expected to bring around four hundred fifty million dollars. It's led by Van Gogh's "Chestnut Trees in Blossom," around one hundred eighty million, which could set a record for the artist, and a Cézanne "Harlequin" at around one hundred twenty million. At Christie's on the seventeenth, a Kandinsky abstraction, "Emphasized Corners," is estimated at sixty to eighty million dollars, and someone has already committed to bid above Kandinsky's auction record. A Klimt landscape carries the same estimate. Phillips has a Modigliani head that has never been at auction. This week will show just how strong the very top of the market really is.

And for interiors lovers: the New York designer Christopher John Rogers, known for bold color, made ten rugs with The Rug Company, priced from about two thousand to almost six thousand dollars.

## Travel

Now travel, with good news and bad news for flying out of Tel Aviv. The good news: El Al announced its biggest-ever summer schedule to the Americas, starting late March. That's sixty-three weekly flights and about thirty-five thousand seats a week, including thirty-six weekly flights to New York's JFK and Newark, with up to seven on some days, plus more to Miami and Los Angeles, and three a week to San Francisco. So getting home to New York next summer should be easier. If you're dreaming of France, La Compagnie, the all-business-class airline, will fly Newark to Nice year-round for the first time.

The bad news is heading east. Flydubai says it won't resume Israel flights until at least the end of November, and Israeli airlines haven't returned to the UAE, so Etihad is the only option to the UAE, at about one thousand one hundred eighty dollars round trip to Abu Dhabi in December. Globes found that December fares to Bangkok are up from last year, while Arkia's direct flight to Hanoi is actually a bit cheaper. One tip from the experts: a connecting flight via Zurich, Vienna or Munich can sometimes beat a direct one, but don't assume a stopover is always cheaper. Always compare.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with some news for cheese lovers. A small University of Toronto study of seventy-four adults with overweight or obesity found that eating three servings a day of full-fat dairy for twelve weeks did not worsen their weight, body fat, cholesterol or insulin resistance compared with a low-dairy diet, and the dairy groups even saw better blood pressure and got more calcium, protein and vitamin D. It was a randomized trial, meaning people were assigned to groups by chance, but it was short and was funded partly through a dairy research program, so it's not a license for unlimited brie. The practical takeaway: you don't need to fear a real yogurt or a slice of good cheese as part of a balanced day.
