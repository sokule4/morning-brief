Good morning Sophie, and welcome to your Morning Brief. It's Saturday, October tenth. Here are the three big ones. The Nobel Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay, the judge whose UN commission accused Israel of genocide, and within hours, America slapped sanctions on the International Criminal Court. The UAE has officially confirmed that the flydubai copilot planned a 9/11-style suicide attack on Ben Gurion Airport. And today marks exactly one year since the Gaza ceasefire began. Plus the Liberty's season ends by two points, Anthony Vaccarello walks out of Saint Laurent, and a company that started with forty thousand dollars now owns AOL, Vimeo and Evernote. Let's go.

## Headlines

We start in Oslo. The 2026 Nobel Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay, an eighty-five-year-old South African judge. Her story is remarkable. In 1967 she became the first woman to open a law practice in her province of South Africa. She defended Nelson Mandela and other anti-apartheid activists, became the first woman of color on South Africa's High Court, and later served at the Rwanda war crimes tribunal and at the ICC, the International Criminal Court, the world's war crimes court. The Nobel committee singled out her work in Rwanda, where her court established that rape and sexual violence can count as genocide. The committee's chairman said international law is "under tremendous pressure."

So why is this a big deal in Israel? Because Pillay also chaired the UN commission of inquiry into the Gaza war, which concluded last year that Israel committed genocide. Israel rejects that completely. The Foreign Ministry called the prize a "grotesque weaponization" of the award to legitimize anti-Israel hatred. Pillay dedicated the prize "to all of the survivors of international crimes," speaking from the courtroom in Nuremberg where Nazi leaders were tried.

Then came the response from Washington. Hours after the announcement, the US imposed sanctions, meaning financial penalties that cut off money and business, on the International Criminal Court itself. Not just individual judges this time, but the whole institution. That could force American banks, insurers and software companies to stop working with the court. Secretary of State Marco Rubio put it bluntly: "Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC." Netanyahu praised the move. The court's deputy prosecutor said the sanctions "must be condemned" and that its work will continue.

And President Trump, who has openly campaigned for this prize, was not happy. At a rally in Syracuse, New York, he called Pillay a "mystery person," said "I never heard of the person. Nobody did," and called the decision "an indelible stain on the country of Norway."

Next, the flydubai story, and this one is chilling. The UAE's attorney general, the country's top legal officer, has officially confirmed what Israeli media had been reporting. The Omani copilot, Hamam al-Hammami, planned to seize flydubai Flight 1073 on September thirtieth and crash it into a terminal at Ben Gurion Airport. He took two earlier flights to Tel Aviv to study the route and the airport, and he prepared by watching terror propaganda and taking boxing lessons. Over Saudi airspace he attacked the captain with a crash axe. The plane dropped sixteen thousand feet in thirty seconds before the badly wounded captain, two other pilots and several passengers overpowered him. The Emirati statement said none of the background checks revealed his extremist views. He's reportedly expected to face the death penalty, and Shin Bet officials, from Israel's internal security service, are in the UAE taking part in his interrogation.

One of the Israeli passengers who helped subdue him, Asaf Rajuan, told Channel Twelve he flagged two flight attendants to investigators because "something there smelled off." The UAE reportedly checked and found no connection. And in a sign of how tense this has gotten, the UAE has pulled landing permits for Israeli airlines.

Now Iran. The war is in its eighth month. The New York Times reports that the Pentagon has drawn up options to restart it, including "a short, intense campaign lasting about three days" against Iran's missiles, drones and energy sites. The report says Trump has vetoed major operations against Iran or the Houthis, the Iran-backed rebels who control much of Yemen, five times in recent months. On Friday, Trump said the war will "be over soon, one way or the other." He's also promised relief on fuel prices: he says Putin agreed to supply more than three hundred thousand tons of Russian diesel right away, and the US Treasury gave it a temporary license. President Zelensky called that "unworthy" and "weak."

Speaking of the Houthis, their war with Saudi Arabia is escalating fast. A Houthi ballistic missile hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on Thursday, killing three people, including a pilot for Saudia, the Saudi national airline. The Houthis are openly trying to shut down every Saudi airport, in what they call an "airport for airport" exchange for Saudi strikes on Yemen. The Saudi-led coalition says it has now destroyed a hundred and thirty-six Houthi military targets. And Pakistan and Turkey are sending troops to Saudi Arabia under a defense pact. Why it matters to you: this regional war keeps finding new fronts, and it's hitting oil and air travel all over the Gulf.

Now Gaza. The ceasefire took effect exactly one year ago today. The first phase brought the remaining hostages home, in exchange for hundreds of Palestinian prisoners. Since then, not much has moved. Hamas has not disarmed. It says it would store its weapons under a Palestinian technocratic government, meaning one run by non-political experts, but rejects the word "disarmament." Israel says it won't pull back until Hamas is fully disarmed, and its army now controls about two-thirds of Gaza, beyond the Yellow Line, the boundary between the Israeli-held and Hamas-held parts. The Board of Peace, the Trump-led body overseeing Gaza's future, unveiled a two point four five billion dollar first reconstruction plan, but the EU and UN estimate the real bill at more than seventy-one billion. More than a hundred aid groups released a statement saying the ceasefire "exists in name only," citing more than fourteen hundred Palestinians killed since it began, according to Gaza's Hamas-run health ministry. Israel's army says five of its soldiers have been killed in that time. And the UN warns that El Niño rains could flood the shelters of the nearly two million people who still need them.

Back in Israeli politics, seventeen days to the election, and a fight over hostage families. On his radio show this week, Yinon Magal, a right-wing TV host and Netanyahu supporter, called people who blamed Netanyahu for hostages' deaths "the most despicable people who live in the State of Israel," and read out the names of more than a hundred and sixty contributors to a book criticizing the prime minister. They included bereaved families and Ofri Bibas Levy, the sister of Yarden Bibas. She answered that the only people she'll ever apologize to are Shiri, Ariel and Kfir. Yair Lapid called Magal "a traitor from within." On Friday, Magal posted an apology video. Ofri Bibas called it a manipulation.

Also on the campaign trail: on Thursday, Haredi protesters, meaning ultra-Orthodox Jews, planned to block highways and access to Ben Gurion Airport over arrests of men avoiding the draft, meaning mandatory military service. Netanyahu reportedly asked the Haredi parties to call it off because of how it looks before an election. They told him the protests were organized by an extremist faction they don't control.

Quickly around the world. A massive earthquake, about magnitude seven point seven, hit Panama on Friday, felt across Costa Rica too. Buildings swayed in Panama City and offices were evacuated. A tsunami warning was issued, and early reports showed no casualties, but damage assessments were still coming in. And the Palestinian Authority is set to delay its parliamentary elections, which were planned for November and would have been the first in twenty years.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where an immigration arrest turned into a shooting.

On Thursday afternoon in Marble Hill, the neighborhood at the very top of Manhattan, agents from ICE, the US immigration enforcement agency, tried to arrest a twenty-eight-year-old man named Oscar Belgal in his car. His five-year-old son was in the back seat. One agent fired seven shots. Belgal was grazed below the ear, and his son wasn't hurt. The Department of Homeland Security says Belgal is undocumented, rammed two cars while trying to escape, and has prior arrests, and that agents didn't know a child was in the car. On Friday a federal judge blocked the government from deporting him. Mayor Mamdani called it "an unconscionable and incredibly serious situation." Governor Hochul called for a pause on all ICE actions in New York State. Protesters gathered outside the hospital, and there were clashes with police.

That shooting set off a political fight inside the mayor's own camp. The DSA, the Democratic Socialists of America, which Mamdani calls his political home, is now demanding that Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch end any NYPD help with ICE operations, and be fired if she can't. Seventeen democratic socialist officials and nominees signed a letter, and one incoming congresswoman wrote that "Commissioner Tisch must step down." They also want the Strategic Response Group, the NYPD's protest and riot unit, disbanded. Here's why this matters: Tisch is a self-described proud Zionist from a prominent Jewish family, and keeping her on was one of the biggest signals of reassurance Mamdani sent to Jewish and moderate New Yorkers. If she goes, that changes a lot.

Sports, and it's heartbreak. The Liberty's season is over. They lost Game Three to the Atlanta Dream at Barclays Center, eighty-five to eighty-three, and got swept out of the semifinals. Breanna Stewart and Jonquel Jones each scored twenty-six. Down two with nine seconds left, Stewart missed a contested layup, and DeWanna Bonner sealed it at the free throw line. Angel Reese led Atlanta with twenty-two. The Dream go to the WNBA Finals for the first time since 2013. So with the Yankees out too, it's a quiet fall for New York sports fans.

Now some good news for the skyline. The Chrysler Building has a new owner, and it's an old one. Tishman Speyer, which bought it back in 1997, is taking it over again on a hundred and fifty-year ground lease, meaning it rents the land, from Cooper Union, which has owned the land since 1902. They'll pour two hundred and thirty-five million dollars into it: restoring the facade, renewing that famous crown, and turning the sixty-first floor, the one with the eagle gargoyles, into a fancy lounge with food and drinks. The pitch is to make it Manhattan's "most distinctive new boutique office building."

And the race for governor. A new Marist poll has Governor Hochul leading Republican Bruce Blakeman fifty-three to forty-two percent, an eleven-point lead, less than a month before Election Day on November third. One more for your calendar: the Columbus Day Parade marches up Fifth Avenue on Monday.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting in France.

Jordan Bardella is the thirty-one-year-old star of France's far right, the most nationalist end of politics, and Marine Le Pen's top ally. If Le Pen wins the presidential election next spring, and she's leading the polls, he'd probably become prime minister. Last month, the investigative site Mediapart published what it says are his private Facebook messages from 2013, when he was a teenage activist, including antisemitic lines about Jews dominating other peoples. Bardella says they're fake and is suing for defamation, meaning damaging someone's reputation with false claims. On Friday, the newspaper Le Canard enchaîné reported that all the data from one of his old Facebook accounts was deleted between September twenty-eighth and October second, right after the story broke. Bardella called that "completely false" and promised another lawsuit. Here's why it matters: Le Pen has spent years trying to clean up her party's antisemitic past, which goes back to her own father, and Bardella was the face of that makeover. He even visited Israel last year at the invitation of an Israeli minister.

In London, two men accused of spying for Iran denied the charges at the Old Bailey, London's main criminal court, on Friday. Prosecutors say Nematollah Shahsavani tasked Alireza Farasati with watching Israeli and Jewish sites, including the Israeli embassy, the JW3 Jewish community center and Bevis Marks, Britain's oldest synagogue. Their trial is set for April. A reminder that Iranian threats to Jewish communities abroad are very real.

And that ties into this weekend. Israel's Diaspora Ministry counted more than two hundred and sixty anti-Israel protests planned in thirty-seven countries around the anniversary, peaking today and tomorrow, including a big march in London. The Foreign Ministry is urging Israelis abroad to stay alert and keep away from protests.

In American politics, a debate moment people are talking about. In Michigan's Senate race, the Democrat Abdul El-Sayed, who won his primary with criticism of Israel at the center of his campaign, was asked again and again which country is the bigger threat to America, Israel or Iran. He wouldn't answer, calling it a "ridiculous hypothetical" and saying Israel "has lobbied us to go to war with Iran." His Republican opponent, former Congressman Mike Rogers, jumped on it. Polls show El-Sayed slightly ahead. It's a race that says a lot about where the Democratic Party is heading on Israel.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting in Israel.

A story about money that's worth understanding before the election. Calcalist found that foreign investors have nearly stopped buying Israeli stocks. In the first nine months of this year they made net purchases of about two point seven billion shekels, down almost seventy percent from last year. And they've become net sellers of Israeli bank stocks, dumping four point nine billion shekels' worth. Banks are seen as a bet on the whole economy, so that's a signal. One banker who works with foreign funds said they expect "continued turmoil" if the current government wins, so they're cutting their exposure, at least for now. The interesting twist: foreign money is still flowing into Israeli tech stocks, more than four billion shekels. Investors are nervous about Israel, but not about Israeli tech.

Now globally, and the theme of the week is the giant pile of money flowing into AI. Nvidia, the company that makes the chips that power AI, hit a record market cap, the total value of all its shares, of five point six five trillion dollars. And SpaceX, which went public in June, is reportedly raising forty billion dollars in debt, mostly to buy Nvidia chips. Morgan Stanley estimates AI data centers will need one and a half trillion dollars in outside financing by 2028. Meanwhile SoftBank, the Japanese investment giant, is reportedly seeking up to a hundred billion dollars from Gulf investors for a new AI fund.

But there's a reality check too. Axios reported that OpenAI's yearly revenue pace is about fifty billion dollars, not the seventy billion that had been floating around. The difference reportedly comes from how sales through cloud partners are counted, and OpenAI expects to reach about seventy billion by the end of the year. And an AP-NORC poll found sixty-four percent of Americans think AI is developing too fast. Only eight percent think it's too slow.

Now a story with a real impact on people in tech. On Thursday, the US Labor Department suspended eight companies from the main program employers use to sponsor workers for a green card, America's permanent residency. The list: Microsoft and Adobe, plus six big outsourcing firms including Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services. Vice President Vance accused Microsoft of laying off about six thousand Americans last year while getting more than six thousand H-1B visas, the main US work visa for skilled foreigners, and the US attorney general, Todd Blanche, said criminal charges are possible. None of that has been proven. Microsoft said most of its US employees are Americans. If you know Israelis hoping to move to the US with a big tech company, this matters.

And one more on jobs, this time from Britain. The UK government confirmed plans to ban non-compete clauses, the contract rules that stop you from joining a competitor or starting your own company after you leave a job. The tech and AI startup world pushed hard for this. It's a reminder to always read that section of your offer letter.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM.

The big number of the week, and it's a great one for your job search. Global cybersecurity investment hit five point two six billion dollars in the third quarter, July through September. That's the strongest quarter since 2022, and seventy-eight percent higher than a year ago. And Israeli startups raised at least two point four billion of it, nearly half the entire world's total, across twenty-six rounds. The headliners: Island, which makes a secure web browser for companies, raised four hundred million at a six point four billion dollar valuation. Cyera, which protects company data, raised four hundred million from Goldman Sachs and is now worth more than twelve billion. Glow raised a hundred and eighty million and became a unicorn, a startup worth over a billion dollars. Zenity raised a hundred and twenty-five million, and Onyx Security a hundred and thirteen million. Above and Zafran also raised undisclosed amounts. About twenty of those twenty-six rounds were for companies securing AI agents or the systems behind them.

Here's the GTM takeaway. Every one of those rounds means a sales and marketing build-out, usually in the US, often run from Tel Aviv. So the companies to target right now are the ones that just raised. The caution: Calcalist questions whether the market can support so many startups doing very similar AI agent security, which means the winners will be the ones with the clearest story and the best sales teams. That's your skill set.

Now the threats. In South Korea, an update on the bank hacking crisis. Investigators found traces of an open-source Chinese tool, meaning free code anyone can use, called ARTEX in the attacks on at least seven financial firms, which exposed the data of about sixty-eight thousand people, including their incomes and loan limits. ARTEX is an AI agent that was built to help companies find holes in their own networks, and it plugs into existing AI models. Officials believe hackers turned it into a weapon. It even won a contest in China last month for the best AI cybersecurity agent. Since then, its creator has updated the rules to ban illegal use. The lesson: the same AI that defends you can be used to attack you.

At Pwn2Own Ireland, a famous hacking competition where researchers get paid to break products before criminals do, hackers used thirty-two zero-days on the first day alone. A zero-day is a flaw the maker doesn't know about yet. They broke into a Samsung Galaxy S26, smart speakers, printers, and even OpenAI's Codex coding agent with a single bug. Payouts topped a million dollars over the event.

And a busy patch week: Citrix urged customers to immediately fix a critical flaw in its NetScaler products, and Cisco patched about a dozen critical vulnerabilities. If you sell security, weeks like this are why customers pick up the phone.

A people move to know: Scott Simkin has joined the security startup Vega as CMO, chief marketing officer. When a young company hires a marketing chief, it's getting ready to scale its pipeline.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Bending Spoons, the Italian company that buys the apps you grew up with, and is now hunting for more.

It starts with a failure. In 2013, three friends, Luca Ferrari, Francesco Patarnello and Matteo Danieli, had just watched their startup, Evertale, fall apart. They had forty thousand dollars left. Instead of giving up, they used it to start a new company in Copenhagen, then moved it home to Milan a year later. They called it Bending Spoons.

At first they built their own apps, mostly photo and video tools. But their big idea came later. Instead of building new products from scratch, why not buy software that already has loyal users, and run it much more efficiently?

So they started shopping. Evernote, the note-taking app, for about two hundred million dollars. Then WeTransfer. Meetup. Vimeo, for one point three eight billion. AOL, yes, the "You've got mail" AOL, for around one and a half billion. Eventbrite. And this year, Airtable. This is called a roll-up, buying many companies and running them together.

The playbook is controversial. After many deals, Bending Spoons laid off most of the existing staff. At Evernote, everyone. At WeTransfer, about seventy-five percent. At Vimeo, most of the team. Critics say they hollow out beloved products and raise prices. Ferrari says they buy companies "with the intention of owning and operating them for the long term."

And the numbers back him up. Revenue went from six hundred and seventy-one million dollars in 2024 to one point three billion in 2025. In July, Bending Spoons went public on Nasdaq in an IPO, its first sale of shares on the stock market, valued at about eighteen billion dollars. The stock is up about thirty percent since.

Here's why it's in the news this week. Software companies that sell subscriptions, known as SaaS, for software as a service, have seen their values crash, because investors fear AI will replace a lot of their products. People are calling it the "SaaSpocalypse." In a Bloomberg interview on Friday, Ferrari said that's his opportunity. His words: "Valuations in SaaS were unreasonably high and had been for probably a decade." And he says AI now writes at least ninety percent of Bending Spoons' code. Last month, they agreed to buy Miro, the online whiteboard so many teams use, for one point three six billion dollars. In 2022, Miro was valued at seventeen and a half billion. That's about ninety percent less.

Two lessons for you. First, when markets panic, the people with cash and a clear playbook win. Second, if you're interviewing at a software company, ask how it's protecting itself from AI. Because somewhere in Milan, someone is waiting for its price to drop.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, starting back home.

Tomorrow, the Whitney Museum opens "Roy Lichtenstein: Like New," a huge survey, meaning a career-spanning show, of more than a hundred and thirty works. Lichtenstein is the Pop Art master, Pop Art being art that borrows from comics and ads, famous for those dotted comic-strip paintings like "Drowning Girl." The headline: for the first time since 1962, the show reunites all eight paintings from his debut at the Leo Castelli Gallery. It also recreates a nearly ninety-six-foot mural, and it's co-curated by the artist Alex Da Corte. The curators' argument is that Lichtenstein was asking questions about copying and images that feel very twenty twenty-six. A great weekend plan if you're ever in the city.

In Paris, the Fondation Louis Vuitton opened a show about a collector most people have never heard of: Gustave Fayet, a French winemaker, painter and designer. Between 1901 and 1909, he bought sixteen Van Gogh paintings, and ten of them are in the show, including the "Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear and Pipe." He also owned more than a hundred works by Gauguin. One painting he bought for two thousand francs was valued at a hundred times that about two decades later. Bernard Arnault calls him "one of the best kept secrets in the history of Modern art." For your agency, there's a lesson here: the collectors who buy early, before everyone agrees, are the ones history remembers.

And a charity sale to watch: Paul McCartney donated a lyric from "Band on the Run" for more than sixty artists to respond to. The works go up for an online auction from October twenty-seventh, with all the money going to War Child, which helps children in Sudan, Ukraine and Gaza.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today. If you ever end up on antibiotics, go easy on sugar while you're taking them. A study published in Nature last week, from Memorial Sloan Kettering and NYU Langone, followed a hundred and seventy-three hospital patients on antibiotics and tracked every meal they ate. Sweets were the only food linked to damage to the gut microbiome, the community of good bacteria in your gut. Every extra hundred grams of sugary food meant about a quarter less diversity, and harmful bacteria got a chance to take over. The researchers say it's not about cutting sugar forever. It's about skipping the sweets specifically while you're on the antibiotics.
