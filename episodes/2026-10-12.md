Good morning Sophie, and welcome to your Morning Brief. It's Monday, October twelfth. Here are the three big ones. Riyadh's airport was hit again on Sunday, the fourth strike in five days, and America's aviation regulator is now warning US airlines about flying over Saudi Arabia. ## Headlines

We start in Saudi Arabia, where the Houthi war keeps getting closer to ordinary travelers. On Sunday, a projectile hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh again, this time striking Terminals Three and Four, which handle domestic flights, and starting a fire. Saudi civil aviation said one person was killed, a Pakistani resident, and eleven were injured. According to CBS News, that's four strikes on the same airport in five days. The airport has been closed since Saturday, and the death toll from Saturday's attack is also rising: an Indian man who was wounded Saturday died on Sunday. India's foreign minister, S. Jaishankar, said he was "deeply grieved."

Here's the new part that matters for anyone who flies in this region. The FAA, the US government's aviation regulator, told American airlines to "exercise caution" in and over Saudi Arabia until December tenth. Its warning says the threats "could pose a potential risk to aircraft at all altitudes," including planes just flying over. And Riyadh's schools are going remote for a week.

The Houthis, as you know, aren't backing down. Their military spokesman, Yahya Saree, warned all airlines to stay away from Saudi airports, except the ones serving the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. And early this morning, the Saudi-led coalition said it shot down another Houthi missile aimed at the city of Khamis Mushait.

There was also a phone call worth noticing. Saudi Arabia's foreign minister, Prince Faisal bin Farhan, spoke with Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, on Sunday. Al Jazeera says it's the first publicly reported contact between them since this escalation began. Remember, the Houthis are backed by Iran, so Riyadh is talking to the sponsor, not just fighting the group it backs. So far, no US military action has been announced.

Speaking of Iran, the diplomacy is stuck. About two weeks ago, Iran sent Washington seven conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow Gulf waterway a fifth of the world's oil passes through. On Sunday, a top Iranian security official, Mohsen Rezaei, told state media that the American answer was "insufficient." He says the US accepted some conditions but not others, and until they're met, the strait stays closed to the US and there are no talks. Meanwhile, the attacks at sea are spreading. Early Monday, the British maritime agency that tracks Gulf shipping confirmed a drone hit a tanker near Sohar, in Oman. India says eleven Indians were on board, nine were rescued, and two are still missing. Nobody has officially claimed it.

All of this is landing in Israel too. On Sunday evening, Prime Minister Netanyahu held an urgent security meeting at the Kirya, the army's headquarters in Tel Aviv, with Defense Minister Israel Katz and the security chiefs, covering Iran, Gaza and Lebanon. And something you don't hear often: Netanyahu publicly condemned the "horrific terror attack" at the Riyadh airport and sent condolences to the victims' families. A rare public gesture from Israel toward Saudi Arabia.

Now, the election. Fifteen days to go, and the story of the weekend was Naftali Bennett. Speaking at an event hosted by a religious Zionist newspaper, Bennett suggested Netanyahu could even serve in a future government, if he accepted the other side's terms, including a state commission of inquiry, an independent investigation led by a judge, into October seventh. His words: "he's welcome to give me a call."

His own allies were not amused. Gadi Eisenkot said that "everyone who was in office" on October seventh is unfit to lead. Yair Golan said Netanyahu would get no job at all, not even a minister without a portfolio. And Avigdor Liberman urged the anti-Netanyahu parties to promise flatly that they'll never sit with him. Bennett then walked it back, saying he wasn't serious, and that he's "said a thousand times" that Netanyahu is personally responsible for October seventh and must leave public life.

Meanwhile, Likud is already planning for the day after. Ynet reports that Likud has set up a team to convince candidates from Eisenkot's party, Yashar, to switch sides after the vote, and it's combing through every name on Eisenkot's list for past statements and connections. Yashar's response: "There are no Silmans here, and there never will be." That's a reference to Idit Silman, the lawmaker who quit Bennett's coalition, his group of parties governing together, in 2022 and helped bring it down.

And the polls. Four new ones came out over the weekend, and they all say the same thing: nobody is close to sixty-one seats. On Channel Twelve, Yashar has twenty-three seats to Likud's twenty-one, and the anti-Netanyahu bloc leads fifty-four to forty-nine. On Kan, Israel's public broadcaster, it's fifty-two to fifty-one for Eisenkot's side. Channel Thirteen has it the other way, fifty-five for Netanyahu's bloc and fifty for Eisenkot's. And Maariv has a perfect tie, fifty-two each. The Arab parties sit between twelve and fifteen seats. One more detail: in Kan's poll, Ofer Winter's party, People of Israel, falls below the electoral threshold, the minimum share needed to win any seats, for the first time. Haaretz's headline sums up the trend: the Eisenkot bloc is slumping, and Netanyahu's coalition is gaining ground.

Also in Israeli politics, a courtroom moment. Avichai Mandelblit, the former attorney general, the government's top legal officer, who indicted Netanyahu seven years ago, testified at Netanyahu's corruption trial on Sunday, with Netanyahu sitting in the room. Netanyahu's lawyers called him as a witness, and he'll be on the stand until October twenty-second, five days before the election. Under questioning, he said police pressuring a key witness through someone close to him was "illegitimate and wrong." Justice Minister Yariv Levin said this proves the case "was stitched up from the outset" and called for the trial to be cancelled. Expect to hear that line a lot in the next two weeks.

In Gaza, the strikes go on in the ceasefire's second year. According to Palestinian media, an Israeli drone strike on a tent west of Khan Younis killed three people on Sunday. And in the West Bank, Mahmoud Abbas, president of the PA, the Palestinian Authority, which governs parts of the West Bank, postponed the Palestinian parliamentary elections that were set for next month, all the way to September 2027. The PLO, the umbrella Palestinian leadership body, said conditions were "not conducive" and blamed Israel for not letting election materials into Gaza. Palestinians last voted for parliament in 2006, twenty years ago.

Quickly around the world. In Ukraine, President Trump announced on Sunday, quote, "Effective immediately, there is an ENERGY CEASEFIRE," meaning both sides stop attacking power plants and oil sites. President Zelensky told Axios it "was news for me," and shortly after, Russia hit a power plant in Kharkiv. Overnight, a Russian strike near Kharkiv killed three people and injured twenty-five. Off the coast of Djibouti, in East Africa, a boat carrying about three hundred migrants toward Yemen capsized. The IOM, the UN's migration agency, says at least a hundred and thirty-nine people died, including fifteen children, and eighty-four are missing. Its head, Amy Pope, said, "No one gets into a boat like that unless they believe there is no other safe way."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where today is Columbus Day, and the mayor is skipping the party.

The eighty-second Columbus Day Parade marches up Fifth Avenue today, from Forty-Seventh to Seventy-Second Street, with more than thirty-five thousand marchers and up to a million people watching. This year's grand marshal, the person who leads the parade, is Ray Dalio, the billionaire founder of Bridgewater, the giant investment firm, who grew up in Jackson Heights, Queens.

But Mayor Mamdani won't be marching. City Hall says he'll host an Italian American heritage reception instead. Recent mayors have marched, so this is a big deal for the city's roughly two point two million New Yorkers with Italian roots. The New York Post reports he also turned down the Brooklyn parade in Bensonhurst, and Queens organizers say he never answered their invitation. Angelo Vivolo, who leads a coalition of more than fifty Italian American groups, said: "He's disrespectful to the Italian-American community." The New York Times found the other side too, with a headline saying some Italian Americans are glad he's staying away, because of Columbus's history. Back in 2020, Mamdani posted "Take it down" about the Columbus statue in Astoria. Public schools, by the way, call today "Italian Heritage and Indigenous Peoples' Day."

Now, the governor's race, with the one and only debate tomorrow night between Governor Kathy Hochul and Republican Bruce Blakeman. Here's what to listen for. On immigration, Blakeman attacks Hochul's new law limiting cooperation with ICE, the US immigration enforcement agency, saying, "I had to tell our police and sheriffs that they could no longer cooperate with ICE because of Kathy Hochul." Hochul calls his the "most pro-ICE ticket in America." Hochul's line: "I'll put my record up to anybody's, anytime."

Football, and what a Sunday. The Giants were down twenty-seven to thirteen in the fourth quarter in Washington, and Jameis Winston brought them all the way back, with two touchdown passes, the second to Malik Nabers with under two minutes left. Then overtime got chaotic. The Commanders' kicker missed his third field goal of the day, and in the final seconds the Giants sacked Jayden Daniels, and the clock simply ran out. Final score: twenty-seven, twenty-seven, a tie. Coach John Harbaugh: "It's a heck of a lot better than a loss, but it's not as good as a win." Winston threw for three hundred and twenty-six yards and three touchdowns, and Harbaugh said, "He was amazing." The Giants are now three, one and one, and still first in their division.

And the Jets finally won. They beat the Cleveland Browns seventeen to twelve at MetLife, ending a three-game losing streak. Running back Allen ran for a hundred and sixteen yards and a touchdown, and rookie David Bailey had three sacks. The Jets are two and three.

Something fun from the subway. The MTA held its yearly sale of old subway stuff in Brooklyn: station signs, old seats, MetroCards and maps. Three used subway seats went for a thousand dollars. And one famous shopper showed up: Spike Lee, who bought a Thirty-Fourth Street Penn Station sign. His explanation: "I'm a collector." The MTA's Paul Dvoskin put it perfectly: "There's nothing more New York than the subway system."

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting with a poll from back home.

A new poll of Jewish voters in New York City, by Honan Strategy Group and reported by the Forward, shows a clear split in how they see City Hall. Sixty-five percent of Jewish voters disapprove of how Mayor Mamdani is handling antisemitism, forty-seven percent strongly. But his police commissioner, Jessica Tisch, gets a seventy-five percent approval rating, with just six percent disapproving. Seventy-nine percent say antisemitism has gone up since October seventh. Among non-Jewish New Yorkers, the mayor's handling of antisemitism splits almost evenly, forty-six to forty-three. Tisch's message to the community: "we renew our pledge to Jewish institutions that they will not be left to protect themselves."

Next, Britain, where new government numbers are grim. The Home Office says antisemitic hate crimes in England and Wales rose ten percent in the year to March, to three thousand one hundred and sixty-two, the second-highest number ever recorded. Jews had the highest rate of religious hate crime by far, about nine times the rate against Muslims, per person. The CST, the charity that protects British Jewish communities, said it remains "at an unacceptably high level."

And that connects to a big British anniversary. Ninety years ago, in 1936, Jews, dockworkers and neighbors in London's East End blocked a march by Oswald Mosley's fascists. It's called the Battle of Cable Street, and it's a proud chapter of Jewish history. But this year's anniversary march was taken over by pro-Palestinian protesters chanting "Zionists are not welcome here," so Jewish descendants held their own separate march with hundreds of people. One of its leaders said, "We held a separate march because we felt unsafe to join them." A ninety-three-year-old man who was three at the original battle said, "the seeds of antisemitism have come out." A new Cable Street festival launched in East London last week, and the president of the Board of Deputies, the main body of British Jews, Phil Rosenberg, admitted the community's leaders back in 1936 were wrong to tell Jews to stay away.

In Boston, an experiment in Jewish education. A former Jewish nonprofit fundraiser named Leah Robbins is planning what she calls a "diasporist" Jewish day school, meaning a full-time Jewish school, built around Jewish life outside Israel and teaching Israel's history from a non-Zionist view. It's called Achvat Olam, "global solidarity," and the plan is to open in 2029 with eight to ten families in kindergarten. Robbins says, "There is no stemming the tide of the growth of the Jewish left." Historians quoted by JTA are skeptical, noting that the people who usually fund day schools are "very different" from the families she's trying to attract.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI. The biggest Israeli tech story of the day, Amazon and Decart, gets the full business story later, so let's start with jobs.

One Zero, Israel's first digital bank, is laying off about twenty-five people, around seven percent of its staff, and leaving about ten open jobs unfilled. It's the bank's third round of cuts since 2024, and it's openly calling this an "AI-driven efficiency" push. The business is actually growing: revenue rose thirty-three percent in the first half of the year, and it passed two hundred thousand customers. CEO Eyal Gafni told staff the bank must keep growing "without expanding our workforce or cost base." That's the new playbook, Sophie: growth without hiring. When you interview, it's worth asking how a company is using AI to change team sizes.

Next, a tax story that affects anyone holding startup equity in Israel. Calcalist reports that Israel's Finance Ministry is preparing to recommend a full exemption from capital gains tax, the tax on profit when you sell an asset, for tech founders. It's modeled on an American rule, and the goal is to stop founders from moving to the US or setting up their companies there. But there's a separate fight: the Tax Authority wants to raise the tax on employee stock options, the right to buy company shares at a set price, from twenty-five percent to thirty percent. Nothing is decided yet. Finance Minister Smotrich said back in July, "I believe it is a mistake to load taxes onto the high-tech sector." If you're weighing a job offer with options, keep an eye on this.

Now globally. A startup called TypeSafe AI just hit a valuation of seven and a half billion dollars, weeks after its product launched. It raised eight hundred and seventy million dollars, led by Andreessen Horowitz. The product, called Jev, is an AI model that doesn't write text at all. Instead, it outputs probabilities, basically calibrated yes-or-no decisions, for business automation. Co-founder Diogo Almeida, who used to be at OpenAI, told TechCrunch: "We have been super good at human language for four years, but it's not useful for automation." So investors are now betting on AI that makes business decisions, not just chatbots, and that's a new category someone will need to sell.

Then there's Manus, the AI agent company that has had a dramatic year. Manus started in Beijing and was bought by Meta in December for more than two billion dollars. Then China stepped in and ordered the deal undone. And last week, it raised more than five hundred million dollars, led by Chinese investors Boyu Capital and IDG, and it's reportedly aiming for a valuation of around four billion dollars, double what Meta paid. It's a reminder that in AI, geopolitics can kill a deal as easily as a bad product.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM, starting with an Israeli hire that tells you something about selling.

Cytactic, an Israeli startup that helps companies manage the business side of a cyberattack, hired Tim Brown as its Chief Strategy Officer. Brown is famous in the industry: he was the CISO, the chief information security officer, the executive in charge of a company's security, at SolarWinds during the SUNBURST attack in 2020, one of the biggest hacks in history, when Russian hackers hid inside SolarWinds software used by US government agencies. Since then he's been at Team8, the Israeli cyber startup creator. His pitch: "A major cyber attack isn't just a SecOps incident; it is a business crisis." Cytactic's CEO, Nimrod Kozlovski, said Brown will help "bring CIRM to more enterprises," that's cyber incident response management. Here's the GTM lesson: security buyers trust other security leaders. Hiring a famous former CISO is basically hiring a door-opener to every other CISO.

Now my favorite story of the week for a salesperson. The Record, a cyber news site, got leaked chat logs from a ransomware gang, hackers who lock your files for ransom, called the Silent Ransom Group, also known as Luna Moth. And they run extortion like a sales team. Victims, mostly American law firms, moved through pipeline stages labeled "chat," "offer," "contract," and finally "gold." Contract values ran from half a million to six million dollars. When one victim opened with a hundred thousand dollar offer, the gang mocked it as "missing a zero." They even sent people into offices pretending to be IT staff, with one message saying, "We buy these insulated bags and uniforms for agents." The takeaway: criminals use CRM-style pipelines, the systems sales teams use to track deals, too. And law firms are a hot vertical for security vendors right now.

Next, a government takedown. Seven countries, including the US, Britain, Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand and Spain, disrupted two hacking tools built by a Beijing company linked to a Chinese state hacking group called Flax Typhoon. One was a scanner for finding weak spots in systems, with more than thirteen hundred attack scripts. The other was a spear-phishing platform, meaning targeted fake emails aimed at specific people, which hit at least twenty universities in Taiwan. Victims also included an American power company and airports in Japan and Poland. The US seized six websites. When governments call out state hackers like this, companies in critical infrastructure tend to buy more threat intelligence, research on who's attacking and how, and email security.

A breach in health tech. iRhythm, the company that makes those wearable heart-monitor patches, says at least three hundred and sixty thousand people had data stolen, including names, dates of birth and insurance numbers. The attackers got in through social engineering, tricking a person instead of hacking a computer, targeting business apps hosted by outside vendors, and then demanded payment to keep quiet. The company says its medical devices weren't affected. Notice the pattern, Sophie: just like the EY breach we talked about yesterday, the weak spot was a third-party app and a human being. That's the pitch for every company selling security for logins and cloud software out there.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Decart, the AI startup founded by young Israelis that went from zero to a reported seven billion dollar offer from Amazon in under three years.

Let's start with the founders. Decart was founded in late 2023 by Israeli engineers including Dean Leitersdorf, now twenty-seven, and Moshe Shalev, who met in Unit Eighty-Two Hundred, the Israeli army's elite intelligence tech unit. Leitersdorf was billed by the Technion as its youngest ever PhD; he finished all three of his degrees in just eleven semesters.

Their first big moment was a viral demo called Oasis: a playable video game world that AI generated in real time, as you played it. No game engine, no designers drawing levels, just AI imagining the next frame. That kind of AI is called a world model, AI that simulates a realistic, interactive environment. And it got the attention of serious investors. In May, Decart raised three hundred million dollars at a valuation of about four billion, led by Radical Ventures, with Nvidia joining in. In total it has raised more than four hundred million, from names like Sequoia and Benchmark.

But here's the clever part, and it's a GTM lesson. Decart doesn't just make flashy demos. It also built a product called DOS, which helps companies move their AI models between different chips, Nvidia's, Amazon's, Google's, in weeks instead of months. That matters enormously, because AI chips are the most expensive thing in tech, and every big cloud company wants customers to be able to switch to its own chips instead of Nvidia's.

Now the twist. According to reports, Nvidia was interested first. Then, Bloomberg says, Anthropic explored a deal worth around six billion dollars in August, but walked away in September after doing its due diligence, the deep check of a company's books and technology before buying. Many startups would panic after losing a buyer like that. Instead, Amazon came in. The Wall Street Journal reports Amazon is now in talks to buy Decart for about seven billion dollars, and that a deal could happen soon. Amazon's cloud engineers had reportedly been so impressed by the founders that they gave Decart early access to Amazon's newest AI chip. Neither company has commented, and nothing is signed. If it happens, it would be one of the biggest acquisitions Amazon has ever made.

Three lessons for you. One: a lost deal doesn't have to lower your price. Decart walked away from one buyer and ended up with a higher offer from another. Two: the flashy product gets you attention, but the boring, useful product, DOS, is what makes a giant like Amazon pay. And three: when you're looking at Israeli startups, watch for the ones solving a problem that the biggest companies in the world urgently need solved. That's where the money, and the jobs, go.

## Art and design

Next, art and design, starting with the November auctions in New York.

Sotheby's has won a collection worth more than a hundred million dollars from Ted and Ruth Baum. It includes a 1967 Mark Rothko, the couple's first contemporary art purchase back in 1983, estimated at ten to fifteen million dollars, an Ed Ruscha painting at the same estimate, and Andy Warhol's version of "The Scream," after Munch, at four to six million. The evening sale, the prestige auction night where the biggest works sell, is November sixteenth. The whole collection started with a small sketch Ruth bought at a charity auction in the late seventies. Her philosophy: "you love things that you don't get easily."

In London, on Thursday, Sotheby's is selling a rare self-portrait by David Bowie. Yes, Bowie painted seriously. It's from 1996, on paper, and includes two of his own handprints. He gave it to his gallery owner in Saint Ives, and it's never been sold before. The estimate is thirty-five to fifty-five thousand pounds, which feels low for a Bowie fan. Sotheby's calls it "one of the most striking and personal self-portraits by David Bowie ever to appear at auction."

And the best story of the week: a painting found in a dark hallway in Michigan by someone who was shopping for vinyl records, bought for about thirty dollars, just sold for one point three five million dollars at Freeman's in New York. It's "Alderman Merriam's Shells," from 1951, by the Chicago surrealist Gertrude Abercrombie. The estimate was a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty thousand, and it smashed that, setting an artist record, the highest price ever paid for her work.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today. As the days get shorter, a lot of people start taking vitamin D. If you do, check the label for D3, not D2. A University of Surrey analysis of randomized trials, newly reported this week, found that D3 raises and holds vitamin D levels better, and people taking D2 actually ended up with lower D3 levels than people taking nothing. The lead researcher, Emily Brown, said D3 supplements "may be more beneficial for most individuals." If you're vegan, there's D3 made from lichen. And if a doctor put you on D2 for a specific reason, ask before switching.
