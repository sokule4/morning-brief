## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, this is your Cyber Brief. It was a quiet weekend for new deals, so today we zoom out. First, Palo Alto Networks' talks to buy the Israeli startup Clutch are reportedly advanced but not signed, and they sit inside a much bigger shopping spree for AI agent security. Second, Forrester, the research firm, has a warning for every marketer in tech: AI agents will soon shape most business buying, and marketing teams may shrink.

## Funding and acquisitions

Let's start with the deal everyone in Tel Aviv is watching. On Friday, Security Boulevard reported that Palo Alto Networks is in advanced talks to buy Clutch Security for about five hundred million dollars, but stressed that no binding agreement has been signed and neither company has confirmed. As a reminder, Clutch protects the logins that AI agents use, the keys and tokens that let software act inside a company's systems. It has raised only about twenty-eight and a half million dollars and is nearing ten million in annual recurring revenue, the yearly subscription income a software company can count on. Security Boulevard says Clutch would plug into Palo Alto's AI security product, Prisma AIRS, and its identity business.

What's new is the context. Put the recent deals side by side, and you see a clear pattern of consolidation, big companies buying up small ones in the same hot space. In September, Cyera, the Israeli data security company, bought Oasis Security, an Israeli startup that manages what AI agents can access, for about one billion dollars. Earlier this year, Cisco bought Astrix Security, another Israeli company in the same niche, in a deal Security Boulevard puts at roughly four hundred million. Okta, SailPoint and CrowdStrike have each bought smaller identity and AI security startups. Palo Alto itself has been on a spree: CyberArk for twenty-five billion dollars, Chronosphere, a monitoring platform, for about two point nine five billion, Console for five hundred million, and Koi Security for two hundred thirty-one million. And Upwind, the Israeli cloud security company, reportedly turned down an offer from Datadog that valued it at forty times its annual recurring revenue.

Why this matters to you: nearly all of these targets are Israeli, and nearly all are young. Big buyers are paying for being early and clearly positioned in one specific problem, AI agents' identities, not for years of revenue. For a marketer, that's the job description in one line: help a small company become the obvious name in a category before a giant comes shopping.

One money-behind-the-money item. Flashpoint Capital, an investment firm with offices in London and Tel Aviv, closed a one hundred sixteen million dollar fund for B2B, business-to-business, AI companies built by European and Israeli founders scaling into the US. It has already backed ten companies and plans fifteen to twenty in total, and it also announced a new CEO, Denis Mosolov. It's not a cyber fund, but more money for Israeli B2B companies heading to America means more go-to-market hiring, the teams that bring a product to customers.

## People moves

Now people moves, and today's theme is strategy chiefs, not just salespeople.

First, Hydden, a New York identity security company. It builds what it calls an identity system of record, one place that tracks every human, machine and AI agent that can log into a company's systems, and it says Fortune five hundred companies, America's largest corporations, in finance, telecom, manufacturing and healthcare use it. Hydden named Charles Tango its chief strategy officer, the executive who sets long-term direction, here including the product roadmap and technology partnerships. Tango has been a CISO, a chief information security officer, the person in charge of a company's security, three times: most recently at Sysco, the food distribution giant, and before that at Altria, the tobacco company, and Sterling National Bank. Hydden's CEO, Jai Dargan, said Tango "brings a perspective that's difficult to find in the cybersecurity industry." Translation: when you sell to security chiefs, having a former security chief help shape the product and the story is a credibility weapon.

Second, an Israeli company. Cellebrite, the Petah Tikva-founded, Nasdaq-listed company whose AI-powered tools help police and other investigators legally extract and analyze evidence from phones and computers, named Rogerio Rizzi executive vice president of corporate strategy and development. Corporate development usually means acquisitions and partnerships. Rizzi spent six years at Okta, the identity security giant, where he set up its corporate strategy team and its growth plan to reach three billion dollars in annual recurring revenue. Before that he led strategy at SAP and Hewlett Packard Enterprise. Cellebrite serves more than seven thousand law enforcement, defense and enterprise organizations. CEO Shiv Ramji highlighted Rizzi's "deep experience in AI, technology and M and A," mergers and acquisitions. When a company hires someone like this, it's often preparing to buy other companies.

A few more from SecurityWeek's weekly list. Keyfactor, which manages the digital certificates that prove websites and devices are who they claim to be, named Gerald Beuchelt as CISO. Presidio, the IT services company, named Doran Stienike as chief information officer. And MIT named Jeremy Rosenberg as its CISO.

No new marketing chiefs at cyber companies crossed the wires since our last episode. We'll keep watching, especially at Torq, after its CMO left for Abnormal AI.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and Forrester's new predictions for business-to-business go-to-market are worth knowing almost by heart for interviews.

Number one: Forrester expects AI agents to influence three-quarters of business purchase decisions by twenty twenty-seven, from finding suppliers to gathering information to analyzing contract renewals. But it thinks fully automatic buying will stay limited to simple, low-risk, repeat purchases.

Number two, and this is the surprising part: buyers will actually double their human interactions, talking more with analysts, references and partners to double-check what the AI told them. So the human side of marketing, customer stories, analyst relations, meaning working with research firms like Forrester, communities and events, gets more important, not less.

Number three: two-thirds of vendors will lose deals because buyers can't verify their claims. That's a direct challenge to vague AI marketing. If you say your product stops ninety-nine percent of attacks, you'd better have proof a buyer, or a buyer's AI agent, can check.

Number four, the uncomfortable one: Forrester expects many CMOs, chief marketing officers, to cut programs or accept less capacity, and some marketing leaders may remove up to a quarter of their roles, because AI productivity gains may disappoint. Forrester's chief research officer, Sharyn Leaver, said the defining challenge is to "make deliberate choices about what to automate."

Put that together with today's people moves and you get a clear picture. Hydden hiring a former CISO and Terra Security hiring a trust-focused CMO last week are the same bet: in a market flooded with AI claims, credibility is the product. For your own positioning, the strongest pitch right now is that you're a marketer who builds proof, through case studies, analyst relationships, customer voices and content that technical buyers believe, and who uses AI to move faster, not to make noise.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, the Israeli AI agent security startups that aren't being bought, yet. If the Clutch deal closes, rivals like Zenity, Onyx and Noma will be under pressure to sharpen their positioning fast, and acquirers' interest means investors will push them to grow. That's when companies invest in product marketing and brand.

Second, Hydden. A company selling to Fortune five hundred security teams that's adding senior strategy talent is likely sharpening its story and partnerships, which usually means more product marketing and partner marketing work to follow.

Third, Cellebrite. It's a public company with deep Israeli roots, and a new strategy and acquisitions chief can mean deals ahead. After an acquisition, someone has to merge brands, messaging and launch plans, a classic job for a senior marketer.
