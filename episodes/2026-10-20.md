## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Tuesday, October twentieth, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. Benny Gantz has pulled out of Israel's election, eight days before the vote. Egypt's parliament has delayed its vote on defending Saudi Arabia against the Houthis, while Russia's foreign minister is in Tehran trying to cool things down. And Amnon Shashua, the man behind Mobileye, has launched a new AI company with two hundred million dollars. Plus the Knicks raise their championship banner tonight, and fashion's exit season claims two more designers.

## Headlines

We start with the election, and the end of a political career, at least for now. On Monday night, Benny Gantz announced that his Blue and White party is leaving the race. He took the blame himself. "Unfortunately, at this time, I have not been able to convince enough of the people," he said, "and the responsibility is mine." He warned the country, "Do not take us back to October sixth," meaning the divided, distracted Israel of the day before the Hamas attack. He said he still wants a broad unity government, rival parties governing together, but he did not endorse anyone.

Why now? For months, polls had Gantz well below the electoral threshold. The Times of Israel's latest poll had Blue and White at just one point two percent. His name stays on the ballot, but any vote for it is now a wasted vote, meaning it won't turn into a seat for anyone. It's a long fall. Right after October seventh, Gantz was leading the polls. Then his allies drifted away, one by one.

The reactions tell you where the race is going. Gadi Eisenkot, who leads Yashar, the biggest opposition party in the polls, called it a "right and responsible decision." Yair Golan of the Democrats said everyone's duty now is to make sure the anti-Netanyahu "change bloc" wins. On the other side, Gideon Sa'ar told small right-wing parties near the threshold to follow Gantz's example and quit. Smotrich's Religious Zionism party went after Ofer Winter's People of Israel party, calling it irresponsible for staying in. And there's a report that Netanyahu himself is pushing a small ultra-Orthodox party, Charedi Public, to drop out. The logic is the same on both sides: every party that falls below three point two five percent throws away tens of thousands of votes. In a race this close, that can decide who forms the coalition.

Netanyahu is also leaning on security. On Monday he visited an air force base and told Israel's enemies, "Do not test us." He said that in a call with Trump, the two agreed "we have a job to finish." Army chief Eyal Zamir has put the military on heightened alert for election week, and cancelled soldiers' leave. Eisenkot and Yair Lapid both attacked Netanyahu's new security doctrine as campaign talk.

Now the West Bank, where there's finally an arrest story. Police, working with the Shin Bet and the army, detained ten suspects on Monday, in an operation called Olive Leaf. They're suspected of the attacks on Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine last Friday and on the European diplomats near Taybeh on Saturday. Police also raided outposts, small unauthorized settlements, near Ofra and Shiloh. Police Commissioner Danny Levy said, "the law in Judea and Samaria applies to everyone." It's a rare move, and it came after days of pressure from the EU and Washington. But the attacks haven't stopped. Overnight, settlers were reported setting fire to a car in Beit Ummar, and to a shed in the South Hebron Hills.

To Iran. Russia's foreign minister, Sergei Lavrov, arrived in Tehran on Monday, his first visit in eight months. Moscow says it's willing to mediate between Washington and Tehran. There's no outcome yet. Iran's position hasn't moved: its seven-day plan, a ceasefire and a gradual reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, is still on the table. And Iran's brand-new defense chief, Mehrdad Akhlaghi, just approved by parliament, warned the US of "enormous damage" if attacks resume. October has been the worst month yet for attacks on ships around the strait. Brent crude hit a five-month high of a hundred and ten dollars on Friday, and was around a hundred and eight on Monday. In America, regular gas now averages four dollars and thirty cents a gallon. Before the war, it was two ninety-eight.

Next, Egypt, which is taking its time. We told you yesterday that Egypt's parliament was expected to vote today on letting President Sisi send forces to defend Saudi Arabia. That vote has now been pushed to next Monday, October twenty-sixth. Egypt's constitution needs a two-thirds majority, meaning two out of every three lawmakers, for combat missions abroad. One small opposition party, the Social Democrats, told its members to abstain, saying parliament hasn't been told the mission's goals, length, cost or risks. Sisi insists the focus is defense only. Money is part of this story too. Egypt says the Suez Canal has lost about eleven billion dollars since the Houthis started attacking ships in twenty twenty-three. Meanwhile the fighting goes on. Saudi strikes on the Houthis reportedly killed nine people, as the two sides trade fire.

Today, a Lebanese military delegation sits down with Israel in Tampa, Florida, in talks backed by the US. Lebanon says it will press for a halt to Israeli attacks. Israel wants proof that Hezbollah, the Iran-backed militia in Lebanon, is really being disarmed. And in Gaza, the World Food Programme says it will stop monthly food aid to five hundred thousand people because it's run out of funding. That leaves about one point one million still getting help.

A few from around the world. Trump is defending his decision to waive a long list of sanctions on Russia, meaning penalties that block trade and money, days before they were due to kick in. He said he wants to keep Moscow at the negotiating table, and wrote that Zelensky "doesn't want to end this war, and probably never did." Zelensky answered that the waivers "will hurt Ukraine" and make Putin's war machine stronger. In France, protests that started with students and grew into fury over prices and fuel have turned into riots. Estimates put the crowds between five hundred and fifty thousand and eight hundred thousand people. Thousands have been arrested. President Macron condemned what he called "unbridled" violence. And in Kenya, health officials confirmed an eighth Ebola case, spread from Congo, where the outbreak has passed nine thousand four hundred cases, the worst in that country's history.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where the big night is tonight.

The Knicks open their title defense at Madison Square Garden against the Philadelphia seventy-sixers. Before tip-off, the players get their championship rings and the banner goes up into the rafters. The Jets lost in New England, twenty-six to nineteen, and they're now two and four. And guess who plays who next week? The Jets and the Giants, against each other.

On to politics, and an awkward moment in the governor's race. On Monday, Republican Bruce Blakeman attacked Governor Hochul for not acting on a bill that boosts tax credits for volunteer firefighters and EMTs. One problem: she had already signed it, on Saturday, at a fire department in Hempstead, which is in Blakeman's own Nassau County. The law raises the credit from two hundred to eight hundred dollars. Blakeman still called her "the most pro-criminal governor in America." Hochul's campaign answered with one dry line: "Wow, what a slam. This legislation sounds like a great idea." Hochul also visited Kiryas Joel on Monday, the mostly Hasidic village upstate, home to the Satmar community, a large ultra-Orthodox group, to meet voters there.

Now the dates you need. Early voting, in-person voting before Election Day, starts this Saturday, October twenty-fourth, and runs through Sunday, November first. Voting groups say the biggest threat isn't violence, it's confusion. Susan Lerner of Common Cause New York said, "We are seeing a lot of misinformation and disinformation coming from the federal government designed to confuse voters." The League of Women Voters had a simple reminder: "Accurate results take time."

Up at Columbia, students found a loophole. The Morningside Heights campus has been closed to the public for nearly three years, since the protests that began in October twenty twenty-three. Each student can create five guest passes with QR codes. So on Sunday evening, students set up what they called "The People's Kiosk" outside the gates at a hundred and sixteenth and Broadway, with cardboard signs reading "It's Fun Inside," and handed out passes so families could come in for a night market and performances. Mayor Mamdani has called for public access to come back, and has threatened legal action.

And the City Council is set to vote this Thursday on ending Central Park's horse carriages by June twenty twenty-eight. Mamdani has already said, "The horse carriage industry is coming to an end in New York City." We'll tell you how the vote goes.

## Jewish world

Next, the Jewish world, starting in Paris.

During the riots in France on Saturday night, a temporary prayer space used by the Ohalei Yaakov congregation, in Paris's nineteenth district, was broken into. Their regular synagogue is being renovated. Reports say prayer books and tefillin, the small leather boxes with prayers inside that Jewish men wear during weekday prayers, were thrown on the floor, and the rabbi's office was ransacked. The Torah scrolls were not touched. There's some disagreement about how bad it was. The congregation's own committee said nothing was stolen and there were no signs of forced entry. The community's security group says there's no evidence Jews were singled out, and urged "caution and responsibility." One committee member said it looked like an attempted robbery, but added, "Of course, it can't be a coincidence that they chose to target a Jewish place of worship." There are also reports that Jewish-owned shops in Marseille were looted or burned. France recorded about thirteen hundred and twenty antisemitic incidents last year.

Back in Manhattan, a note reading "Nuke Jews, Save Lives" was left at the front entrance of the Center for Jewish History, in the Flatiron District. The center holds sixty-five million documents of Jewish history. And on the Upper East Side, the windows of a Capital One branch on East Eighty-Sixth Street were smashed early Monday, apparently with bricks. Red graffiti read "Pal Action" and "Drop Elbit." Elbit is an Israeli defense company, and protesters have targeted Capital One over its loans to it. Nobody was hurt and nobody's been arrested. The NYPD's Hate Crimes Task Force is investigating, and Council Speaker Julie Menin called it "deeply disturbing and unacceptable." Context from the NYPD: through September, hate crimes in the city are up about thirteen percent, and anti-Jewish incidents make up more than half of all of them.

In music, the rapper Macklemore is in a new controversy. Last month he was dropped as an opening act on Ed Sheeran's US stadium tour after pro-Palestinian comments on stage. Now he's launched what he calls a "Free Palestine" tour, in Dublin, Paris and London at the end of the month, with proceeds going to twelve groups. One of them, the Palestinian Youth Movement, once called the October seventh massacre a "prison break" in an essay. Britain's Jewish Leadership Council said that framing "risks legitimizing violence against Jews." The Israeli-American Council wants Live Nation, the giant concert company, to explain how it vets these things. Macklemore says the shows "sold out immediately."

In Colorado, Boulder could elect a mayor who won't say whether Israel should exist. This is the city where, in June last year, a firebomb attack on a walk for the hostages killed one person and injured about a dozen. Council member Taishya Adams, backed by the local DSA, the Democratic Socialists, is challenging the current mayor, Aaron Brockett. Asked if Israel should exist, Adams said, "I don't believe that creating false binaries is going to lead to our collective liberation." Brockett's answer: "I do." A local rabbi, Fred Greene, put it this way: "Israel's right to exist is not a partisan position."

And a gentler one to end. In Argentina, a small museum dedicated to the Jewish gauchos, the Jewish cowboys and farmers who settled the Argentine countryside in the eighteen-nineties, says it is staying open. It's in the town of Villa Domínguez, where only nine Jews still live. It has had no director since last year, and water damage drew worries around the world, but the local community leader says simply, "The museum is still operating."

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a big new name from one of Israel's most famous founders.

Amnon Shashua, who built Mobileye and co-founded AI21, has taken his new company, doubleAI, out of stealth, meaning it was operating in secret until now. It's raised a two-hundred-million-dollar Series A, co-led by Lightspeed and Bessemer, with Nvidia and Dell's investment arm also in. Calcalist reported earlier that the valuation is over a billion dollars, so it's a unicorn on day one, with only about forty employees. Its first product is called Maxwell. The idea: instead of rewriting software, Maxwell looks at a program while it runs, on its actual chips and data, and rewrites the low-level instructions so it runs faster and cheaper. The company claims data pipelines run more than thirty-eight times faster. Those are its own numbers, not independently checked yet. The part you'll find interesting is the pricing. Customers pay based on how much computing cost they actually save. That's outcome-based pricing, you pay for results, not for seats. It means the sales team's whole job is proving the savings.

Next, Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude AI models, announced on Sunday that it's putting five percent of its shares into a new fund for the public good. It will add two percent more, at the board's discretion, for every fifty percent increase in the company's value. The fund could give to charities or make payments directly to groups of people, and AI's impact on jobs is named as one possible focus. It can't be used for lobbying or political donations. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, a company legally required to weigh public good alongside profit. It's also reportedly preparing an IPO before the end of the year.

Three Israeli startups made TIME's Best Inventions of twenty twenty-six. SpotitEarly uses trained beagles to sniff breath samples that people send from home, with AI watching the dogs, to catch early signs of lung, breast, colon and prostate cancer. It reports ninety-four percent early-detection success in clinical trials. Beewise makes robotic beehives. And Bria AI built an image generator that pays royalties to the artists whose work shapes each new image. Its CEO, Yair Adato, said, "We understand which data was leveraged for the newly generated image, and then we compensate the creator of the original."

Now a reality check on AI in Israel: electricity. Israel's Electricity Authority is capping new large data centers at two hundred megawatts each. A megawatt is a unit of power; two hundred is roughly what a mid-sized city uses. The grid operator received requests for twenty-seven thousand megawatts, about three times Israel's entire average consumption. The new rules allow about three thousand megawatts in total, and developers must accept being switched off during extreme demand. Lesson: AI growth here now has a hard physical limit.

Two numbers on the job market you're in. A new study from Reichman University says Israeli high-tech employed about four hundred thousand people last year, roughly eighteen thousand below where the old growth trend would have put it. The share of startup staff based in Israel fell from about sixty-nine percent in twenty nineteen to about sixty-two percent now. And the money is concentrated. AI's share of investment jumped to thirty-five percent in three years, and cyber took forty-four percent last year. The researcher's line: "the recovery is not as broad-based as it might appear." Separately, one of Israel's best-known venture firms, NFX, run by Gigi Levy-Weiss, says it will stop raising money from outside investors. Its partners will write personal checks instead, and staff were told to look for new jobs. Israeli funds are on track to raise about two billion dollars this year, seventy percent below the twenty twenty-one peak. Fewer local investors means more Israeli startups depend on American money, and often American headquarters.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and go-to-market, starting with a breach that has a lot of IT companies nervous.

ConnectWise, which makes software used by MSPs, the outside IT firms that manage technology for small and mid-size businesses, has shut down three cloud services after a compromise. On October fifteenth it found unauthorized access inside its Cloud Backup product. By the weekend, customers couldn't back up, restore or reach their archives. The security firm Huntress says a Microsoft three sixty-five API secret, basically a password that one app uses to talk to another, was stolen. In one customer's account, a ConnectWise app gave itself top administrator rights. ConnectWise says archives remain encrypted with "no evidence of unauthorized access." People are comparing it to the Kaseya attack of twenty twenty-one, a classic supply-chain attack, where hackers hit one supplier to reach all its customers. For sales teams, the pitch writes itself: protect the identities of apps and machines, not just people. That's called non-human identity, and it's one of the hottest categories in security.

Now, numbers for your job hunt. A CyberSN report says US cybersecurity job postings fell twenty-six percent in the first nine months of this year, to a four-year low. But AI security engineer postings rose two hundred and sixty-eight percent. Postings for pen testers, hackers hired to test defenses, fell, and so did postings for CISOs, chief information security officers, though those picked up again last quarter. And here's the useful bit for you: sales is the most remote-friendly function in cyber, with almost a third of sales postings remote. The report's own warning: "Job titles are noisy." So read the job description, not just the title.

A deal from the AI world: Anthropic has acquired Opaque Systems, a startup that does confidential computing, a way of keeping data encrypted even while AI is processing it. Opaque, which was valued at three hundred million dollars earlier this year, is winding down and its team is joining Anthropic. Terms weren't disclosed. The bigger trend: AI labs are buying security companies so they can win big companies and governments as customers.

On the people side, Darktrace, the British AI security company, rebuilt its top team. Mike McGuinness is the new CRO. Adam Towns, formerly CFO at Sisense, becomes chief financial and operating officer, and Beth Shea, formerly of Snyk, is chief customer officer. Two of those three came from Israeli-founded companies. When a whole revenue team changes at the top, hiring and reorganizing below usually follows.

And some perspective from Gil Shwed, Check Point's founder and executive chairman. On a podcast, he said he doesn't see economic models that justify AI spending heading toward eight hundred billion dollars this year. "We will know in ten or twenty years," he said. He also pointed out that only about twenty percent of a software company's staff is in development. The rest is support, marketing and sales. And he expects AI to multiply how many security holes are found, maybe a thousandfold. More holes found means more to fix, and more to sell.

## Business story

Now our business story, about a Jerusalem company that got sold three times and kept getting more valuable.

In nineteen seventy-six, three physicists with PhDs, Yaakov Zerem, Ephraim Greenfield and Ephraim Secemski, started a small optics company in Jerusalem. They called it Ophir. It made precise components for infrared light, the kind of light you can't see but can feel as heat. That technology sits inside thermal cameras and night-vision gear, the equipment that lets soldiers and security forces see in the dark.

The early years were patient. In nineteen ninety-one, Ophir went public on the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange. In twenty oh-eight, the private equity fund FIMI bought twenty percent for twenty-three and a half million dollars.

Then the first sale. In twenty eleven, the American company Newport bought Ophir at a valuation of about two hundred and thirty million dollars. Five years later, in twenty sixteen, a bigger American company, MKS, bought Newport, with Ophir inside it, for nine hundred and sixty million.

And now, this week, the third chapter. MKS signed a deal to sell just the optics part of Ophir to TTM Technologies, for six hundred and fifty million dollars in cash. One division, alone, worth almost three times what the whole company sold for fifteen years ago. Globes estimates the price at about ten times EBITDA, a common measure of a company's operating profit. As far as is known, the laser division and the Ophir brand stay with MKS.

Today Ophir has about two hundred and fifty employees, based in Jerusalem's Har Hotzvim tech park, with facilities in Romania, under CEO Kobi Lasri. Why does a buyer pay that much now? Because defense spending around the world has exploded, and thermal and night-vision optics are exactly what militaries are buying.

Here's why the story matters. In Israeli tech, we mostly hear about startups that raise huge rounds and sell within a few years. Ophir is the opposite. Fifty years, three owners, no hype, and it kept growing in value by being very good at one hard thing. Not every great Israeli company is a unicorn. Some are just quietly excellent for half a century.

## Wellness

Finally, a wellness idea, and it's a relief. You don't have to cut out every ultra-processed food, meaning industrially made packaged foods.

A big new study in the journal PLOS Medicine followed more than four hundred and sixty thousand American adults for over twenty years. Total ultra-processed food was not linked to bowel cancer. What mattered was which ones. Processed meats like hot dogs were the worst. Some ultra-processed foods with whole grains, fiber or dairy, like yogurt, some breakfast cereals and bread, were linked to lower risk. And swapping just ten percent of ultra-processed food for less-processed food was linked to a small drop in risk. The lead researcher, Erikka Loftfield, said we "should avoid treating all ultra-processed foods as one group." It's an observational study, so it shows links, not proof, and most participants were older. But the practical takeaway is simple. Don't panic about every packaged thing. Cut back on processed meat, keep the yogurt, and make one small swap a day.
