Good morning Sophie. It's Monday, November sixteenth, and this is your Morning Brief. Today, a Channel 12 investigation says Prime Minister Netanyahu's office was behind the tip that led to the arrests of Arab party activists on election eve, and Gadi Eisenkot says he'll launch an official inquiry. In the region, Yemen's government pulled its forces back from one of the world's most important shipping lanes as the Houthis push toward Aden. ## Israel

We start here at home, with a report that could reshape the whole fight over who forms the next government. On Sunday night, Uvda, Channel 12's investigative program, aired a report on the arrests of three activists from Ra'am, the Islamist Arab party, in southern Israel on the eve of the October twenty-seventh election. Police said at the time that the activists were carrying large sums of cash and Ra'am vouchers meant to sway voters.

Here's what Channel 12 says really happened. On election eve, Ido Norden, Netanyahu's chief of staff, meaning his top aide, contacted the police commissioner's office about the three activists, and referred the police to an intelligence source, a former employee of the Prime Minister's Office. Police then raided the activists' homes, according to the report without valid search or arrest warrants. Netanyahu publicized the arrests quickly, repeated the allegations on election day, and the case became a pillar of his campaign claim that Arab voters were "stealing" the election. The report adds one more detail: on election day, the head of the Shin Bet, David Zini, reportedly asked for permission to wiretap, meaning secretly listen in on, five Arab lawmakers over suspected foreign interference. The attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara, turned him down.

The reactions were fierce. Eisenkot wrote on X that Netanyahu and his associates are a "direct and immediate threat" to Israeli democracy, and that "as long as he is in office, there will no longer be fair and lawful elections in Israel." He demanded answers from the police and the Shin Bet, and promised a state commission of inquiry into the Prime Minister's Office and the security agencies. Joint List chair Yousef Jabareen called it a "despicable blood libel," a term for a false accusation used to incite hatred against a whole group. The Prime Minister's Office rejects the claim that it manufactured the arrests.

Why does this matter? The report doesn't settle whether those activists did anything wrong; that's for the investigators. But if the most famous case was steered from Netanyahu's office, it hands Eisenkot a powerful argument as he courts the Arab parties for Knesset votes, and it puts the police and Shin Bet under a spotlight.

Meanwhile, the unity talks happened, and went nowhere. Negotiators from Likud and from Yashar, Eisenkot's party, met for about an hour in Herzliya on Sunday and released a short joint statement about a "businesslike atmosphere" and more talks to come. Hebrew media reported no progress. Likud asked Yashar to drop Matan Kahana as its candidate for Knesset speaker; Yashar refused. Senior Yashar lawmaker Chili Tropper reportedly told Likud's team: "Eisenkot is determined to create a government in any scenario. You ought to internalize this." There's one intriguing report: Israel Hayom says people close to Netanyahu told Eisenkot that Likud wouldn't insist on including Itamar Ben-Gvir's Otzma Yehudit, and that the US has demanded the far-right party be left out. Publicly, Likud still says no one in its bloc should be excluded.

Not everyone in Eisenkot's camp even wanted the meeting. Avigdor Liberman called it a trap, saying "the most important goal is to put an end to the Netanyahu era." His party colleague Yulia Malinovsky put it more colorfully: making a deal with Netanyahu, she wrote, "is like making a deal with the Tinder Swindler," the conman from the Netflix documentary.

The real battleground is still the ultra-Orthodox. A source in United Torah Judaism told Channel 12 the party is in daily contact with Eisenkot's bloc and making "real progress." But party chairman Yaakov Asher denied reports of draft agreements, and Shas leader Aryeh Deri was blunt: "We are going with Netanyahu, and that's what will happen."

What's next: tomorrow, Tuesday, is the speaker vote to replace Likud's Amir Ohana with Kahana. Eisenkot's bloc has also agreed to give Yair Golan's Democrats the chair of a new Knesset committee on legal and constitutional affairs, which is expected to push bills reversing the judicial overhaul. "This is the beginning of the repair," Golan said.

One more piece of the security debate. According to Axios, Mossad director Roman Gofman flew to Washington on Friday, met CIA Director John Ratcliffe and was expected to see envoy Steve Witkoff, as part of Netanyahu's push to persuade Trump to resume strikes on Iran. Inside Israel, opinion is split. Ynet's military analysts report that defense officials see no urgent operational need to strike now, and would prefer any new campaign to be run alongside the US, with broad political backing rather than under a caretaker government.

And in Gaza, the army and the Shin Bet confirmed they killed Samir Abu Younis, the commander of Hamas's Rafah Brigade, in a strike in southern Gaza. He'd held the job for about two months, since his predecessor was killed in September.

And some good news for your wallet. On Sunday, the Central Bureau of Statistics said the consumer price index, the official measure of what everyday things cost, fell zero point one percent in October. Analysts had expected a rise of zero point two to zero point four percent. That brings inflation over the past year down to one point one percent, the lowest in five and a half years, and near the bottom of the Bank of Israel's target range of one to three percent. Home prices fell for a second month in a row and are down one and a half percent from a year ago, led by Tel Aviv. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich credited his policies. Why it matters: with inflation this low, pressure grows on the Bank of Israel to cut its interest rate, now three point two five percent, which would make mortgages and loans cheaper. One catch for renters: one report says rent for new tenants is still up about five and a half percent.

## World

Now to the world, and the war at sea is getting worse even as Washington talks about holding off. On Sunday, Bahrain, the small Gulf kingdom that hosts the US Navy's Fifth Fleet, issued its first threat alerts since September and said it intercepted several Iranian aerial attacks. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it launched six drones at an American radar system at a base in Bahrain. Bahrain also revealed an earlier Iranian attack on November thirteenth and said it won't let its land become an "arena for settling scores."

In the Strait of Hormuz, Iran's navy said three tankers caught fire after hitting mines, and Iran later claimed that a supertanker, one of the largest kinds of oil ship, carrying two million barrels sank in the Gulf. That's Iran's claim, not independently confirmed. Senior Iranian official Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Tehran won't accept a situation of "neither war nor peace" with the US. On the diplomatic side, Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi is due in Oman on Tuesday, and Oman has often been the go-between for Tehran and Washington.

Now the other front, and it's a big one: Yemen. On Sunday, government-allied forces said they were pulling out of the area around the Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow strait at the bottom of the Red Sea, the gateway between the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean, and the Houthis took control. Here's the background. The Houthis, the Iran-backed movement that runs northern Yemen, drove the internationally recognized government out of the capital Sanaa more than a decade ago, and since then the government has been based in the southern port of Aden. A ceasefire from twenty twenty-two collapsed this summer. In September the Houthis seized large parts of the west coast; a government counteroffensive retook some ground, including the area around the strait, but couldn't push them out. Now the government says it's pulling back to defend Aden itself, where Houthi forces are about sixty kilometers away, and to try to break the Houthi siege of Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city.

The government's spokesman stressed that the strait is vital for free shipping and asked the world for help. That's the point: with the Houthis holding Yemen's side of the Red Sea's southern gate while mines keep hitting ships in Hormuz, two of the world's key oil and shipping chokepoints are under pressure at the same time. The Houthis also fired two ballistic missiles toward Riyadh, which the Saudi-led coalition says it destroyed.

And Trump leaves Washington today for his Asia trip, with a stop at the ASEAN summit, the gathering of Southeast Asian nations, in Manila, before Thursday's three-way meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in China, where he's expected to raise Iran.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where a big test for eighth and ninth graders turned into a mess. On Saturday, a problem with a vendor's testing platform stopped many students from logging into or finishing the SHSAT, the Specialized High School Admissions Test, the exam that decides who gets into schools like Stuyvesant and Bronx Science. Students were sent home early. Local officials say the vendor is Pearson. Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels said the exam will be rescheduled for students who couldn't finish, that it won't affect their scores or admission, and that he's ordered a full investigation of the provider.

And the money: Mayor Mamdani released his November financial plan, a mid-year update to the city budget. Next year's budget is now one hundred twenty-nine point two billion dollars. The budget gap for the following year, the shortfall the city must close, is six point two billion, about a quarter of a billion smaller than in June. The plan adds money for the Code Blue cold-weather response and to cover federal cuts to food stamps and homeless services.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a Republican fight over Israel. On Sunday at the Jewish Leadership Conference in Manhattan, hosted by the conservative Tikvah Fund, Senator Ted Cruz of Texas said he "certainly" disagrees with Vice President JD Vance. After Republicans lost both chambers of Congress in the midterms, Vance posted on X citing a pollster who said defecting Republican voters wanted America to change its relationship with Israel. Cruz said he sees no "mandate to abandon Israel" in the election.

He went further. Cruz warned about antisemitism on both sides, naming Mayor Mamdani and Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed on the left, and Tucker Carlson, Candace Owens and former congresswoman Marjorie Taylor Greene on the right. He estimated that a third of young people on the right are receptive to their message, and warned of a future where both parties are anti-Israel. Why it matters: Vance is widely seen as the front-runner for the twenty twenty-eight Republican nomination, so this is an early preview of a fight over the party's future on Israel.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and a huge week for Nvidia, the company whose chips power most of the world's AI. On Tuesday, November seventeenth, Nvidia reports its quarterly earnings, the results every public company releases every three months. It's the first since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned last week after reports about his relationships, including one with a woman allegedly linked to Chinese intelligence. Colette Kress, the CFO, is interim CEO, the temporary boss, and Harvey Jones, the former chief of chip-design company Synopsys, is Nvidia's first board chair. Analysts will be listening for two things: whether demand for AI chips is still growing, and what the board says about finding a permanent CEO. This one matters for Israel too: Nvidia is one of the biggest multinational tech employers in Israel, much of it through Mellanox, the Israeli chip company it bought for nearly seven billion dollars.

And some Israeli AI with a health twist. Clalit, Israel's largest health fund, together with Ariel University and a startup called ALZAI Health, says it has validated an AI model that flags people at risk of Alzheimer's using only routine medical records, blood tests, past diagnoses and age, with no brain scans. The validation covered more than one point six million patient records in Israel and the US. A caveat: the announcement didn't share accuracy numbers or how many years ahead it can predict, and it hasn't been published in a peer-reviewed journal, one checked by independent scientists, so treat it as promising, not proven.

## Business story

Our business story is about a smart ring that wants to be your doctor. Last Tuesday, Oura, the Finnish company behind the ring that tracks your sleep, heart rate and recovery, agreed to buy Counsel Health for six hundred twenty-five million dollars in cash.

Counsel is an AI-based primary care service, meaning everyday doctor visits, not specialists. You message an AI about your symptoms; it takes your medical history, checks guidelines, reviews records and watches for emergencies, and a human doctor can join the chat to diagnose, prescribe or refer you. It's already been inside the Oura app since June. Counsel has about seventy employees and raised roughly thirty-six million dollars in total, including a Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz and GV, Google's venture arm. So Oura is paying about seventeen times what investors put in.

Why now? Oura is big: more than six million members, a valuation of eleven billion dollars, and revenue of five hundred thirty-three million dollars last quarter, up one hundred fifty-three percent from a year earlier. But in September it postponed its IPO, its stock market debut, citing a shaky market. And rivals like Ultrahuman and RingConn are catching up. The strategy is clear: a ring can be copied, but a ring plus your data plus a doctor on call is a service people keep paying for.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with a gentle idea for your evenings. Researchers at New York University's nursing school followed one hundred ninety-six adults with prediabetes, meaning blood sugar higher than normal but not yet diabetes, who all slept less than six and a half hours a night. Published in the journal Nursing Research, the study found that falling asleep earlier was linked to more weight loss over eight weeks, regardless of how many hours people slept. Each hour earlier was tied to a little over half a kilo lost. The researchers think earlier bedtimes may mean less late-night snacking and less screen light. The caveats: it's a small, short study in a specific group, and it shows a link, not proof. Still, it's an easy experiment: try putting your phone away and lights out thirty minutes earlier this week.

## Funding and acquisitions

It was another quiet weekend for brand-new cyber rounds, and I couldn't confirm a single new funding round or acquisition in the last forty-eight hours. So today we'll go deeper on one Israeli money story, then spend real time on the most important sales story of the week.

The money story is about Cyberstarts, the Tel Aviv venture fund that backed Wiz before Google bought it for thirty-two billion dollars. Calcalist reported on Sunday that Cyberstarts has raised about three hundred sixty million dollars across two new funds. Yesterday we told you about the big part: the three hundred million it's putting into Cyera's new round. What's new is how, and the second fund.

First, the how. The Cyera money sits in a special purpose vehicle, an SPV, a separate fund created for one investment. Many investors pool their money into it, but it shows up as a single shareholder on Cyera's books. For the startup, that's simpler: one name on the cap table, the list of who owns the company, one voice in votes and fewer signatures in future funding rounds. For you, it's a sign of how much demand there is to own a piece of Cyera.

Second, and more useful for your job search: Cyberstarts also raised its fifth seed fund, about sixty million dollars, the same size as its previous four. The limited partners, the investors who put money into a venture fund, are mostly entrepreneurs and private investors from the cyber industry itself, meaning founders who've already sold companies are betting on the next generation. Cyberstarts was founded in twenty eighteen by Gili Raanan, and as of last year managed more than one point four billion dollars. Its portfolio includes Wiz, Upwind, Fireblocks, Cyera and Island. One more number tells the story: in twenty twenty-one, Cyberstarts put four point two million dollars into Cyera at a seed valuation of about fifteen million dollars. Today it's being valued at around twenty billion. A fresh seed fund means a new batch of Israeli cyber startups over the next year or two, each of which will eventually need someone to build its brand from zero.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and the one event every cyber marketer should watch this week. On Wednesday, November eighteenth, after the US market closes, Palo Alto Networks, one of the biggest cybersecurity companies in the world, reports its quarterly results, with CyberArk, the Israeli identity security giant it bought, now part of its numbers.

Why is an earnings report a marketing story? Because Palo Alto's entire go-to-market strategy has a name: platformization. That means persuading a customer to rip out many separate security tools from different vendors and replace them with one Palo Alto platform, sometimes with incentives to ease the switch. Last quarter it reported about two hundred twenty platformizations, meaning customers who made that move. Investors treat that number as proof that the pitch works.

The numbers show how far it's gone. Palo Alto says next-generation security ARR, annual recurring revenue from its newer products, topped nine point one billion dollars. Its AI security product, Prisma AIRS, passed one hundred million dollars in ARR within four quarters, and identity security, where CyberArk lives, passed seven hundred million. Analysts expect about three point three one billion dollars in revenue this quarter, up about thirty-four percent from a year ago, though much of that growth comes from acquisitions. According to Earnings Whispers, the stock has fallen about six percent since the last report, which they call a credibility test for its upbeat AI security story.

Three lessons for you as a marketer. First, a strategy with a name travels. "Platformization" is one word that salespeople, investors and journalists all repeat. Ask yourself in every interview: does this company have a phrase for how it wins? Second, the metric is the message. Palo Alto doesn't just say customers love the platform; it publishes a count every quarter. That's the kind of proof point, hard evidence behind a claim, that product marketers fight for. Third, notice the risk. Last year's growth got a one-time boost of more than one hundred million dollars from a single AI customer moving onto Chronosphere, a monitoring company Palo Alto bought, and that boost is over. When a story runs ahead of the numbers, marketing gets blamed first. Watch Wednesday's call for how management describes demand.

Here's why this matters for Israel. Palo Alto is one of the most active buyers of Israeli cyber startups. It's reportedly in talks for Clutch Security and Beacon Security, as we covered recently, and every platformization pitch needs new products to plug in. A strong quarter usually means more appetite for deals here.

One more lesson, from the Cyberstarts story: in cyber, your investors are part of your brand. When a startup can say Cyberstarts or a founder who sold to Google is behind it, that line goes straight into the sales deck, because buyers trust companies with famous backers. If you join a seed-stage startup, that's your first marketing asset.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Cyberstarts' new seed fund. Follow its next investments over the coming months. Its past bets, like Wiz and Cyera, grew from small Israeli teams into giants, and young companies like these often hire a first marketing lead soon after a big seed round. Getting to know the founders it backs could put you in front of those roles before they're even posted.

Second, Palo Alto's Israel operation. With CyberArk now inside the company, it has a big presence here, and a platform company that sells many products under one brand needs product marketers and partner marketers for each piece. Wednesday's results will tell you whether it's in a growth mood.

Third, the companies Palo Alto reportedly wants to buy, Clutch and Beacon. If those deals close, their teams get folded into a giant, and some marketers may leave rather than stay. That can open spots at similar startups competing in the same space.
