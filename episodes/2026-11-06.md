## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Friday, November sixth, and this is your Morning Brief. Gadi Eisenkot now has sixty-seven lawmakers recommending him for prime minister, the most for any candidate in more than a decade, and President Herzog will announce his decision on Sunday. On Wall Street, a one-day post-election party turned into the worst day for tech stocks in a year and a half, wiping out about three trillion dollars. And Trump says he'd "rather not destroy the rest" of Iran, but "maybe I'll have to." Let's go.

## Headlines

We start here at home, where the math finally works for Eisenkot. Last night President Herzog finished his consultations, the meetings where each party tells the president who it wants as prime minister. The final count: sixty-seven recommendations for Eisenkot, out of one hundred twenty Knesset members, and forty-six for Netanyahu. Eisenkot's party, Yashar, says that's the most any candidate has received since twenty fifteen. Herzog called the result "clear."

The decisive moment was the Joint List, the alliance of Arab-led parties, with eleven seats. It recommended Eisenkot, but without much warmth. Its chair, Yousef Jabareen, said Eisenkot hadn't firmly opposed incitement against Arab citizens, and described the choice as one between "bad and even worse." His colleague Ahmad Tibi said they'd support a minority government from outside, without joining it, but warned they won't be a long-term safety net, backing from outside without taking ministries, for a government that collapses fast. Ra'am, the Islamist party, also backed Eisenkot and promised not to interfere in defense matters, though its leader Mansour Abbas still didn't explicitly call Hamas a terrorist organization, something Eisenkot has demanded from partners.

Why didn't Netanyahu get his fifty-three? Because United Torah Judaism, the seven-seat ultra-Orthodox alliance, refused to recommend anyone. Its rabbis are furious that Netanyahu's government never passed a law exempting yeshiva students from military service. That's the single biggest issue for the Haredi community, and it just cost Netanyahu seven names.

Here's the twist to watch. Channel 12 reports that Avigdor Liberman, the secular right-winger who has spent years attacking the ultra-Orthodox parties, has dropped his refusal to sit with them. He'd reportedly rather partner with United Torah Judaism than depend on Arab parties. That kind of government could include a rotation, where Liberman and Eisenkot take turns as prime minister, plus talks on a deal over Haredi military service. Nothing is agreed, but it shows how many shapes this coalition could take.

Netanyahu's side isn't conceding. In a recorded video on Wednesday, his first public appearance since election night, he warned that a government relying on Arab parties could not confront what he called radical Islam, and called for a broad unity government under his leadership. He also repeated claims of "serious suspicions" that foreign countries spent "enormous sums" to influence the vote. In Herzog's room, Likud minister Miri Regev called Eisenkot's camp a "disaster bloc." Herzog shot back that you can't extend a hand and use that phrase in the same breath. Eisenkot's party rejected sitting under Netanyahu.

What's next: Herzog announces on Sunday who gets the mandate, the official task of forming a government. Then that person has twenty-eight days, plus a possible fourteen-day extension. The new Knesset is sworn in on Tuesday, and Eisenkot's allies want an early vote to replace the Knesset speaker, the lawmaker who controls what comes to a vote. The current speaker, Amir Ohana of Likud, says he'll schedule that debate if sixty-one lawmakers formally ask.

Now to Iran, where Trump sounds a bit less sure of himself. Speaking in the Oval Office on Thursday, he said, "I would rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I'll have to." He added that a decision is coming, "You'll know soon enough," and that rebuilding Iran would take twenty-five years. Remember, Trump had promised not to strike before the midterms, and that promise has now expired. Meanwhile Al Jazeera reports Iran has sent a new nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatari mediators, which the US is reviewing, while Tehran insists its seven conditions, including lifting sanctions and ending the US naval blockade, must be met. Iran also warned that any country helping the US attack it would be a "legitimate target," a clear message to its Gulf neighbors.

The war at sea goes on. Britain's maritime security agency says a tanker was hit by two unidentified projectiles on Thursday, about fifty nautical miles off northern Qatar. Iran's Revolutionary Guard also claims more ships hit mines or caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz. Even so, oil prices fell sharply right after the midterms, with Brent crude dropping below one hundred dollars a barrel on Wednesday for the first time since the end of September. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said, "This war must end," and suggested Congress could use funding to force it.

In America, the post-election reckoning continues. Al Jazeera reports Vice President JD Vance is already eyeing twenty twenty-eight, as Republicans debate a change of direction after their losses. And Los Angeles elected Nithya Raman as mayor, the city's first South Asian mayor.

Two quick world stories. In Paraguay, Congress refused to accept President Santiago Peña's resignation after the collapse of a digital bank he had ties to, and is moving toward impeachment instead, a formal trial to remove him. And Turkey's parliament ratified a defense pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, signed in Mecca in August, part of a new web of alliances forming around the Iran war. In the same spirit, Syria's president, Ahmed al-Sharaa, was in Riyadh this week, his second visit in under a month, and signed a defense cooperation deal with Saudi Arabia, plus about four hundred million dollars in Saudi loans. It's a remarkable turn for a country that was Iran's closest Arab ally until its old regime fell. And it comes as Yemen's Houthis, who are backed by Iran, keep firing missiles and drones at Saudi Arabia, including a claimed strike on Riyadh's main airport on Wednesday that the Saudis haven't confirmed.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where a fire in Brooklyn killed six people early Thursday. It started around one forty in the morning in a two-story row house on Fifty-Eighth Street in Sunset Park. The first and second floors were fully engulfed, the second floor and staircase collapsed, and the flames spread next door. Firefighters found four people dead inside, and two more died in the hospital. Seven others were hurt, including three firefighters. More than two hundred thirty fire and emergency workers responded. Mayor Mamdani called it "an intense, fast-growing three-alarm fire," three alarms meaning the city sent three waves of crews. The cause is under investigation, but hazmat teams were called after a lithium-ion battery was found, the rechargeable kind in e-bikes and scooters. The fire marshal is checking whether it started the blaze. These batteries have been behind many deadly fires in the city, so if you know anyone charging an e-bike overnight, it's worth a reminder.

On a brighter note for the mayor, he's still celebrating Tuesday's results. All five of his ballot proposals passed, Democrats won both houses of Congress, and his friend Abdul El-Sayed became the first Muslim elected to the Senate. Mamdani's line, borrowing from Ronald Reagan's famous nineteen eighty-four campaign ad: "It's morning again in America."

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and the community in Michigan is trying to heal after the El-Sayed race. JTA reports that many traditional pro-Israel leaders opposed him, pointing to his criticism of Israel and his ties to the streamer Hasan Piker. More than five hundred sixty Michigan Jews signed a petition pledging to vote for his Republican opponent. But early exit polls suggest a majority of Michigan Jews may have voted for El-Sayed anyway. Rabbi Asher Lopatin of Ann Arbor, who campaigned against him, put it honestly: "I think the election divided the Jewish community," but "we have to work with what we have." The head of the Jewish Federations of North America urged everyone to move forward "with optimism and pride."

In California, Scott Wiener, a progressive Jewish state senator who has accused Israel of genocide, won Nancy Pelosi's old House seat in San Francisco, after a race in which both candidates attacked Israel. It's another sign of how much the Democratic Party's conversation about Israel has shifted.

And a moving moment: Boy George, the eighties pop icon, who has become an outspoken supporter of Israel, made an emotional visit to the memorial for the Nova music festival, where hundreds were murdered on October seventh.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech and AI, starting with a number that should make everyone sit up. OpenAI ran an experiment where AI agents, AI tools that take actions on their own, hunted for security holes in Linux, the free operating system that runs most of the world's servers. They found two thousand three hundred potential flaws and built working attacks for nine hundred forty-eight of them, at a cost of about one point two million dollars in computing, roughly one thousand three hundred dollars per working attack. In July, a similar test found one hundred sixty-six. The attacks need an existing foothold on the computer, and Linux maintainers are already patching, but the speed jump is the story.

OpenAI is also buying again. It plans to acquire Pierre Computer, a developer-tools company whose code-navigation technology already powers parts of Codex, OpenAI's coding agent. Axios says it's part of OpenAI's push to challenge GitHub. Meta, for its part, bought Conductor, which lets programmers split a project among several AI agents. And there's movement between the labs: Cat Wu, who led product for Claude Code, has left Anthropic for OpenAI.

Two smaller signals. Bloomberg reports Apple is trimming some engineers' daily AI allowances, the budget for using AI tools, from three hundred dollars to two hundred, and pushing cheaper models. Even Apple is watching the AI bill. And Anthropic signed its first paid licensing deal with a news publisher, giving Claude access to the Associated Press's election coverage. Until now, Anthropic had avoided the kind of publisher deals OpenAI made.

## Business story

Our business story is the most dramatic market day in a long time, and it's a story about politics, not technology.

On Wednesday, Wall Street threw a party. The Dow jumped more than fifteen hundred points, its best day after a midterm election in forty-four years. Investors liked the idea of a divided government: a Democratic Congress, a Republican president, fewer surprises.

Then on Thursday, it all reversed. The Nasdaq fell about five percent, the S&P 500 almost four, and chip stocks around ten percent. AMD and Micron each lost roughly fifteen percent, Nvidia almost nine. Fortune estimates about three trillion dollars in US market value disappeared. In Asia, South Korea's market fell more than five percent.

So what happened? Data centers. These are the giant warehouses full of computers that power AI, and they use enormous amounts of electricity and water. Voters noticed. On Tuesday, forty-one local ballot measures aimed at slowing data center construction were on the ballot, and most passed, especially in Ohio. In Texas, Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat Governor Greg Abbott, and she wants a moratorium, a full pause, on new data centers. Wells Fargo estimates twenty-seven percent of planned data center capacity in America is in Texas. New Democratic governors in Ohio and Georgia also campaigned on limiting them. Interestingly, it's not purely partisan: Abbott himself paused new permits in September while the state audited its power grid.

The fear is simple. The whole AI boom depends on building more data centers. If states slow that down, chipmakers sell fewer chips. Some analysts think this just lets "air out of an inevitable AI bubble without popping it." Others think the selloff is overdone. The winners on Thursday, by the way, were boring companies: consumer staples rose, and Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway hit an all-time high.

Why it matters to you: Israel's tech economy is deeply tied to AI chips, with Nvidia one of the biggest tech employers here. And as a marketer, notice the lesson. A product can be loved by investors and hated by neighbors. Data center companies never told a story voters liked.

Six women testified about incidents between nineteen ninety-nine and two thousand nine. None of them knew each other, and they came forward after a twenty twenty-three TV documentary. He was cleared of two other rape charges and one other count. Brand denied everything, saying the encounters were consensual or invented. He's in custody, and the judge, who will sentence him on Monday, said he faces a lengthy prison term.

At the movies this weekend, "Godzilla Minus Zero" opens, the follow-up to the Oscar-winning "Godzilla Minus One," picking up right after that film's story in nineteen forty-seven and shot for IMAX. ## Art and design

Now art and design, and a story where fashion, museums and the Jewish world meet. The Met's Costume Institute, the museum's fashion department, whose spring show launches the Met Gala every May, announced what replaces its canceled John Galliano exhibition. Quick background: in July the Met announced the first full retrospective, a whole-career exhibition, of Galliano, the designer fired from Dior in twenty eleven over antisemitic remarks. After a backlash over that record, including racist and anti-Asian comments, the show was canceled at the end of August. Galliano said he remains "fully accountable for the pain caused." The new show is called "extraORDINARY: Fashion and the Poetics of the Everyday," running from May ninth, twenty twenty-seven. It looks at how designers turn familiar garments and materials into something new. Some critics already call it a safe choice. Nobody knows yet whether it will be the Met Gala theme, so yes, jeans on the red carpet are technically possible.

And a sweet crossover: Stella McCartney released her third collaboration with Yoshitomo Nara, the Japanese artist famous for his defiant cartoon girls, a limited sustainable collection of knits, scarves and a reworked sneaker. Nara also has a big new New York gallery show at David Zwirner, with nearly one hundred works, according to Artnet.

## Travel

Travel now, and a practical tip if you're dreaming of a European Christmas market but can only get away between Christmas and New Year's. Many markets close on December twenty-fourth, but not all. Vienna's market at Schönbrunn Palace opens today and runs until January sixth, open daily even after Christmas. Prague's big markets in Old Town Square and Wenceslas Square run from November twenty-eighth to January sixth. Tallinn's Town Hall Square market also runs to January sixth, and in Cologne, the Heumarkt winter market stays open until January tenth. But Strasbourg's main market closes on Christmas Eve. The rule of thumb: check the exact market, not just the city, and expect shorter hours and less public transport on Christmas Day and New Year's Day.

## Wellness

And your wellness idea: mix it up. A Harvard study followed about one hundred ten thousand Americans for three decades, and the people who did the widest variety of exercise were nineteen percent less likely to die during the study than people who stuck to one activity. Walking, running, weights, rowing and tennis all helped on their own. It's observational, so it shows a link, not proof. The lead researcher, Dr. Yang Hu, thinks different activities give different benefits, and that the right blend is personal. So if you love Pilates, keep it, and add a long walk and one strength session a week.
