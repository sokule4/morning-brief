## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Thursday, November twelfth, and this is your Morning Brief. Gadi Eisenkot sent Netanyahu a sharp letter warning him not to make big security moves before a new government is sworn in, as reports say the army chief is again resisting action against Iran. Nvidia's board met to decide whether to push out Jensen Huang, the man who built the world's most valuable chip company. And on Veterans Day, Trump promised "total victory" over Iran, while his vice president weighs sitting down with Iran's security chief. In your Cyber Watch: Torq gets a new president, a former marketing chief becomes a CEO, and an AI security startup gets bought.

## Israel

We start here at home, with a letter that says a lot about this strange in-between moment. On Wednesday evening, Gadi Eisenkot, who has the mandate to form the next government, wrote to Benjamin Netanyahu. Netanyahu is still the caretaker prime minister, meaning he runs the government temporarily until a new one forms, and he still controls the army and the security services. Eisenkot urged him not to send security forces into action unless there's an immediate threat, warning of "devastating consequences" for Israeli democracy. He wrote that "extraneous considerations," meaning political ones, are creeping into security decisions, and that only forty-six lawmakers still back Netanyahu staying on.

The letter never mentions Iran. But it comes as Channel 12 reports that the army's chief of staff, its top commander, Lieutenant General Eyal Zamir, once again opposed military action Netanyahu wanted against Iran. Zamir reportedly resisted a similar move before the October twenty-seventh election, when defense officials feared it could spark an escalation and derail the vote. Eisenkot also asked the attorney general, the government's top lawyer, to step in, accusing Netanyahu's office of not arranging the security briefings an incoming prime minister normally gets.

Netanyahu, meanwhile, changed his tone. A day after his furious Knesset speech, he told reporters that a unity government is still "possible," saying that in three years of war, "Religious and secular, left and right, simply stood and fought together shoulder to shoulder."

In the Knesset itself, Eisenkot's camp is taking control of the machinery. Lawmakers approved the Arrangements Committee, the panel that runs parliament's business until a government forms, by sixty-three to fifty, with Yashar's Roy Folkman as chair. It didn't go quietly. Likud lawmakers disrupted the first meeting, and two of Netanyahu's allies, Tally Gotliv of Otzma Yehudit and Almog Cohen of Likud, were thrown out. The committee then set up temporary Finance and Foreign Affairs and Defense committees, with Liran Avisar Ben-Horin and Avigdor Liberman as chairs. One historic detail: Yoav Segalovitz of Ra'am, who is Jewish, is set to become the first member of an Arab-majority party on the defense committee, which oversees the army and the intelligence services.

Now the hard part, the math. Eisenkot's four parties have forty-eight seats. Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas said his party is considering working with the two ultra-Orthodox parties, Shas and United Torah Judaism, to back an Eisenkot government. Asked for details, he said, "Leave it for negotiation." One United Torah Judaism lawmaker, Eliakim Stark of the Belz Hasidic community, told Channel 13 that he supports teaching core subjects like math and English and getting more Haredi men into jobs, and that working with Arab parties is legitimate if it serves Haredi interests. But his party colleague Yaakov Asher warned against any government that depends on Arab and ultra-Orthodox abstentions: "That will not happen." And Shas still publicly backs Netanyahu.

The draft is still the wall in the middle. United Torah Judaism's Meir Porush filed a bill letting yeshiva students defer service every year until twenty-six, then be fully exempt, and another to freeze arrests of draft dodgers. On the other side, Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad filed a bill giving active reservists four thousand shekels a month toward childcare. Those two visions are very hard to fit in one government.

Two more threads. In Netanyahu's corruption trial, Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara turned down the judges' offer to talk with them behind closed doors, but said she's "open to a direct dialogue for an arrangement," which would mean a plea deal, an agreement to end the case without a full verdict. The judges have twice suggested dropping the bribery charge in Case four thousand, the case about favors for a news site. And on American aid, Netanyahu's office now says Israel will "phase out" US military aid gradually, not stop it this year as Trump said. Secretary of State Marco Rubio says it was a step "the Israelis themselves proposed."

Finally, Denmark and other European countries are pushing for an emergency UN Security Council meeting next week on E1, the area between Ma'ale Adumim and East Jerusalem. Israel's Land Authority has a November nineteenth deadline for bids to build twelve hundred thirty-four homes there. Critics say building there would cut the Palestinian West Bank in two. Israel's UN ambassador, Danny Danon, called the European move "a moral disgrace."

## World

Now to the world, and the Iran war. At Arlington National Cemetery on Veterans Day, Trump said, "We want to make sure we have a total victory, not a ninety-three percent victory. It's going to be total." He added, "America did not start a war with Iran. Iran has been waging war on America since nineteen seventy-nine." That's tougher than Monday, when he called the war "effectively over."

Behind the scenes, the Wall Street Journal reports that Vice President JD Vance, who leads the talks with Iran and is one of the most skeptical voices on the war, is weighing a face-to-face meeting with Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, the body that sets Iran's defense policy. Vance sent him a letter last month, and Doha could host. But it's very early, and there's a split. Rubio called Iran's demands "totally unrealistic," and he and the new White House chief of staff, Stephen Miller, want to keep the naval blockade and sanctions until Tehran gives in. Mediators have floated a small first step: the US moves its aircraft carriers farther away and eases the blockade, and Iran lets twenty-five oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz.

Rezaei's answer was blunt. He called the reports of talks "a pure lie," said "not even a particle" of Iran's enriched uranium, nuclear fuel refined close to bomb grade, will leave the country, and warned that if attacked again, Iran could hit American bases in Europe. On the same day, Iran said a tanker caught fire after hitting a mine in the strait. So: two sides talking about talks, and nobody actually talking.

In Ukraine, two days of American-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi ended with no breakthrough, but with one concrete result: a large-scale prisoner exchange, expected within two to three weeks. Jared Kushner said there was progress on security guarantees for after the war, but that territory, meaning which land Russia keeps, will be the hardest part. He and Steve Witkoff now go to Moscow to brief Putin, then meet Zelenskyy in Dublin. Ukraine's negotiator, David Arakhamia, talked of "good momentum" before the end of the year.

Back in America, one more midterm shock. The Associated Press called Mississippi's Senate race for Democrat Scott Colom over Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, after days of counting absentee and provisional ballots. Colom, a local prosecutor, will be Mississippi's first Black senator since Reconstruction, the period right after the Civil War. In a state run mostly by white conservatives for generations, that's huge.

And one Israel link in Washington: twelve senators led by Chris Van Hollen want to strip provisions from the NDAA, the yearly US defense budget law, that would deepen US-Israel defense cooperation, including naming one Pentagon official to coordinate it. They say it would give Israel too much leverage over American weapons programs.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where it was a terrible afternoon in Flatbush, Brooklyn. Just after four p.m. on Wednesday, part of a three-story building on Flatbush Avenue, with a store downstairs and apartments above, collapsed into an open construction site next door, near Bedford Avenue. At least two people were killed and more than a dozen were hurt, several critically, most of them construction workers. Firefighters pulled people out of the rubble, including a woman rescued about two and a half hours later. The Department of Buildings is checking the stability of nearby buildings, and no cause has been given yet. Governor Hochul sent her condolences.

And the Upper West Side lost a legend. Stanley Zabar, co-owner of Zabar's, died on Monday at ninety-four, a year after his brother Saul. His parents, Louis and Lillian, opened the business in nineteen thirty-four as a twenty-two-foot-wide shop selling coffee and fish. Stanley, a lawyer, was the quiet force with the legal and financial skills behind its growth into the institution at Broadway and Eightieth Street. City Council Speaker Julie Menin thanked him "for the bagels, the babka, and the sense of belonging."

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world. Israel's government approved a one hundred million shekel plan, about thirty-one and a half million dollars, to strengthen Jewish education in North America, built with the Jewish Federations of North America. The goal is to get more kids into day schools, full-time Jewish schools, and other programs, and the federations will add half as much again. The background is worrying numbers: a federations survey in February found only one third of American Jews aged eighteen to thirty-four call themselves Zionist, meaning supporters of a Jewish state in the Jewish homeland, even though nearly nine in ten support Israel's right to exist as a Jewish and democratic state.

Here in New York, fewer than a hundred anti-Zionist activists held a "Day of Rage" protest outside Hunter College, demanding the City University of New York divest from Israel and chanting "Globalize the intifada," a call to spread a Palestinian uprising, which many Jews hear as a call for violence.

And something lovely: the National Library of Israel has digitized almost fifty years of The New York Jewish Week and made it free online. Historian Jonathan Sarna calls it "the most important American Jewish newspaper of its time." There's a free event about it next Tuesday at Hebrew Union College in the Village.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the drama at Nvidia got much bigger. On Wednesday, the Wall Street Journal reported that Nvidia's board is preparing to oust CEO Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company in nineteen ninety-three and has run it for thirty-three years. Quick recap: on Sunday, Huang admitted an affair with a woman US officials consider a Chinese spy. Then the New York Times reported a second relationship, with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of health care, who reports directly to him. Reuters says the board met late Wednesday to decide. Its rules let a simple majority of the eleven directors remove him. The problem: Nvidia has no clear successor, and Huang has about sixty people reporting directly to him. The stock fell about eight to nine percent after hours. This matters here, because Nvidia is one of Israel's biggest tech employers, and S and P credits it for much of Israel's growth forecast.

Anthropic, meanwhile, is having the opposite week. Its IPO roadshow, the tour where a company pitches big investors before listing, started on Tuesday, and Bloomberg reports the offering is oversubscribed, investors wanted in for more than offered, several times over. And OpenAI's revenue reportedly topped ten billion dollars in a single quarter for the first time.

Two Israeli notes. Edelbyte, founded by former Microsoft data engineers, came out with seventy million dollars from Sequoia and Quiet Capital for a cheaper way to search huge amounts of company data. And Innoviz, which makes lidar, the laser sensors that help cars see, is cutting about twenty percent of its workforce after revenue fell short.

## Business story

Our business story is about a very Israeli invention. Netafim was born in nineteen sixty-five at Kibbutz Hatzerim in the Negev. A kibbutz is one of Israel's collective farming communities. Netafim turned the idea of drip irrigation, feeding water drop by drop straight to a plant's roots, into a global company. Today it's the world's biggest drip-irrigation maker, with about one point two billion dollars in yearly sales, five thousand employees and nineteen factories, working in one hundred ten countries.

Here's the twist. In twenty seventeen, Orbia, the Mexican chemicals group, bought control at a valuation of about one point nine billion dollars. Now, Calcalist reports, Orbia is close to selling eighty percent of Netafim at an enterprise value, the price for the whole business including debt, of one point two billion. That's roughly a third less. The buyer is MatHolding, a family-owned Spanish company from nineteen thirty-five that already owns two Israeli valve makers and has sold Netafim products in Spain for years. A French investment firm, Tikehau, would help finance it.

And the best part: Kibbutz Hatzerim is buying back in. It sold part of its stake to Orbia in twenty seventeen for two hundred forty million dollars, and now plans to buy ten percent for about eighty million, ending up with about thirty percent. Sell high, buy back lower.

Why did the value fall? Cheaper Chinese competitors, and reports of less spending on research. A Chinese billionaire offered more, about one point four billion, but that bid faced likely US regulatory trouble, since a quarter of Netafim's sales are in America. The lesson: being the inventor of a category doesn't protect you forever if you stop investing in staying ahead.

## Art and design

Next, art, and a mystic is about to make auction history. On November nineteenth, Bukowskis in Stockholm will sell "Ser Three Number Five," a small abstract painting from nineteen twenty by Hilma af Klint, the Swedish artist who painted abstract works years before Kandinsky. It's estimated at six to eight million Swedish crowns, about six hundred to eight hundred thousand dollars. Her current artist record, the highest price ever paid for her work, is only about one hundred sixty-six thousand dollars, so even the low estimate would more than triple it. Only two of her abstract paintings have ever been sold at auction. The painting is full of her symbols: blue and yellow squares for feminine and masculine forces, and a circle over a green triangle from the seal of the Theosophical Society, a spiritual movement she followed.

And a follow-up from Paris: Art Basel will give every gallery at its Paris fair a twenty percent discount on booth fees, after the fair closed two days early in October because of planned protests.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, with a surprise about protein. Researchers at the University of Wisconsin reviewed more than three hundred fifty studies and found that eating less protein extended lifespan in flies and mice, and in some human trials led to weight loss and better blood sugar. They point to certain amino acids, the building blocks of protein, that may drive inflammation. But be careful: this doesn't prove it works for human lifespan, and older adults, pregnant women and people who train hard often need more protein, not less. The practical idea: if you're not very active and you're adding protein powder to everything, you may simply not need it all.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a quieter day for big rounds, so today's money story is about who's buying, and what an old deal is worth now.

First, an acquisition. Cogent, a San Francisco startup that calls itself an applied AI lab, bought Specular. Cogent's AI agents find security holes inside a company and fix them. It's backed by Greylock and Bain Capital Ventures, and its founders come from Google DeepMind, Abnormal Security and Coinbase. Specular, backed by Google, does the opposite side: its AI agents look at a company from the open internet the way a hacker would, find exposed systems, and test whether the weak spots can really be used to break in. That's offensive security, attacking your own systems to find holes. The price wasn't disclosed. Specular's founder, Peyton Smith, who used to lead red teaming, the ethical hackers who test defenses, at CrowdStrike, becomes Cogent's head of offensive security. Cogent's CEO, Vineet Edupuganti, put the pitch in one line: "Defenders can't keep up by adding more dashboards, alerts, and manual processes." The pattern is familiar from the last few weeks: buyers want one clear capability and the team behind it.

Second, a number that tells you a lot about Israeli exits. Calcalist looked at Palo Alto Networks' purchase of CyberArk, the Israeli identity security giant. The headline price was twenty-five billion dollars. When the deal closed in February, its accounting value was about twenty-one billion, because most of it was paid in Palo Alto shares. At Tuesday's closing price, those shares are worth about thirty-seven billion, almost double, even though Palo Alto's stock dropped about twenty-two percent in the past month. So CyberArk holders who kept their stock did very well. The marketer's angle: when the deal closed, about seven hundred jobs were cut, including around a hundred in Israel, mostly in support roles, but also in marketing and sales, and Calcalist reported the two companies' marketing and sales teams would run separately for six to nine months. That window is closing about now, which usually means another round of reorganizing.

Third, a smaller round on the edge of our world. Lumbra, based in Washington and New York and founded by a former CIA officer, raised a twenty-five million dollar Series A led by Point seventy-two Ventures and Ansa. It runs AI agents on top-secret networks for the Pentagon and US intelligence, recording every action and tracing every claim to its source. One of its investors named the real sales obstacle: "Trust and verifiability remain one of the largest barriers to relying on AI agents."

And Palo Alto's talks to buy Clutch Security, the Israeli startup that protects the credentials AI agents use, for about five hundred million dollars, still aren't signed. If it happens, it would be the latest Israeli company to land inside the same buyer that now owns CyberArk. We'll keep watching.

## People moves

Now people moves, and we start with an Israeli company you know. Torq, the Israeli-founded company that uses AI to automate security operations, named Peter Polizzi president and COO, chief operating officer, the person who runs day-to-day operations. He'll oversee operations, product and go-to-market. Polizzi comes from HashiCorp, where he helped scale the technical field team and played a key role in its six point four billion dollar sale to IBM, and before that Splunk and Palo Alto Networks. Torq says its plan is to double in size every year, and it reports one hundred fifty percent growth in revenue through partners. Remember, Torq's marketing chief, Don Jeter, left for Abnormal AI last week.

Next, a career path to notice. Expel, an American MDR company, that's managed detection and response, round-the-clock attack monitoring, named Steve Kelley chairman and CEO. He replaces co-founder Dave Merkel, who ran it for about ten years and stays on the board. Merkel's line was classy: "Knowing when to hand off is part of the job." Here's the part for you: Kelley was once the chief marketing officer at Trustwave, during its repositioning before its sale to Singtel, the Singapore telecom giant. He later turned around a seven-hundred-person unit at Bitdefender and ran EdgeWave until its sale. So yes, marketing leaders do become cyber CEOs.

Two new CMOs. Hadrian, an Amsterdam-based startup whose AI agents hack companies to find weak spots, and which raised forty million dollars in October, named Eugenia Kendrick chief marketing officer. She comes from Censys, the company that maps everything exposed on the internet. And AprosSyber named Elizabeth Sweet-Wilson CMO, coming from CYPFER, a cyber incident response firm.

On the revenue side, Netwrix, a data and identity security company with more than thirteen thousand customers, hired Tony Orlando as CRO, coming from Google, where he led go-to-market teams for cloud, AI and security. And Netskope, the cloud security company, named Carrie Francey senior vice president of global channel sales.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, with three lessons.

First, partnership marketing, Israeli style. Orca Security and Aryon Security, both Israeli, connected their products after a joint hackathon, a short coding marathon, and say the systems were talking to each other in under twenty-four hours. Orca finds cloud risks. Aryon stops them from coming back. And they explained it with one concrete example anyone gets: you fix a database that was accidentally open to the internet, and Aryon makes sure nobody creates a new one with the same mistake. A speed story plus a simple example beats a page of feature lists.

Second, notice how companies turn research into headlines. Netwrix's press release about a sales hire was packed with stats from its own survey: companies where AI multiplied the number of accounts needing access had a forty-three percent breach rate, versus eleven percent elsewhere. And Gravitee, an API company, published a survey of nine hundred tech leaders: fewer than one in ten organizations has one named person accountable for its AI agents, and only six percent can cut off a rogue agent within an hour. Netwrix added that only nineteen percent of companies fully govern non-human identities, the identities of apps and machines, like AI agents. Those numbers will end up in sales decks all quarter, and every startup in agent security will quote them in its pitch. If you write the report everyone else cites, you set the terms of the conversation. Owning the stat everyone quotes is one of the cheapest ways to own a category.

Third, the channel. Torq's growth through partners came after it launched a partner program in October with very generous terms: partners keep one hundred percent of the margin on their services, get deal protection, and are onboarded in ten days. When a company makes partners this much money, partner marketing becomes a growth engine, not a side job.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Torq. A new president in charge of go-to-market, a plan to double every year, a fast-growing partner channel, and a marketing chief who just left. That's a company that will be rebuilding its marketing leadership soon.

Second, Aryon. An Israeli company that raised a twenty-nine million dollar Series A in June and is now building partnerships. That's exactly when a company needs its first strong product and partner marketer.

Third, Hadrian. A brand-new CMO at a startup that just raised forty million dollars usually means a new marketing team, and with customers in both the US and Europe, there may be room for someone who knows how Israeli cyber companies sell.
