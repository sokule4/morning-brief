## Cold open

Good morning Sophie, it's Thursday, October fifteenth, and here's your Morning Brief. Three big stories today. ## Headlines

We start with security, and the election, which is now just twelve days away. On Wednesday, the army's chief of staff, Lieutenant General Eyal Zamir, visited Central Command, the headquarters responsible for the West Bank. He warned of what he called the "potential for escalation" on all fronts before the vote on October twenty-seventh.

Here's what that looks like on the ground. The army has sent the ninety-eighth Division, one of its big combat divisions, into the West Bank as reinforcements. Zamir called the olive harvest a sensitive period, and he was blunt about settler violence. He said it "must be condemned and eradicated using every tool available to the State of Israel." That's strong language from an army chief.

Netanyahu, for his part, warned that Iran may try to disrupt the election, either directly or through Hamas or Hezbollah, the Iran-backed militia in Lebanon. He said of Iran's regime, "This regime must go." But he also said an attack on Iran before the vote would not be "convenient." That's interesting timing, because it comes a day after the Channel Twelve report we told you about, that he had pressed President Trump to restart the Iran war before the election.

And there's more from Washington. Axios reported that Zamir told the top US general, Dan Caine, that full-scale fighting could resume within three weeks, and that this could even lead to Israel's election being postponed. One US official told Axios, "Bibi seems to want to go in again." But a White House official said, "The President has said that we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections." Meanwhile Trump, at a rally in Alaska, said, "Wait until you see what we do to Iran." And asked whether he'd endorse anyone in Israel's election, he said, "We haven't even thought about it. I may be doing something."

Next, October seventh is moving into the courtroom. Ronen Bar, the former head of the Shin Bet, filed a defamation lawsuit, meaning a suit over false, damaging statements, against Netanyahu. He's asking for three million shekels. It's about a post on Netanyahu's X account from late September that accused Bar of "failures and crimes."

Bar says he warned Netanyahu again and again before the attack. His filing quotes him telling the prime minister in July twenty twenty-three, "I am giving you a strategic warning of war." He also argues Netanyahu shouldn't get parliamentary immunity, the legal protection lawmakers get for things said in office. Bar said, "In the absence of a state commission of inquiry, the truth will be established in court." That's the independent investigation, led by a judge, that Netanyahu has resisted. Netanyahu's reply, on Telegram: "Ronen, are you serious? You had my secure phone number and could have called me immediately."

Now the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran is hitting the world economy without firing at Israel. On Tuesday, a Singapore-flagged container ship, the GFS Pride, was hit by a projectile and caught fire. According to the shipping news site gCaptain, it sank. All twenty-one crew got off safely. A day later, its sister ship, the GFS Giselle, was hit too, but made it to a safe anchorage. The International Maritime Organization counts more than a hundred incidents and at least twenty-four sailors killed since the war began in February. CENTCOM, the US military command for the Middle East, insists, quote, "American forces and regional partners clearly control the Strait of Hormuz."

The money side: Brent crude, the global benchmark oil price, is around a hundred and five dollars a barrel, up from ninety-eight at the end of September. In America, gas averages four dollars and thirty-three cents a gallon, compared to about three dollars before the war. That flows straight into prices everywhere, including in Israel.

To the north, Defense Minister Israel Katz, speaking inside the army's buffer zone in Lebanon, the strip of land Israel holds past the border, issued a warning to Hezbollah. If it joins an Iranian attack, he said, Hezbollah "will be dealt a heavy blow and lose additional territory in southern Lebanon," which would become part of a permanent Israeli security zone. He even said Beirut's Dahiyeh neighborhood, Hezbollah's stronghold, would have to be evacuated again.

And the Houthis. Debris from intercepted Houthi missiles wounded four people in Riyadh, including a child, and damaged fifteen homes. One more worrying detail, from the Wall Street Journal: the Houthis are reportedly building a version of an Iranian missile with Chinese parts, with a range of about nineteen hundred miles. That's enough to reach Israel.

Some good diplomatic news. Colombia opened its embassy in Jerusalem on Wednesday, the tenth country to do so. Colombia's new right-wing president, Abelardo de la Espriella, is reversing his predecessor Gustavo Petro, who cut ties with Israel in twenty twenty-four. A free trade agreement, a deal that removes taxes on goods between countries, was also reinstated. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar said, "In just two months, we have reversed most of the negative consequences of the Petro administration."

In Gaza, a year into the ceasefire, the army says it killed a Hamas commander in Gaza City who it says held hostages. And the Guardian reports Israel still hasn't allowed construction of a base for a hundred and fifty international troops under the Board of Peace, the international body overseeing Gaza's future. The Board reportedly expects approval only after the election.

Finally, the polls. A Channel Twelve poll this week has Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party at twenty-three seats and Likud at twenty. The opposition bloc gets fifty-four, Netanyahu's bloc forty-nine. Other polls are tighter, around fifty-two each. But the key point: nobody reaches sixty-one, the majority you need in the Knesset. And a fight over surplus-vote deals, agreements where two parties share leftover votes, got personal. Itamar Ben Gvir refused a deal with Ofer Winter, calling him "Bennett two point oh." Winter's reply: "Itamar, grow up."

## New York

Next, back home in New York, where an iconic tourist scene is about to disappear.

The City Council plans to vote next Thursday, October twenty-second, on a bill to phase out the horse carriage industry by June twenty twenty-eight. It's called Romanch's Law, named for Romanch Mahajan, an eighteen-year-old tourist from India who died in June after being thrown from a carriage when the horse bolted. The bill would stop new licenses, ban carriages in Central Park, and create a job transition program for drivers.

Mayor Mamdani had doubts at first, but now he's on board. He said, "The horse carriage industry is coming to an end in New York City." Council Speaker Julie Menin is pushing it too. The drivers' union is fighting back. Its international president, John Samuelsen, says the bill offers "no meaningful economic relief for workers."

Next, a story that's both New York and Jewish world. City Comptroller Mark Levine, the official who manages the city's pension money, says he won't reinvest that money in Israel Bonds, the bonds Israel sells to raise money abroad. That breaks a promise he made in his twenty twenty-five campaign. Levine, who is Jewish, told the New York Times the decision was "based on financial considerations only, not politics." Mayor Mamdani praised it, saying the city doesn't buy bonds from any other country and shouldn't make an exception for Israel. Why it matters: Levine was seen by many Jewish New Yorkers as a moderate counterweight to the mayor, and on this one, he's landed in the same place.

Mamdani also hit back at Bruce Blakeman, the Republican running for governor, who called the mayor an antisemite during Tuesday's debate. Mamdani called Blakeman's attack "directly racist." Early voting starts October twenty-fourth, and Election Day is November third. The latest Siena poll, from late September, had Governor Hochul ahead fifty to forty-one.

On the subway, a scary one. Early Wednesday morning, around two thirty, a forty-year-old man was stabbed several times on an uptown six train near Astor Place, after an argument. The attacker wore a ski mask and got away. The victim is in stable condition at Bellevue. Subway safety is exactly the issue Blakeman is running on.

And sports. The Rangers are rolling even without their star goalie. They beat Tampa Bay four to one at the Garden, with two goals from Alexis Lafrenière, who has now scored in four straight games. They're six and two, and perfect at home. The Islanders beat Vancouver three to two, with Kyle Palmieri scoring his first two goals since coming back from a knee injury. And mark your calendar: next Tuesday, October twentieth, the Knicks open their season at the Garden against the seventy-sixers, and as defending champions, they'll raise their first championship banner since nineteen seventy-three. It's also LeBron James's debut for Philadelphia.

## Jewish world

Now, the Jewish world, starting with a wave of hoax threats.

Over the past week, four Jewish day schools, meaning full-time Jewish schools, got threats. In Brooklyn, the Yeshivah of Flatbush high school and United Talmudical Academy in Williamsburg were threatened about two minutes apart last Friday and evacuated. On Monday, a Jewish preschool in Boca Raton was evacuated, and the Milken Community School in Los Angeles went into lockdown after a call first reported as an active shooter. All turned out to be hoaxes. Congressman Jared Moskowitz said, "Let's be clear: there is no cause in the world that's served by scaring children."

And it's not just schools. On Tuesday, a Delta flight from New York was evacuated after landing at Ben Gurion, after an anonymous report of a suspicious object. Nothing was found. That evening, Delta's JFK to Tel Aviv flight was held after a bomb threat call, and only left after American and Israeli security cleared it. Authorities suspect the same caller was behind both.

Next, Italy. A network of Jewish early-childhood teachers called Paradigm Project cancelled a study trip to Reggio Emilia for sixty-eight Jewish educators. Reggio Emilia is famous in the education world for its approach to teaching young children. The reason: leaked audio aired on Italian TV, in which the head of the local education organization discussed having participants sign a statement including the line, "I distance myself from the occupation of Gaza." The organization says that was only discussion material. The head of Manhattan Day School, Pesha Kletenik, said, "I never felt so targeted as a Jew before in my life." The local Jewish community said treating Diaspora Jews as stand-ins for Israel's government is antisemitic.

Back to Israel's election, with a pitch to Jews abroad. Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid's joint party, Together, says it would make Israel-Diaspora ties a top priority. Diaspora Jews would elect a non-voting observer to the Knesset, and Robinson's Arch, the mixed-prayer section at the Western Wall, would stay open. Lapid put it this way: "Israel's relationship with world Jewry isn't merely of critical strategic importance; they are our family."

In Britain, a follow-up to the Green Party's "Zionism is racism" motion. At PMQs, Prime Minister's Questions, the weekly grilling in Parliament, Prime Minister Andy Burnham said of the motion, "It is wrong, it is antisemitic." And he ruled out any partnership with the Greens.

And to end on something lovely. Venice's Jewish Museum reopens next Thursday after six years and a nine million euro renovation, on the main square of the Ghetto, Europe's first Jewish ghetto. It was founded in nineteen fifty-three by Holocaust survivors, and it's almost doubling in size. And in Australia, Daniella Gilboa, the twenty-two-year-old freed Israeli hostage, will headline concerts called "Unbreakable" in Melbourne and Sydney later this month, singing her debut single.

## Tech, startups and AI

Now, tech, startups and AI, starting with a very big check for a very young Israeli company.

Axonica Labs is a new chip company in Herzliya, founded just last June by Assaf Touboul, who was a vice president of technology at Qualcomm. Globes reports it's raising a pre-seed round, the very first money a startup raises, of up to eighty million dollars. That's huge for a first round. Fifty million was committed before it even closed. Investors include Qualcomm Ventures, Amplify Partners, Wing, and Israeli funds Pitango, Viola and 10D. The company is building an inference chip, a chip designed to run AI models after they're trained, and it claims it'll be much cheaper and use less power than Nvidia's chips. Globes did add a note of caution: two Israeli chip companies, Pliops and Hailo, reportedly sold for hundreds of millions less than their valuations. Why it matters to you: new hardware companies need partnerships and sales people early.

Next, Zenzap, a Tel Aviv startup that wants to be "WhatsApp for work," with AI agents built in. TechCrunch reports it has raised forty-four million dollars in total, including a thirty-three million dollar Series A, its first big funding round, co-led by Bessemer and Lightspeed. The clever part is the target customer: what's called the deskless workforce, people who don't sit at a computer all day. Its customers include FedEx, Starbucks, Taco Bell and Dollar General. CEO Guy Weiss said, "We are targeting non-tech organizations that Slack hasn't reached." His pitch is that you can't use WhatsApp at work with an AI agent on top, because IT departments won't allow it. That's a great example of a positioning line, one sentence that explains why you exist.

Now globally. Meta released Muse Wonder, its first frontier model, meaning one of the most advanced AI models out there. It comes from Meta's new Superintelligence Lab, and Meta says it scores about the same as the top models from OpenAI and Anthropic. The big selling point is memory. One Meta researcher described the goal as "making sure your model can remember what's important to you over days, weeks, months, and beyond." The price is four dollars per million tokens going in, tokens being the small chunks of text AI reads, roughly the same as competitors. So in one week, Google and Meta both launched top models. AI that remembers you is going to be in every pitch you hear this year.

A job market story. Netflix is cutting about a thousand jobs, around six percent of its staff, mostly in product, technology, legal and HR. That's a profitable company with forty-five billion dollars of revenue last year. Netflix says it will "continue to invest aggressively in high impact areas."

Something for your art agency side. AI music is getting a backlash. Deezer, the music streaming service, will let users filter out AI-generated songs, and YouTube Music is adding labels to AI tracks. Expect the same debate in visual art: buyers will increasingly want to know what was made by a human.

And quick hits. Japan will invest up to twenty-one billion dollars in two GlobalFoundries chip plants in upstate New York, with Apple expected as a customer. ASML, the Dutch company that makes the machines used to print computer chips, reported record revenue of eleven point seven billion euros for the quarter.

## Cybersecurity and GTM

Next, cybersecurity and GTM, and a record that tells you where the whole industry is heading.

On Tuesday, Microsoft had its biggest Patch Tuesday ever. Patch Tuesday is Microsoft's monthly batch of security fixes. This month: more than fourteen hundred fixes. SecurityWeek says that's more than Microsoft patched in all of twenty twenty-five. Seventy are rated critical. One is a zero-day, a flaw hackers were already using before a fix existed. It lets a booby-trapped file get past the warning Windows shows on files downloaded from the internet. Interesting detail: it was reported by Fujitsu, an independent researcher, and Anthropic. CISA, America's cyber defense agency, told federal agencies to patch it by Friday.

Why so many? AI is finding bugs faster than ever. And here's your interview line: when AI finds vulnerabilities faster than teams can fix them, the companies that help you decide what to fix first become essential. Microsoft itself introduced fifteen new "risk cohorts" to help customers prioritize. That's the pitch of a whole category, called exposure management.

Next, a company that's hiring sales people. Harmonic Security, based in San Francisco and London, raised a fifty million dollar Series B, a growth-stage round, led by Crosspoint Capital. It was oversubscribed, meaning investors wanted in for more than was offered. Harmonic watches and controls how employees use AI tools at work. It has more than a hundred enterprise customers, went from thirty to more than ninety employees in two years, and says the money goes to engineering, sales and marketing in the US and UK. It also just signed new distributors. CEO Alastair Paterson said, "Every board I talk to is asking two questions: how do we reduce the risk of AI use, and are we getting value?" Remember Pluto Security from last week? Same space. That's a hot category.

A breach that hit grocery shopping on two continents. Ahold Delhaize, the Dutch-Belgian company behind Stop and Shop, Food Lion, Hannaford and Giant, took its websites offline after a cyberattack on Sunday. Online ordering and home delivery stopped, though stores stayed open. In the Netherlands, its Albert Heijn chain suspended app discounts. And on the same Sunday, a separate attack in France heated supermarket fridges at hundreds of stores to about fifty degrees Celsius for six hours. Retail is now a serious buyer of security.

A darker trend. The security company Semperis surveyed twelve hundred organizations hit by ransomware, and found criminals are now threatening people, not just computers. A quarter of victims had personal details posted online, called doxxing. Twenty-three percent were swatted, meaning someone made a fake emergency call to send armed police to their home. One Semperis expert described getting an unsigned package of gifts during an investigation: "We-know-where-you-are kind of stuff." His advice: "Never pay them." And a GTM note: this is how security companies market themselves. A big research report gets headlines, and headlines become leads.

Now sales tools. At its big conference this week, Seismic, the sales content platform, launched AI agents that flag risky deals and find outdated sales materials. CEO Rob Tarkoff said, "AI gives teams speed, but speed without control is not a growth strategy." And a new G2 report found two-thirds of companies now run AI agents in real work, but almost a third say staff spend six or more hours a week supervising them. G2 calls that the "distrust tax." Another finding: only nine percent of buyers think per-seat pricing, paying per user, is the fairest model. Expect pricing to shift toward paying for results or usage.

And from SaaStr, the big startup sales community: it now runs most of its outbound with AI SDRs, sales development reps, the people who book first meetings, but it built its own tool for its top accounts, where a human approves every pitch. Founder Jason Lemkin said that for top accounts, "it can't be bought." Translation for your job search: the human sales jobs are moving toward the biggest, most complex deals. One warning story, too: former Snowflake CRO, chief revenue officer, Chris Degnan joined the AI coding company Cognition, and a rival CEO accused him of sharing confidential information. Degnan denies it, and Cognition rejected the claims.

## Business story

Time for today's business story: Zach Yadegari, the Long Island teenager who built a calorie app in his high school classroom, sold it, and is now raising money from an Israeli fund for his next act.

Zach started coding at seven. In ninth grade, he built a website called Totally Science that let students get around school internet blocks to play games. He sold it for about a hundred thousand dollars when he was sixteen.

Then came college applications, and a twist. Fifteen of the eighteen universities he applied to rejected him, including Harvard, Yale and Stanford, despite reportedly near-perfect grades and test scores. He ended up at the University of Miami.

By then, he was already building Cal AI with a teenage co-founder, Henry Langmack. The idea is simple: take a photo of your meal, and AI estimates the calories. It took off. More than fifteen million downloads. MyFitnessPal told TechCrunch that Cal AI was making over thirty million dollars a year in under two years, and Zach has cited more than forty million in sales over twelve months. With a team of about seven people.

And here's the best part. Cal AI started passing MyFitnessPal, the giant of calorie counting since two thousand five, in App Store rankings. So the incumbent did what incumbents often do: instead of competing, it bought the upstart, in March of this year, for an undisclosed price. MyFitnessPal's CEO said, "We've been incredibly impressed with Cal AI's speed, agility, and what they've built."

Zach left in June, and now the news. TechCrunch reports he has raised ten million dollars for a new company called Persona, a personal AI assistant, and the round was led by the Israeli venture firm Vine Ventures. It lives in your iMessage for now, and there's a hundred and seventy-nine dollar wristband coming in December. It can order food and rides, book travel and answer email, and it asks your approval before buying anything. His pitch: "It can offer help and solve problems before you even know you have a problem." He's also speaking about viral growth this week at TechCrunch Disrupt in San Francisco.

Three lessons for you. One: distribution beats pedigree. Fifteen rejections didn't stop him, because he knew how to get an app in front of millions of people. Two: big companies often buy what they can't build fast enough. If you're small and growing, the competitor can become your buyer. And three: the second company is much easier to fund. Once you've shown you can win, investors come to you, including Israeli ones.

## Wellness

And finally, one wellness idea for today: make some of your workouts short and hard. A study from the University of the Sunshine Coast in Australia followed a hundred and twenty-three older adults, average age seventy-two, through six months of supervised gym sessions, three times a week. Some did high-intensity interval training, called HIIT, short bursts of hard effort with rest in between. Others did moderate or easy workouts. All the groups lost some fat, including visceral fat, the deep belly fat around your organs. But only the HIIT group kept all its muscle. The lead author said, "only HIIT retained lean muscle." The researchers say the differences were modest, but the idea is easy to use at any age: once or twice a week, swap a steady workout for intervals, like one minute fast, one minute easy, repeated. Muscle is something you want to keep for life.
