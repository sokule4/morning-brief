## Cold open

Good morning Sophie. It's Saturday, November twenty-eighth, and this is your Morning Brief.

Here are today's three biggest stories. Netanyahu is reportedly ready to let Gadi Eisenkot serve first as prime minister in a shared deal, and Eisenkot says he'll form a government within eight days anyway. In the Gulf, American aircraft disabled a ship trying to break the blockade of Iran, and Iran says two tankers hit mines. And back home, after a storm pushed it off Thanksgiving for the first time in a hundred years, the Macy's parade marches this morning.

## Israel

We start here in Israel, where the coalition race got a surprise twist late Friday night.

According to Channel 13, as reported by the Times of Israel, Benjamin Netanyahu is preparing to say he'd accept being the second prime minister in a rotation, with Eisenkot going first. That's a big shift. Until now, Likud's line has been that Netanyahu leads. The offer has reportedly been passed on unofficially, and Likud itself is divided over it. Eisenkot's camp says it's "highly skeptical."

Why skeptical? Because of what Eisenkot said earlier on Friday. He told reporters that Likud's team has met his five times in two weeks with no progress, and he accused Likud of offering "the same government, the same partners, no change, no conscription law." Conscription means the draft, and that's the core fight: whether young Haredi men who study full time in yeshivas will finally serve in the army. Eisenkot then made a promise that raises the stakes. He said he aims to form a government within eight days, and that he will not ask President Herzog for the extra fourteen days the law allows.

His partners pull in different directions. Avigdor Liberman and Yair Golan have both ruled out sitting with Netanyahu, and Channel 12 reports Eisenkot told them he won't form a unity government without their consent. Liberman's view is blunt: "What Netanyahu wants more than anything is new elections." The Knesset speaker, Matan Kahana from Eisenkot's own party, shares that suspicion, that Netanyahu is stalling to force another vote. On the other side, Naftali Bennett still prefers a unity government with Likud, with Eisenkot first in the rotation. Colleagues quote him calling a minority government "a crappy government," and Channel 13 says Bennett might even vote against an Eisenkot government formed before talks with Netanyahu are exhausted. Talks with Bennett's party were pushed to Sunday.

So what is Eisenkot building instead? A Times of Israel analysis says Liberman, in what it calls a "spectacular flip-flop," has become the architect of a minority government, one that holds about fifty-five seats and relies on the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism and the Arab party Ra'am supporting it from outside. A Liberman associate put it this way: "We will come to the president with fifty-five seats and a solid Jewish majority." The irony: Liberman once tried to get Ra'am banned from running.

And tonight is a big moment for that plan. Ra'am's leader, Mansour Abbas, is set to say on Channel 12 that he recognizes Hamas as a terror organization. Eisenkot requires that of every partner. Abbas said, "I've reconciled myself to it," and Channel 12 reports he promised "full coalition discipline," meaning his party votes with the government, even on security decisions. In return, Ra'am wants cabinet posts, a plan against crime in Arab towns, and a five-year development plan worth tens of billions of shekels. Netanyahu's camp attacked hard. Defense Minister Israel Katz said Abbas and Hamas have "completely identical" strategies, and Likud called the statement "lip service."

There are cracks on the religious side too. United Torah Judaism reportedly vetoed Gilad Kariv of the Democrats, a Reform rabbi, as education minister, and Eisenkot reportedly agreed. And Shas is split. Aryeh Deri was quoted saying that if the Shas rabbis force his party to support a new government, "it will be very difficult for me to remain in politics." What's next: Ra'am's statement tonight, the Bennett talks on Sunday, and Eisenkot's own eight-day clock.

Now Gaza. On Friday, Israel reportedly struck an apartment in Gaza City targeting Ezzedine al-Hayya, the son of Hamas's chief, Khalil al-Hayya. He survived, and six people were reportedly wounded. The army also said it killed ten operatives in Gaza in the past week, four of them October seventh attackers, accusing them of "systematic violation of the ceasefire." Since the ceasefire began in October twenty twenty-five, Hamas-run health officials say more than one thousand six hundred Palestinians have been killed, a figure that can't be verified, and seven Israeli soldiers and a Defense Ministry contractor have been killed. Under American pressure, the security cabinet meets on Sunday to move forward on the stalled Trump plan, including bases for the International Stabilization Force, foreign troops meant to keep order in Gaza, which could deploy along the Yellow Line in January.

In the West Bank, Katz rejected a request from the army chief, Eyal Zamir, to use administrative detention, holding someone without charges, against violent settlers. Channel 12 says seventy suspects were arrested recently and all were released without charges. And inside Israel, eight people were hurt in Kafr Qasim when a driver rammed people four times. Police suspect a racist motive, after he allegedly said he wanted to run over Arabs.

One wallet note: the shekel recently weakened to about three point three four to the dollar, its weakest since September last year, and home prices keep slipping, down about one and a half percent from a year ago.

## World

Now to the world, and the war with Iran at sea.

On Thursday, American aircraft fired what Central Command called "precision munitions" at the back of a cargo ship, the Pacific Archer, in the Gulf of Oman. It had left the Iranian port of Bandar Abbas, listed as heading for Singapore. Its engines were disabled, and the crew was unharmed. Some background: the war began in February with US and Israeli strikes, and the US runs a naval blockade, stopping ships from reaching or leaving Iran, to squeeze its economy. Since July, a hundred fifty-eight ships have been turned around, and this was at least the fifteenth ship disabled since April.

Iran is pushing back. The Revolutionary Guard says two tankers took an "unauthorized" route and caught fire after hitting naval mines, one near Oman's Khasab port and one in the eastern Strait of Hormuz. Its story changed during the day, and none of it has been independently confirmed. Iran's navy commander said Iran will act to break the blockade.

President Trump, speaking to troops on Thanksgiving, said "we have victory at hand," and that the war will end "whenever I want it to be." He also said that if the US strikes again, "we will definitely hit Pickaxe Mountain," an underground Iranian nuclear site, while adding, "We're giving them every chance to make a deal." Qatar and Pakistan are still passing messages between the two sides.

And a follow-up on Thursday's deadly drone attack in Kuwait, which killed two Italians. Italy's foreign minister, Antonio Tajani, said Saturday that Kuwaiti investigators confirmed the drone was made in Iran. Tehran still denies launching it and has offered to join an investigation with Italy and Kuwait. Kuwait summoned Iran's ambassador, and UN chief António Guterres condemned the attack.

Now Ukraine. In an interview with the BBC, President Zelenskyy criticized Trump for posting a photo hugging Vladimir Putin and calling him "my friend." Zelenskyy said: "He's an aggressor, and a killer. He can't be a friend. Just cannot be." His bigger worry is practical. He says Ukraine has a "very, very big" shortage of Patriot interceptors, the American missiles that shoot down incoming missiles, because the US is focused on the Middle East. "The deficit of these missiles, that is people's lives," he said. Peace talks are expected to resume in December. And one sci-fi detail: Ukraine says a laser weapon called Blysk shot down a jet-powered Russian drone for the first time.

## New York

Next, back home in New York, and finally, parade day. After Thursday's storm, with gusts of fifty-four miles an hour in Central Park and sixty-six at LaGuardia, Macy's moved its one hundredth Thanksgiving Day Parade to this morning, the first time in history weather pushed it off the holiday. It starts at eight thirty New York time, three thirty in the afternoon here, on NBC and Peacock. The forecast is dry, with gusts around twenty-five miles an hour, under the limit for flying the giant balloons. Macy's added LL Cool J and Gloria Gaynor to the lineup, and says ninety to ninety-five percent of performers could stay. The best story is the bands. A high school band from Pearland, Texas, with more than five hundred students, raised an extra three hundred fifty thousand dollars in under a day to stay in the city. A Hawaii all-state band raised over seven hundred thousand. Alabama's famous Million Dollar Band went home for its big football game.

Mayor Mamdani will be watching from his couch. He had surgery on Friday for the torn Achilles tendon, the tendon at the back of the ankle, from his soccer game against the press. His office called it successful and says he's resting at Gracie Mansion, the mayor's official home. Before going in, he admitted, "I'm scared, it's my first surgery."

And a big win for him in court. On Wednesday, a Manhattan judge upheld the rent freeze voted by the Rent Guidelines Board, the city panel that sets yearly rent increases, for about one million rent-stabilized apartments, the units where the law caps rent hikes. Landlords had sued. The judge said the freeze "may cause negative collateral consequences," but it's legal. Mamdani called it "a victory for tenants." Small landlords say their taxes and insurance aren't frozen, and an appeal is possible.

## Jewish world

Now the Jewish world, and a security story from Germany. On Thursday, more than two hundred fifty officers searched seventeen properties and arrested two Iraqi men over plots to bomb synagogues, in Cologne and another European city. Prosecutors say people went to both synagogues on two nights in March, presumably carrying explosives, but the attack never happened, "for reasons that are still unknown." They link the cell to a founder of an Iran-backed group that has claimed attacks on Jewish sites across Europe this year.

That's the backdrop as Hanukkah approaches; it starts on the evening of Friday, December fourth. France's interior minister ordered police to step up security around synagogues and Hanukkah events, citing "the high level of the terrorist threat." In Paris, a Hanukkah show with Israeli singers was canceled this week over security. And Australia sanctioned two alleged Iranian operatives over the twenty twenty-four arson attacks on a Sydney kosher restaurant and a Melbourne synagogue.

And in the US, Jewish leaders are angry at NBC for running an anti-Israel ad during an SNL Thanksgiving special on Wednesday. The thirty-second ad contrasts an American girl saving four hundred dollars for a bike with a girl in Gaza who lost a leg. The ADL's Jonathan Greenblatt called it "indefensible," and William Daroff of the Conference of Presidents called it a "shanda," Yiddish for a disgrace. NBC hasn't responded.

## Tech, startups and AI

Next, tech, and the bill for Israel's biggest exit ever. Globes reports that the Israel Tax Authority is demanding billions of shekels more from the four founders of Wiz, which Google bought for thirty-two billion dollars. Globes' headline says twelve billion shekels more, though the article mentions a total demand of twenty billion. About eight billion has been paid so far, and sources expect a compromise around twelve or thirteen billion. The fights are technical but huge. One is the exchange rate: the founders want the rate from March, when the deal closed; the Tax Authority wants an earlier one, when the dollar was worth about fifteen percent more. Another is where the technology was really created, in Israel or in an American company. Why it matters: the same questions will hit the Decart founders after Amazon's deal, and how this ends will shape whether founders keep building here.

And a startup that became a unicorn in six months. Marlowe AI, founded in May, raised a two hundred seventy-seven million dollar seed round at a one point one five billion dollar valuation, led by Accel. It's an American company backed by two Israeli brothers, Lior Susan, founder of the fund Eclipse, and Ido Susan, founder of DriveNets. It builds data centers for AI inference, the stage where AI answers your questions, and it wants to cut the industry's dependence on Nvidia chips. Its first data center could open as soon as December.

## Business story

Our business story is Black Friday, and the results are in. Adobe, which tracks online sales at big US retailers, says Americans spent a record seven point one billion dollars online on Thanksgiving Day, up about ten percent. By the evening of Black Friday, they had spent nine point seven billion, and Adobe raised its full-day forecast to a record of about thirteen billion. Cyber Monday, the online shopping day after the holiday weekend, is expected to hit about fifteen billion. Sixty-three percent of Thanksgiving spending happened on phones.

The number a marketer should circle: traffic from AI tools to retail sites jumped a hundred sixty-two percent. And those shoppers were seventy-four percent more likely to buy, what marketers call converting, than everyone else. Meanwhile, foot traffic in physical stores was down about two percent. The National Retail Federation expects holiday sales to pass one trillion dollars for the first time. As one Conference Board economist put it, "The bargain hunt was part of the budget." The lesson: people are still shopping, they're just starting with a chatbot.

## Art and design

Next, art, and a huge new museum opens today in Brussels. Kanal is a former Citroën car garage and showroom from nineteen thirty-four, on the city's canal, turned into a forty-thousand-square-meter art complex, with more than half of it free public space. It includes a museum run with Paris's Centre Pompidou, and its opening show has three hundred fifty works. But it's controversial. The cost grew from a hundred fifty million euros to about two hundred thirty million, its yearly budget is being cut, and its managing director leaves on Monday with no successor named.

And a story every art dealer dreams about. A self-portrait sold at Sotheby's New York in twenty twenty-four for under seventy thousand dollars, labeled only "attributed to Titian," meaning experts weren't sure he painted it. After cleaning and X-rays, some scholars now believe it's really by Titian, the great Venetian Renaissance painter. If they're right, it's one of only three accepted Titian self-portraits. It's now for sale in London for one and a half million pounds. For your agency, that's the power of research: the same painting, a new story, twenty times the price.

## Wellness

And finally, wellness, and a twist on exercise and the brain. A big review of seventy-four studies with more than four million people in thirty countries, published this month in The Lancet Public Health and led by the University of Southern California, found that people most active in their free time, walking, cycling, swimming, sports, had about a twenty-four percent lower risk of dementia. But people whose jobs were the most physically demanding, heavy lifting and standing all day, had about a twenty percent higher risk. It shows a link, not proof of cause, and stress and lack of control at work may explain part of it. As the senior author put it, "the context of physical activity may matter just as much as movement itself." So the movement that counts is the kind you choose and enjoy. A Saturday walk counts.

## Part 2: Cyber Watch

## Funding and acquisitions

It's a very quiet Thanksgiving week for cyber money. Calcalist's running list of Israeli funding rounds, updated Wednesday, shows no new Israeli cyber round since Dante Security on Monday, and I couldn't confirm a new one anywhere in the last forty-eight hours. So today is a short Cyber Watch, with one closed deal and one big-picture question.

The deal: on Thursday, Quantum eMotion, a Montreal company that sells security built around quantum technology and AI, completed its purchase of Plurilock Security, a small Canadian cybersecurity company listed in Toronto, with customers in Canada, the US and NATO. Plurilock shareholders got a bit of cash plus Quantum eMotion shares, and ninety-nine and a half percent of them voted yes. No total value was reported, and that tells you it's small. Why mention it? It's a pattern: small public cyber companies struggle to get attention from investors, so many choose to combine, and the buyer gets a ready-made customer list and sales team.

The big-picture question comes from Globes: should the CrowdStrike and Zscaler merger worry Israel's smaller cyber companies? Shahar Cohen of Lucid Capital argues it reinforces a trend of buyers purchasing many security services from one big provider, and that this could "actually harm the Israeli cybersecurity industry." The counterargument in the same piece: when giants consolidate, startups with truly unique technology become even more attractive targets to buy. Both can be true. It's harder to sell a single tool to a customer who wants one platform, and easier to get acquired if you're the best at one thing the giants are missing.

## People moves

On people moves, I couldn't confirm a new chief marketing officer at a cyber company this week, but there's one revenue hire worth knowing.

On Thursday, Netwrix named Tony Orlando its chief revenue officer, the executive in charge of all the teams that bring in money. Netwrix sells identity and data security, which means controlling who can access what inside a company and protecting sensitive files, and it says it has more than thirteen thousand customers, including nearly a quarter of the Fortune five hundred. Orlando comes from Google, where he was a managing director leading teams that sold cloud, AI and cybersecurity. Before that, he led global sales at Quest Software and was CRO at Delphix.

Why does a marketer care about a sales hire? Because a new CRO usually resets the go-to-market plan in his first ninety days: which customers to chase, how big the pipeline needs to be, and what marketing must deliver. Netwrix is also promoting that it's been named in Gartner's Magic Quadrant, the influential industry ranking, for privileged access management five years running. Privileged access management means protecting the most powerful accounts, like system administrators. That kind of recognition is ammunition for a new revenue chief, and marketing is the team that turns it into campaigns.

## Marketing and growth trends

Now marketing and growth, and it's all about the week ahead: AWS re:Invent, Amazon's huge cloud conference, which opens Monday in Las Vegas and runs through Friday.

First, look at how the security companies are selling there, and it's not mainly at booths. The public party listings for re:Invent are packed with invite-only CISO dinners, executive receptions and happy hours, and most of them are co-hosted by several companies. Wiz, now part of Google Cloud, has about six events, from a happy hour with Databricks and Nvidia on Monday to its own Wiz Party on Wednesday. Israeli-founded Upwind is hosting a Women in Cyber networking event on Tuesday. Okta is teaming up with the Israeli startup Huskeys for a happy hour, and a group of five younger companies is co-hosting one night at a bowling alley. Why does co-hosting work? Each company brings its own guest list, so everyone meets buyers they didn't know, and the cost is shared. For a small startup, that's how you get in front of CISOs you could never get a meeting with alone. One company even offers a helicopter tour. The theme of almost every event is the same: securing AI agents.

Second, watch Amazon itself. Before the conference, AWS launched a wave of security features built for AI agents, including temporary, limited permissions for agents and sub-agents, and a tool that finds unprotected APIs and AI endpoints, the connection points where software talks to software. AWS also has its own AI penetration testing service, where software attacks your systems to find holes before hackers do. The security writer Chris Farris notes its pricing by the hour and unclear acceptance for compliance may limit adoption. The marketing lesson: when the cloud platform itself starts offering a feature, the vendors selling the same thing on AWS Marketplace, Amazon's online store for business software, have to sharpen their story fast. "We do it too" stops working. You need to explain what you do better, or what Amazon doesn't do at all.

Third, the calendar keeps going. Black Hat Europe, the big security conference in London, runs December seventh to tenth, with keynotes on AI security and on the young hacker groups behind attacks like Scattered Spider. Two big events back to back means a lot of field marketing work in a very short window.

## Worth a look for you

Finally, worth a look for you.

First, Netwrix. A new revenue chief from Google with thirteen thousand customers to grow usually means a refreshed plan, and often new hires in demand generation and product marketing to feed his pipeline.

Second, the re:Invent crowd. The Israeli companies throwing events this week, like Upwind and Huskeys, are investing in events and partner marketing. Look at who they partner with and how they present themselves; it's great material for interviews, and companies that bet on events often hire event and field marketers right after a big show, when they count the meetings it produced.
